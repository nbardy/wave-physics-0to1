## Part 2 — The new outline

### Hero

One artifact: the gray disc in a stream, with the fluid's friction on a knob
and a two-line meter. The meter splits the push on the disc into the part that
is rubbing (wall shear integrated around the rim) and the part that is not
(pressure integrated around the rim), both in units of the stream's ram

Reynolds number printed beside it as a number and nothing more. At the top the
reader sees that halving the friction drops the rubbing line by about a third
and leaves the pressure line where it was, and that at the low end almost none
of the push is rubbing; the reader cannot say what the rest is or why it does
not follow the knob.

It returns at the end with one overlay: the wall layer drawn as a band of
measured thickness and the wake's pressure deficit shaded behind the disc. The
rubbing line is the band; the pressure line is the shaded area; the knob thins
the band and does not touch the area.

Familiarity cost, stated: the disc and its wake are lesson 01's Re-slider
figure (`CylinderFlow`) and the most reproduced picture in the subject. The
cost is real. It is paid two ways: the first thing the reader is given is the
meter, not the picture, and the article's every figure is a measurement on this
one body; nothing in it is there to be looked at. The disc is kept rather than
swapped because the exact frictionless answer exists in closed form only for
simple shapes (`lib/potential.ts` already computes it), and the story needs
that exact zero. A sphere would be truer to the 1912 measurement and the site
cannot simulate one honestly.

The article stays "The History of Navier–Stokes" in this sense: it is the
history of one number, and every name on the equation enters the article on
that number's ladder, dated, or does not enter.

### Thesis

The push on a body in a fast stream is set not by how much the fluid rubs but
by where the thin layer of fluid that does the rubbing lets go of the body, so
a fluid with almost no friction is not almost a fluid with none.

### The five sentences

1. A disc in a stream is pushed with a force that stays put while the fluid's
   friction is turned down tenfold; the meter shows that at the low end almost
   none of the push is rubbing, and the reader cannot say what the rest is.
2. The obvious account — the rest is pressure, the stream piling up on the
   front — fails on the exact frictionless flow of 1752: the pile-up at the
   nose is refunded at the tail, the refund is exact, and the meter on that
   flow reads 0.000.
3. Putting friction back as a term in the equation (Navier 1822, Stokes 1845)
   fixes the honey end — the sinking sphere's drag comes out right where
   friction dominates (Stokes 1851) — and creates a new failure: at the knob's
   low end the term is a hundredth of the inertia it corrects everywhere
   except a rim a few cells thick against the disc, which lights up on the
   friction-share overlay and which the reader cannot explain.
4. The wall condition (the fluid sticks, so its speed must fall to zero at the
   surface however little it rubs; Prandtl 1904) fixes that: the term is never
   small at the wall, the rim is a layer that thins as 1/√Re without vanishing
   — and the layer creates the next failure: fluid inside it has rubbed the
   wall the whole way round, reaches the back short of speed, cannot climb the
   tail's pressure, reverses, and the layer lifts off, so the tail's refund is
   never paid.
5. The disc from (1): the pressure line on the meter is the unpaid refund —
   the wake's pressure deficit times its width, set by where the layer lets
   go, which is nearly the same place across a hundredfold change in friction;
   the rubbing line is the layer's skin, thinning with the friction; and when
   the layer's own flow tears (Reynolds's threshold, inside the layer) it lets
   go later, the wake narrows, and the push drops threefold (Eiffel 1912,
   Prandtl 1914).

## Part 3 — The intro

A post standing in a river and the same post in a wind of the same speed are
pushed with forces in about the ratio of the two densities — eight hundred to
one — although water's friction is fifty-five times air's. Above a crawl, the
push on a post, a ball, or a hand grows with the fluid's density and the square
of the speed, and with almost nothing else about the fluid. So most of that
push is not rubbing — and rubbing is the only force a fluid exerts along a
surface. Below, the fluid's friction is on a knob, and the meter splits the
push on the disc into the part that is rubbing and the part that is not. A
fluid with almost no friction is not almost a fluid with none.
