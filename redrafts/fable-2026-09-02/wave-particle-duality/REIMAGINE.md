### STORY

The five sentences the article as it stands actually implies:

1. One spot on the screen receives fewer photons per second with two slits open
   than with one, and each photon arrives whole at one place.
2. Then we look at a single slit, whose lit patch grows as the slit narrows.
3. Then we look at the tip-to-tail arrow rule, which reproduces the two-slit
   pattern and the factor of four.
4. Then we look at the photoelectric effect, where brightness moves the count
   and not the energy, and $K_{\max} = h\nu - \varphi$.
5. Then we look at which-path detection, a ruler of wavelengths down to a
   baseball, and the electron's magnetic moment.

Sentence 1 is a real (1): visible, unexplained. Sentence 2 in the brief's form
would be "the obvious mechanism is lumps, and it fails" — and the article does
have that failure, but it is argued in prose at L63–67, never built and never
run; the lump prediction appears as a dashed line only in section three. Nothing
after the hero is driven by a failure of the thing before it. The single slit
does not repair anything; the arrow rule is issued, not found; the photoelectric
section breaks a classical field the page never constructed while the arrows the
page did construct are never put under load; which-path adjusts a parameter of
the answer; the ruler and the QED coda are appendices.

**The current hero:** the single-photon two-slit build-up, with a one-slit pane
underneath. Familiarity cost: maximal. It is the most reproduced image in
popular quantum physics — the Hitachi/Tonomura movie, every textbook's opening
figure, every video essay's thumbnail. Rosa predicted it from the title. The
one unfamiliar element, the red column, is handed over as a second pane rather
than made by the reader's hand, and it is paid at L136–147, sixty lines and one
unrelated section later.

**Specimen or lens:** the mathematical object is the amplitude rule — add arrows,
square the closing length, draw one arrival. The bench serves that object in
exactly one section (Tip to Tail, L116–158). The single-slit section is about
the specimen's own optics (Fresnel number, ray limit); the photoelectric section
is a second specimen (metals, work functions, Millikan) with an equation that
never touches an arrow; the ruler is a third; the ending is about QED. The bench
hosts a tour of quantum-optics exhibits; the object gets one room.

**Biggest flaw, one sentence:** nothing the reader builds ever fails — the lump
model is refuted in prose, the classical wave the photoelectric section kills was
never built, and the arrow rule that is the article's actual subject is issued in
bold and never put under load — so the sections are exhibits with their verdicts
already attached, not repairs.

## Part 2 — The new outline

### Hero

One artifact: a single-photon Mach–Zehnder interferometer with a polarization
rotator in one arm. A crystal emits photons in pairs; one photon of each pair
announces itself at a herald detector and its twin enters the bench. The twin
meets a half-silvered mirror, travels one of two arms, and the arms rejoin at a
second half-silvered mirror with detectors A and B on its two outputs. A third
counter fires only when A and B click in the same announcement window.

At the top: every click lands on A, B is silent, the both-counter reads zero,
and a clear plate in the upper arm — turned to ninety degrees — moves half the
clicks to B. None of this is explainable yet: not the silence of a detector that
faces the same mirror as A, not the zero on the both-counter, not a plate that
absorbs nothing and changes no distance switching a detector on.

At the end: the same figure, same knob, with the predicted curve
$P_B = (1-\cos\theta)/2$ drawn as a ghost over both live counters and the
both-counter still at zero; the reader sets any angle and the counts fall on the
ghost, and every term of the ghost is a thing the reader turned by hand in the
sections between.

Familiarity cost, stated honestly: medium. The single-photon Mach–Zehnder is in
every second-year quantum course and in Feynman's *QED*; the polarization tag is
the quantum-eraser family, which has had its video-essay moment. It is not the
image the title makes a reader predict, and the specific fact at the top — a
clear plate switching a detector on — is not one most readers have seen drawn.
The alternative with lower familiarity (the Hong–Ou–Mandel dip) needs two-photon
amplitudes and serves the object worse. The two-slit hero is not kept: its
familiarity is maximal, and its one fresh fact was served on a pane the reader
could not operate.

The bench is the Thorn et al. (2004) / Galvez et al. (2005) undergraduate
apparatus: violet pump, down-conversion crystal, herald, 810 nm signal photons.
Every number below is that bench's.

### Thesis

Each photon is counted whole, at one detector or the other; how often each
detector counts is fixed by a sum that can come out to zero.

### The five sentences

1. With both arms clear, every click lands on detector A, none on B, and never
   on both in one window; a clear plate turned to ninety degrees in one arm
   moves half the clicks to B.
2. A photon that takes one arm and flips a coin at each mirror is never at both
   detectors but puts half the clicks on B at every plate angle, and its ghost
   counter runs at 50% over a real counter reading zero; a wave that splits at
   the first mirror keeps B silent but, at ninety degrees, fires both detectors
   in one window once per four photons, over a real both-counter that has not
   moved.
3. An arrow per route — a turn per reflection the reader sets, the click rate
   the square of the closing arrow, one click drawn per photon — silences B and
   never fires both, but only at a quarter turn per reflection, and the same
   arrows predict that moving the upper mirror by a fraction of a micron changes
   nothing, while the counts swing through a full cycle every 810 nm of extra
   path.
4. An arrow that also turns once per 810 nm of path predicts the swing, and read
   as a phase shifter it reproduces the plate's curve too — until, at ninety
   degrees with the mirror sweeping, the swing is not shifted but flat at one
   half for every mirror position, which no phase can produce.
5. An arrow that carries the light's polarization as a tag, adding only along
   what the two tags share, predicts the swing's contrast as $\cos\theta$, the
   flat line at ninety degrees, and the hero's curve $(1-\cos\theta)/2$ — and
   the two ghosts of (2) turn out to be the two ends of the plate's travel: the
   coin model exactly right at ninety degrees, the split wave exactly right
   wherever B is silent.

### Section ladder

**1. The bench** (hero)

- Built: the apparatus, running. Pump, crystal, herald; first splitter; two arms
  with a plate in each and a mirror at each far corner; second splitter;
  detectors A and B with click strips and running rates; the both-in-one-window
  counter.
- Failure: the reader has no account of a detector that faces the same mirror
  as A and is silent, of a both-counter that stays at zero, or of a clear plate
  that wakes B.
- Savior: "Two accounts of light were on the table before this bench existed —
  a photon takes one arm, or a wave takes both — and each makes a definite
  prediction for all three counters at every angle of the plate."
- Figures: F1 NEW — `Interferometer`, top view, click strips, three counters,
  rate bars. Build cost: an `mzRates(theta, phi, alpha)` closed form in a new
  `interferometer.ts` (~40 lines; the `rng`, Poisson, and fixed-dt patterns
  exist in `optics.ts`), one Bernoulli draw per announced photon, a bench
  drawing of ~250 lines with `drawArrow`/`paneFrame` from `chrome.ts`; a day,
  plus three harness checks (B under 1% at 0°, B at 50 ± 2% at 90°, both-counter
  exactly zero).
- Knob: the upper plate's angle, 0° to 90°.
- Solver sentence beside the figure: every count is one draw per announced
  photon from the exact two-route amplitudes at the current knob settings, and
  nothing is drawn travelling in the arms, because drawing it would take a
  side. Under the figure, one flat fact: on the 2004 bench the both-counter read
  under two clicks in a hundred, from stray pairs.
- Math: none.

**2. Coins, and a wave that splits**

- Built (a): the coin model. Each announced photon picks an arm with a coin at
  the first splitter and a detector with a coin at the second. Its three
  counters run as ghost outlines beside the real ones, on the same photon
  stream.
- Failure (a): the coin ghost on B runs at 50% at every angle. The real B
  counter reads zero at 0° and rises to meet the ghost only at 90°. The model
  is most wrong when the plate is doing nothing. Its both-counter ghost sits at
  zero, matching the bench.
- Built (b): the split wave. Each announced photon's energy divides between the
  outputs as the wave predicts, and each detector fires independently in
  proportion to its share — the best a wave can do, since a wave cannot arrange
  for its two halves to consult.
- Failure (b): the wave ghost keeps B silent at 0° and gives the plate's curve —
  and its both-counter ghost runs at one click per four photons at 90°, where
  the real both-counter has not moved. The model is right wherever B is silent
  and wrong wherever both detectors are live.
- Savior: "Each old answer is right about one counter and at one end of the
  plate. Whatever replaces them has to add like the wave at the second mirror
  and be drawn once, like the coin, at the detectors."
- Figures: F2a OVERLAY on F1 — coin ghosts on all three counters (same seed,
  different routing rule; hours). F2b OVERLAY on F1 — wave ghosts (independent
  Bernoulli per detector at its intensity share; hours).
- Knob: the plate angle, both figures.
- Math: the coin arithmetic in numbers, in prose — one half of one half, twice,
  is one half. No display.

**3. Arrows**

- Built: an arrow for each of the four routes (two to each detector). Length
  $1/\sqrt2$ per splitter pass, so $1/2$ per route. Each reflection at a splitter
  turns the arrow by a fixed amount, and that amount is the knob. At each
  detector the two arrows are laid tip to tail; the click rate is the square of
  the closing length; one click per photon is drawn from the two rates. The
  reader counts reflections on the drawing: one on each route to A; two on one
  route to B and none on the other.
- Failure: with no turn per reflection the arrows at B are parallel and the rule
  predicts a click at A *and* a click at B for every photon — two clicks per
  photon sent, and a meter shows it. A half turn gives the same. Only a quarter
  turn brings the predicted total to one click per photon, and there the two
  arrows at B are opposed and the closing length is nothing. Conservation
  forces the turn, and the turn forces the silence. The both-counter is zero by
  the rule's construction: one draw per photon.
- Savior: "The rule as built assigns no turn to distance — a mirror moved by a
  hair predicts nothing. The upper mirror sits on a piezo stack that moves it by
  nanometres."
- Figures: F3 OVERLAY on F1 — an arrow pane beside the bench (four arrows, two
  per detector, the tip-to-tail sum and closing arrow for the selected
  detector), the routes highlighted on the drawing with their reflection
  counts printed, a meter "predicted clicks per photon: A + B," live counters
  for comparison; the plate fixed at 0°. Half a day.
- Knob: the turn per reflection, 0 to one full turn.
- Math moment: the beam splitter's two arrows $r = i/\sqrt2$, $t = 1/\sqrt2$;
  $P_A = |rt + tr|^2 = 1$, $P_B = |r^2 + t^2|^2 = 0$; and $|r|^2 + |t|^2 = 1$
  with $r \perp t$, which is what the reader just found by turning the knob
  until the total came to one click per photon.

**4. The mirror on the piezo**

- Built: the §3 rule with the piezo knob added. Its prediction is a flat line:
  the reflection count does not change when a mirror moves.
- Predict prompt (the one in the article; the common guess is wrong): move the
  upper mirror 400 nm. Which counter changes?
- Failure: at 405 nm of extra path B has gone from silent to nearly every
  click and A has fallen to almost none; at 810 nm they are back; the counts

- Repair: the arrow also turns with distance, one full turn per 810 nm. The
  upper arrow in the pane now rotates as the piezo moves; the closing length at
  B opens and closes; the ghost matches. 810 nm is the period the reader read
  off the knob; only after that is it named: the crystal's photons have half the
  pump's frequency and twice its wavelength, 405 nm doubled.
- Savior: "The plate in the hero adds no path — its twin in the lower arm sees

- Figures: F4a OVERLAY — piezo knob, flat ghost, swinging counts (half a day

- Knob: extra path in the upper arm, 0 to 2000 nm.

**5. The plate**

- Built: the plate as a phase shifter, $\varphi_{\text{plate}} = \theta$. At

- Failure: set the plate to ninety degrees and let the piezo sweep. The phase-

  flattens a swing; a phase only slides it.
- Repair: the arrow carries the light's polarization direction as a tag. Where
  two arrows meet at a detector they add only along what their tags share; the
  rest is counted separately. Closing length squared at B:

- The other half of the tag: two polarizations separated by $\theta$ can be told

  tag also says which arm the photon took, to that degree. $V = |\cos\theta|$,

- Savior: none needed; the next section is the hero.
- Figures: F5a OVERLAY — plate knob; the piezo auto-sweeps and B's measured

### Ending

## Part 3 — The intro

A violet laser pumps a crystal that emits infrared photons in pairs. One photon
of each pair goes to a detector that only announces it; its twin enters the
bench, whose counters run only in the nanoseconds after an announcement. The
twin meets a half-silvered mirror and splits into two equal arms that rejoin at
a second one, with a detector on each output. The clicks land on detector A.
Detector B faces the same mirror at the same angle and is all but silent. A
third counter, for A and B in the same window, stays at zero.

A clear plate in the upper arm turns the light's polarization; its twin in the
lower arm, fixed at zero, keeps the arms matched. Turn the upper plate to ninety
degrees and half the clicks move to B. Each photon is counted whole, at one
detector or the other; how often each counts is fixed by a sum that can come
out to zero.

**Hero figure:** the bench in top view — pump, crystal, herald, first splitter,
two arms with a plate in each and a mirror at each far corner, second splitter,
detectors A and B with click strips and running rates, the both-in-one-window
counter. One knob: the upper plate's angle, 0° to 90°.

### Audit

1. "A violet laser pumps a crystal that emits infrared photons in pairs." —
   Fact: 405 nm pump, BBO down-conversion, 810 nm pairs (Thorn 2004). Rule 1:
   spends nothing (the wavelength is not named; §4 measures it). Rule 2: no
   schedule. Rule 3: about the machine. Rule 4: cold open on a laser. Rule 6: no
   imagination. Rule 8: standard, checkable. Topic-swap: fails to survive a swap
   (no other article has a down-conversion crystal). Delete: loses the source.
   Who's-talking: the physicist at the bench. Nick test: states hardware, not a
   rule.
2. "One photon of each pair goes to a detector that only announces it; its twin
   enters the bench, whose counters run only in the nanoseconds after an
   announcement." — Fact: heralded detection with a coincidence window
   (~5–10 ns). Rules 1–4, 6, 8 pass as above; "only announces" and "only in the
   nanoseconds" are both checkable against the apparatus. Delete: loses why the
   both-counter means anything. Four tests pass.
3. "The twin meets a half-silvered mirror and splits into two equal arms that
   rejoin at a second one, with a detector on each output." — Fact: a
   Mach–Zehnder. "splits" is the neutral verb the two old accounts disagree
   about (a wave splits; the coin model in §2 says the photon does not), and
   the sentence does not decide. Rule 1: the equal arms are a plant, not a
   payoff. Four tests pass.
4. "The clicks land on detector A." — Fact: at plate angle zero, $P_A = 1$ on
   the page; on the bench, fringe visibility ~98%. Rule 8: "the clicks" not
   "every click," so the ~2% residual is not denied. Four tests pass.
5. "Detector B faces the same mirror at the same angle and is all but silent." —
   Fact: the two outputs of a symmetric splitter are geometrically equivalent;
   $P_B = 0$ on the page, ~1–2% real. Rule 8: "all but." Disputable: yes — an
   expert could ask about the splitter's asymmetry, which is the right question.
   Four tests pass.
6. "A third counter, for A and B in the same window, stays at zero." — Fact:
   true of the figure by construction (one draw per announced photon); the real
   bench's value — under two in a hundred, Thorn 2004 — is stated in §1's body
   directly under the figure, so rule 8 is honoured there rather than by hedging
   the intro. Rule 1: the zero is the plant the whole article pays, stated
   flat. Four tests pass.
7. "A clear plate in the upper arm turns the light's polarization; its twin in
   the lower arm, fixed at zero, keeps the arms matched." —
   Fact: a rotator (half-wave plate or Faraday rotator) and its compensator;
   "clear" is checkable (AR-coated, transmission near 1). Rule 1: the twin plate
   is planted, paid in §5. Rule 3: machine. Four tests pass.
8. "Turn the upper plate to ninety degrees and half the clicks move to B." —
   Fact: $P_B = (1-\cos 90°)/2 = 1/2$. The imperative is the conditional form
   the calibration triptych uses ("Drag it left and…"), not an instruction
   about the reader's experience. Rule 1: states the effect, not its cause.
   Four tests pass.
9. "Each photon is counted whole, at one detector or the other; how often each
   counts is fixed by a sum that can come out to zero." — The thesis,
   a full sentence. First clause: the both-counter, restated as a claim.
   Second clause: disputable (it is the Born rule stated in plain words, and a
   reader could argue "sum" is not yet shown to be the mechanism — which is what
   §3 earns). Rule 1: names the sum, spends neither the arrows nor the quarter
   turn nor the tag. Rule 5 as demoted: one thesis, last, a sentence not a
   fragment because the two clauses carry the two halves. Topic-swap: fails
   (no sibling article counts photons). Delete: loses the claim. Who's-talking:
   physicist. Nick test: a claim about light, not a rule instantiated.

No sentence's only content is an effect on the reader. No "imagine." No
metaphor. The opening sentence is a laser, not an aphorism. Shape against the
p-bits winner: hardware described, two counters stated, the plate, the thesis —
no measured-familiar side, no inversion, no fragment.
