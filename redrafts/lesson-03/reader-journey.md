## READER A — Priya

Backend engineer, 36, strong coder, hasn't touched calculus since a compilers course a
decade ago. It's 10:04pm, laptop on the couch. Her friend Tomás sent the link this
afternoon with the message "this is the fluid thing I was talking about." She did not
read the previous lesson. She will drag every slider.

### Opening (L24–37)

I expected a Wikipedia-with-toys history page. First sentence: "The name reads like a
partnership. It wasn't one." Okay. Never met, never corresponded, one of them dead nine
years before the other published — and *three other men* derived the same equation in
between? That's a better premise than I expected. I predict this is going to be a
who-really-invented-it story.

Line 33 — "If you built the equation in the previous lesson." I didn't. Small pang,
like joining a series at episode two. I decide to keep going and see if it carries me.

### TimelineHero (L39–46)

I drag the year slider back and forth three or four times before reading the paragraph
under it. The same gray disc, and the *water around it* changes personality — dots
pinging off it, then a weirdly polite glide, then goo, then the swirly trail. Then I
read: "The right-hand end of the slider is blank. That is not an oversight, and it
stays blank to the end." I drag to the right end to check. It's blank. The author knew
I'd check. Feels like finding a locked door in a game — I now assume the article ends
at that door, and I want to know what's behind it.

Prediction: each section will be one stop on this slider. (This turns out right, and
knowing it right makes the length feel navigable rather than threatening.)

### Still Water (L48–96)

Archimedes, buoyancy — comfortable territory, I half-know this. I drag the density
slider both ways in the BuoyancyCrown; the half-density-half-submerged thing is
satisfying to confirm by eye. Barometer: "watch what refuses to change" — I tilt it,
and the vertical height genuinely refuses. Small huh. Then the Pascal mountain
paragraph (L79–82) and my eyes start to slide — "pressure is the weight of the fluid
above you" is a sentence I've known since school, and it's 10:15pm. This is the first
place I catch myself skimming. What stops the skim is Leonardo: "turbulence had a name
three centuries before it had an equation, and four before it had a number" (L94). I
reread that. *A number?* What number? The sentence is clearly loaded and I can't
unload it. I file it, slightly annoyed and slightly hooked.

Directional check: I currently believe this is a chronological tour and I'm in the
slow early rooms. I'm willing, but I want the pace to pick up.

### Newton's Guess (L98–148)

"The least read and the most wrong" — good, we're skipping the reverent Newton. The
"want of lubricity" phrase makes me smile; I drag the ShearBlend to honey and watch the
slow layer get hauled along. Then the CorpuscleHail: I watch it for a while because
something is off before the text tells me what — nothing goes *around* the disc,
there's just a dead shadow behind it. The prose then says exactly that (L125–127),
which is a strange pleasure: I saw the wrongness before being told. The parenthesis
about spacecraft in the upper atmosphere (L128–130) — a dead model resurrected 250
years later — I file that one to tell someone.

The sound-speed race: my attention dips through the thermodynamics explanation
(L139–141, the heat-can't-leak-away part reads as detail I'm not required to hold),
but "crassitude" snags me back — Newton fudging with a word that ridiculous is very
human. The calculus-wars paragraph I skim, keeping only "Britain rejoins the story
late" as a promise.

### The Family Business (L150–173)

The father *backdated his own book six years* to steal priority from his son. I sit up.
"I am robbed of my entire Hydrodynamica" — I screenshot this paragraph. This is the
first place the article feels like it's about people with pulses rather than portraits.
And the letter's recipient is Euler, "about to make the quarrel obsolete" — I can feel
a bigger character being walked on stage.

The pipe figure: I narrow it, markers rush, gauge drops. The instruction to read it as
"an observation, not an explanation" (L161) — I notice I'm being told how to hold
something, and I don't mind, because it releases me from a worry (I was about to wonder
*why* pressure drops, and the article said: not yet, not needed).

### The Zero (L175–256)

The string section I read at medium speed — "the first partial differential equation in
physics" is a phrase I nod at rather than feel. But the thirty-year fight about what a
*solution even is* (L188–198) grabs me unexpectedly hard, because I recognize it: it's
a spec dispute. D'Alembert says only well-formed inputs count, Euler says the API must
accept anything a user can produce, Bernoulli says everything decomposes into
primitives. I've been in that meeting.

Then the Predict widget. "Commit to a guess." I commit: **Hard — it's a solid disc in a
current.** Answer: not at all. Zero. My immediate reaction, verbatim in my head: *the
sim is rigged to make a point.* The very next paragraph (L216): "The meter is not
broken and it is not asserting anything. It integrates the pressure over the disc's
surface every frame." The article answered my suspicion a half-second after I formed
it. That is the exact moment I trust the author.

The parcel narrative (L221–245) I read twice. The money framing does it — the parcel
"spends pressure to buy speed" downhill, then "pays its speed back out" uphill, and
"**nothing in this fluid takes a cut**." So the back of the disc gets rebuilt at full
pressure "out of the parcel's own returned momentum." I stare at the red lobe behind
the disc in the figure and it stops looking like graphics and starts looking like a
refund. "A ship in this water needs no sails" — grin, out loud, alone on the couch.

"Keep your eye on that back hill." (L255) Assignment accepted. I now believe I'm not
on a tour; I'm carrying a debt through the rest of the article, and I know it.

### Waypoint (L258–264)

10:40pm. "This article does not pay it for another hundred and fifty-two years." I
check the scrollbar: I'm maybe 40% in. Brief fatigue-flicker — *a hundred and fifty-two

meter wins.

### Euler's Field (L266–323)

The TermStack fills four nameplates at once, all "Euler, 1757." Seeing the equation as

The empty green slot at the end of the section (L319–323): I notice the article is
holding two debts now — the zero meter and the empty slot — and I'm tracking both
without effort, because each one is a physical thing I've looked at.

### The Missing Term (L325–377)

Navier built bridges, his showcase bridge cracked, and the review committee blamed him
for trusting *too much mathematics* — and this is the guy who completes the equation.
The irony carries me through the section. I drive the MolecularSprings lattice and
watch the jagged shear comb itself straight.

Then the bold line: "**the molecules were fiction, and the term was right.**" I stop.
Reread. This is the sentence I will say to Tomás tomorrow, close to verbatim. And the
slider on the lattice "labeled with its honest units: none" — I go back up and drag it
again just to feel the joke: a knob that works, connected to nothing measurable. "I
cannot tell you what to set it to. Neither could he."

The parenthesis about the slip condition having "a modern afterlife" (L375–377) — I
half-register it. A small IOU; I don't hold it tightly.

### Five Times (L379–447)

The StressCube: I drag the top face and all four faces answer. The
would-spin-infinitely-fast argument for symmetry (L394–396) I follow about 70% of and
let go — I accept the cube's behavior as the fact and move on without rereading. Honest
admission: the Poisson and Saint-Venant sentences (L401–404) start to blur — names,
dates, priority — until this: "Eponymy is a lottery, and citing your rivals buys no
tickets" (L407). I stop skimming, copy that line into my notes app. The guy who
credited everyone got erased. That one goes to work Slack eventually.

The FallingSphere: I shrink the second ball and watch it crawl. "Surrender
seven-eighths of your weight to keep half your drag and you crawl" (L428) — reread,
because the R³-versus-R race is genuinely the whole thing in one sentence. And then the
turn I wasn't expecting: run the experiment backwards and Navier's fictional ε becomes
a number you can *look up* (L429–431). The unitless slider from the last section
retroactively gets its units. I feel that connection click without being told to feel
it. The slip-condition IOU pays here too (L431–434) — sticks, not slides — and I only
half-remember it was planted, but the microfluidics afterlife ("channels a few
molecules wide") is a collectible either way.

Poiseuille: halve the radius, flow collapses to a sixteenth. I do the arithmetic in my
head — 2⁴, yes, sixteen — and then squeeze the pipe in the sim to watch it happen. The
sim and my arithmetic agreeing is a specific small pleasure: I verified the article
rather than believing it. "A doctor handed the theorists their first proof" — nice.

### Direct and Sinuous (L457–498)

### Ten Minutes in Heidelberg (L500–542)

The section title alone pulls — ten minutes, after all this? I drag the loupe around

### The Open Question (L552–601)

Richardson computing a weather forecast between ambulance runs, wrong by a factor of a
hundred, undeterred, publishing a fantasy of sixty-four thousand human computers

Kolmogorov: "a postcard here — admired, not derived" (L572). I'm grateful for the

Then the Leray/Ladyzhenskaya paragraph and it's 11:15pm and I hit my worst sag of the

fact.

"Richardson's hall of sixty-four thousand computers, though — that part got built."
(L601) I make an actual noise at this line. The turn from unpayable mathematics to
built machines, in one sentence, aimed straight at my profession.

### The Machine Age (L603–632)

The TimelineHero comes back (L625) and I do run it end to end, as told, and every stop

### One Skeleton, Twice Built + Final Words (L634–681)

## READER B — Marek

Physics PhD who left academia for quant work, 41. Has read every Ciechanowski essay,
most of Distill, watched 3b1b at 2x. Reads at speed, allergic to being managed, and is

the link at 11pm with the tab-close reflex armed.

### Opening (L24–37) — the audition

Two screens to convince me. First paragraph: never met, twenty-three years and one
Channel apart, dead nine years before Stokes published, three other men in between.
That's dates and geography, not vibes — the density of checkable fact per sentence is
high, and nothing has yet told me how to feel. Provisional pass.

L33–37: "you own it the way its authors never did" — the shape of flattery, and I
flinch at shapes like that, but it's addressed to alumni of a previous lesson, not to
me. I log the prerequisite I don't have and move on.

### TimelineHero (L39–46) — first itch

I drag the slider end to end once, precisely, watching the regime changes. Then: "That
is not an oversight, and it stays blank to the end." The author is narrating their own
plant. My managed-allergy itches — *don't tell me what to notice, let me notice* — and
this is the first near-exit. What holds me is the wager structure: announcing a debt on
screen one is either confidence or bluffing, and the opening paragraph's factual
density says confidence. Fine. I'll hold you to it. The blank end is obviously the
Millennium problem; I know the destination. The question is whether the route adds
anything.

### Still Water (L48–96) — the museum wing

Buoyancy tank, barometer, Pascal's mountain. I have seen each of these figures a
hundred times in a hundred essays and I read this section at full skim. This is my
longest sustained itch to tab away — around L68 (the Barometer) I'm consciously
deciding whether the next section justifies staying. Two sentences hold me. First:
pressure's "second career, as the instant enforcer inside moving water, is a century

centuries before it had an equation, and four before it had a number" (L94) — I decode
"number" as Reynolds instantly, and the fact that the sentence is written to be
decoded *later* by slower readers, while handing me the wink *now*, tells me the author
thought about readers at more than one speed. I stay.

### Newton's Guess (L98–148) — the shibboleth

My private test for any fluid-history retelling: does it conflate Newton's shear
hypothesis (the "want of lubricity" sentence, correct) with his corpuscle drag theory
(quantitative, wrong)? Most conflate. This one keeps them surgically apart — the
hypothesis "stated in passing" and left unformalized for 135 years (L113–115), the
corpuscle model shown *failing on screen* with its empty shadow and no wake, and then
the free-molecular-flow resurrection for spacecraft (L128–130), which most popular
tellings don't know about. This is the section where I stop auditioning the author and
commit the evening. The "crassitude" quote is a primary-source touch; the Laplace
correction is stated with the right mechanism (adiabatic stiffening) and no drama.

### The Family Business (L150–173)

I know the Bernoulli feud. The article has the backdated 1732 date and routes the
robbed-of-my-Hydrodynamica letter to Euler as recipient — matches what I remember, and
notably no "juicy academic drama!!" framing; the quarrel is dispatched in five lines
and used as a hinge to walk Euler on. Restraint noted.

### The Zero (L175–256) — where I slow down voluntarily

The d'Alembert string material and the what-is-a-solution fight I know, though "the
PDE was invented *for a wave*, a decade before fluids got theirs" is a sequencing point
I hadn't held consciously, and I can see it's load-bearing for the whole course's
framing. Filed.

The Predict widget: I click "Not at all" without a heartbeat of doubt — this widget is
for someone else. But I'm watching what happens next very closely, because the zero-drag
meter is exactly where interactive essays cheat: is that meter *computing* anything or
is it a `<span>0.00</span>`? The prose meets me at the door: "The meter is not broken
and it is not asserting anything. It integrates the pressure over the disc's surface
every frame, sorted by which way each patch of rim actually shoves" (L216–219). The
author pre-empted my exact suspicion, in order, before I could voice it. That's the
moment I recognize I've been simulated — and, grudgingly, that I don't mind.

Then the parcel walk (L221–245), and here I slow down *by choice* for the first time.
Every telling I know asserts fore-aft pressure symmetry from potential theory and moves
on. This one buys the symmetry with a mechanism: the parcel's speed is a currency,
"nothing in this fluid takes a cut," and the rear red lobe is "the front lobe, rebuilt
out of the parcel's own returned momentum" (L235–236). I reread that clause. It's the
correct energy argument, and it's staged so that Prandtl's sliver — I know he's coming
— will break it at exactly one named point. "Keep your eye on that back hill": I know
precisely where this goes, and my pleasure changes genre here, from suspense to
watching execution — rereading a mystery already knowing the killer, to see if the
clues were really there. They are being placed in plain sight.

### Waypoint (L258–264)

A device Ciechanowski doesn't use. I inspect it for recap-padding and find a ledger
instead — four assets and one debt, in four lines. Tolerated, and honestly useful at
this length.

### Euler's Field (L266–323)

Four nameplates fill at once and I see the article's skeleton whole: the equation *is*
the table of contents, and the timeline is its commit history. The PressureOff figure I
test properly — I check that the caption admits both channels carry the same numerical
stickiness (L310–312, it does, explicitly, "identical on both sides, so the only
difference between them is the term"), which is exactly the confound a lazier build
would leave unmentioned. The Euler quote about analysis itself failing us — correctly
deployed, and "right about the wall for two centuries and counting" is the honest
extension.

### The Missing Term (L325–377)

The bolded thesis — "the molecules were fiction, and the term was right" — is the
epistemological heart of the whole priority mess, and the article backs it with the
unitless slider: ε as a knob that visibly does physics while connecting to nothing
measurable. The figure is carrying the philosophy of science load here, which I have
almost never seen attempted. The bridge-collapse reproach ("too much on mathematics") I
half-remember from somewhere; plausible, unchecked, and I note that I'm now *extending
credit* to this author — a thing I do for maybe one essayist in twenty. The slip
parenthesis: I predict microfluidics before the sentence ends.

### Five Times (L379–447)

My home historiography, and the shibboleth here is Saint-Venant: derived it the modern
way, published two years ahead of Stokes, credited everyone, got nothing. The article
has all of it (L402–405), and then mints "Eponymy is a lottery, and citing your rivals
buys no tickets" — which I copy. That's my line for tomorrow.

Stokes: the R³-weight-versus-R-drag race in the FallingSphere caption I verify in my
head — sound — and I drag the radius slider once to check the quarter-way-down claim
against the R² arithmetic. It holds. The inversion — time the ball, and μ "is the only
unknown left in the sentence" — is the correct story of how viscosity became a
tabulated number, told through the same figure. And the no-slip resolution closes
Navier's slip debt with the microfluidics afterlife I predicted; the books are being
kept in public.

Poiseuille's R⁴ I know; read at speed; "a law strange enough that nobody would guess it
and exact enough that nobody could fudge it" is a compression I pause half a second on.

### Direct and Sinuous (L457–498)

Reynolds I know cold. What I don't know: Stokes refereed Reynolds's 1883 paper at the
Royal Society, at sixty-four, his own pendulum memoir having carried the number in
embryo (L484–487). That is a genuinely new fact to me, thirty years into reading about
this subject, and it's the one I will retell. The Sommerfeld-1908 christening detail is
also correct and rarely included. The Reynolds-averaging framing — "we stop computing
the flow and start computing its *statistics*" (L491–492) — is the honest way to say
it, no euphemism about closure.

### Ten Minutes in Heidelberg (L500–542) — the execution I stayed for

I know Prandtl 1904. The test is whether the article connects separation back to its
own parcel, or just waves at "friction concentrates in a thin layer." L518–526 walks
the same parcel up the same back hill, now short of speed from wall-rubbing, stalling
partway, shoved backwards — and instructs me to park the loupe on the rear shoulder
and watch the arrows reverse. I do it, skeptically, and the near-wall arrows reverse
on screen where the prose says they do. The figure-prose binding I've been auditing
all night closes its last open check right there.

"Zero since 1752 in this article and zero since 1752 in history" (L530–531) — the
double ledger, article-time and calendar-time, in one clause. And then "it was an error
of *where to look*" (L536): I test it for cuteness — is this overclaiming? No; it's
the singular-perturbation reading stated without the jargon, and "he wasn't [wrong]"
is technically precise about the outer solution. I knew this ending and the telling
still added the thing I came for: the debt and its payment staged on one gray disc,
with my own hand on the loupe at the moment of payment.

### The Open Question (L552–601)

Richardson, Kolmogorov, Leray, Ladyzhenskaya, Tao — I know every name here and read at
double speed, but I'm reading the *selection*, and the selection is current and honest:
Leray's own coinage "turbulent solutions" as a warning shot; Ladyzhenskaya actually
credited with the 2D fence (most tellings skip her); the Tao 2016 averaged-equation
blow-up presented as evidence *against* optimism, which is the correct current state
and the opposite of the usual millennium-prize romance. The Heisenberg aside — "the man
who made uncertainty a principle warmed up on the equation that still keeps one" — I
smile, then audit the smile for cuteness, then let it stand; the fact underneath is
real. The section's last line, "that part got built," I see coming one sentence early
and enjoy anyway.

### The Machine Age (L603–632)

Harlow 1965, Chorin 1968, Stam 1999 — the genealogy is right, and the Gibbs–Heaviside
point (the equation's five discoverers all wrote three scalar equations; the vector
"face" is sixty years younger) is the kind of notation-history observation I'd expect
from someone who has actually read the memoirs, or read Darrigol carefully. Which one,
I wonder — and hold the question for the bibliography.

### One Skeleton, Twice Built (L634–652) — my one pushback

The claim: the previous lesson's pedagogical ladder and history's ladder are the same
because both are driven by visible failure. Half-true. History is messier — Poisson and
Cauchy weren't answering a failure anyone could see; they were answering *each other's
foundations*. The section partially concedes this ("Nobody was following a
curriculum"), and it's brief, and I didn't read the previous lesson so the comparison
is addressed past me anyway. I skim, quibble, keep "the next term was whatever it took
to stop lying," and move on. This is my third and mildest near-exit — self-reference
to an artifact I haven't seen — survived by brevity.

### Further Reading (L654–668) — the held check clears

Darrigol, *Worlds of Flow* — named, and the "discovered five times" framing explicitly
attributed to it. Every fact I extended credit on tonight now has a visible collateral.
Eckert for the Göttingen school, Anderson's Physics Today piece for Heidelberg,
Reynolds's 1883 paper flagged as open access with the note that the engravings are the
figure I just played with. An author who shows me the bibliography did the work, and
tells me which framing is borrowed, gets the thing I almost never give: I open the site
root to see what else is here. That is my endorsement, performed rather than felt.

### Final Words (L670–681)

The roll-call unpacking of the name I read once, at speed, recognizing each clause. "It
is not waiting for a monument. It is waiting for a name" — I test it against the Clay
framing (a proof would indeed carry a name; the slider's blank end would get its
nameplate) and it's coherent with the article's own device rather than perfume on top
of it. 11:38pm. I came to grade two screens. I read the whole thing and checked the
lesson index on the way out.

## 4. THE RIDE IN ONE PARAGRAPH

The article opens by breaking the name on its own cover, hands the reader a slider
with a deliberately blank end, and then walks them into a quiet museum wing — the one
stretch where both readers' grip loosens — before the Bernoulli family theft puts
blood in the story. From there it runs on a debt economy: a meter that reads zero, a
parcel whose refund is exact, an empty green slot, an unmeasurable constant, each
opened in front of the reader with the date of its repayment withheld. The middle is a
parade of five derivations that keeps threatening to blur into names and instead keeps
paying out in single transferable sentences — the lottery of eponymy, the
seven-eighths of weight surrendered for half the drag. The route crests where every
debt was pointed all along: a loupe the reader drags to a rear shoulder with their own
hand, arrows reversing on screen, a meter moving for the first time in a hundred and
fifty-two years of history and ninety minutes of reading. Then the road deliberately
runs off the map — the smart money against smoothness, the blank slider end
recontextualized from locked door to open frontier — and drops each reader at a
different home: the engineer into her own profession's chapter of the story with the
previous lesson open in a new tab, the skeptic at the bibliography that retroactively
collateralizes every fact he took on credit, checking the index for what else this
author has built.
