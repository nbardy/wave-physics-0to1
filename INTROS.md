# INTROS.md — how an opening is written here

The one home for opening-paragraph law. ESSENCE §6 measures what the master's
openings do; SLOP families 1 and 20 name the two ways ours have actually failed
(promissory narration — the founding incident; the trailer register — flagged
2026-08-26 on the published p-bits opening). This doc is those lessons inverted
into a working method.

Provenance: a propose-3 → critique → constitution → propose-5 exercise run
2026-08-26 on the p-bits opening, with Nick's pick and reasons folded back in.
The worked examples below are real drafts from that exercise, kept because each
one failed a different way.

---

## The shape that wins

Three moves, in order, and nothing else:

1. **The familiar side, as measured fact.** Something already in the reader's
   world, described by what it verifiably does — "billions of transistors …
   margined, clocked, and error-corrected; most of the watts go to keeping
   thermal noise out of the logic." No adjectives. The facts are the atmosphere.
2. **The inversion.** The subject of the article, stated as the plain negation
   of the familiar side — "a chip now exists that runs on the noise instead."
   The wonder gap is the distance between move 1 and move 2, and the reader
   crosses it alone; the prose never says "surprisingly."
3. **The thesis, last.** One aphorism-weight line the whole article will spend
   itself earning — "The flipping is the computation." The intro terminates on
   it. It is the intro's only fragment and only quotable.

Then the hero figure, playable, within ~150 words of the top.

## The constitution

Ranked by how much work each rule did in the exercise — the top four killed
real drafts; the rest shape survivors.

1. **Spend no payoffs.** Any reveal, number, image, or historical beat that a
   later section exists to earn is off-limits — including the ending's. (Killed
   the coin-flip draft, which pre-chewed "A bit that leans," and the
   Geman-brothers draft, which strip-mined Further Reading. The least obvious
   rule and the most valuable.)
2. **Nothing schedules.** No "will," "is going to," "in these pages," "this
   lesson is about." Every sentence delivers now. The one legal IOU is a flat
   fact pinned to the hero ("the weights ship unexplained — on credit").
3. **Every sentence is about the machine.** Topic-swap and Nick-test each one;
   a sentence whose subject is the article, the author, or the reader's coming
   experience is a stage direction — cut. (This was the tiebreaker that ranked
   every candidate: the winner was the only draft with zero such sentences.)
4. **Open cold on a concrete thing** — a transistor, a current, a watt. Never
   on stakes, wars, or significance; drama belongs to the phenomenon (SLOP 20).
5. **One aphorism maximum, and it is the thesis, and it comes last.**
6. **Hands, never imagination.** "Imagine" is banned; show it below or point at
   something already in the reader's world.
7. **A metaphor in the intro is almost always net-negative** — it must be
   audited on the spot (ESSENCE's "imperfect but convenient"), and the audit
   sentence spends words on words. Prefer the fact-stated inversion, which
   needs no audit.
8. **Every claim survives the math review.** No false universals for rhythm —
   "not one of them ever flips" costs more than it buys; "almost none" keeps
   the cadence and the truth.
9. **The intro's only job is to make the hero land.** If a sentence does not
   sharpen what the reader is about to see, it goes.

## Worked example — the p-bits opening

The shipped failure (trailer register, SLOP 20):

> ✗ "Every computer you own is at war with noise. … A chip exists now that
> surrenders it instead. … Here is the surrender, running."

Rejected drafts, one kill-reason each:

> ✗ "This lesson is about a chip designed the other way…" — rule 3: the
> subject became the lesson.
> ✗ "Flip a coin. … Now imagine a coin with an opinion…" — rules 1 and 6:
> pre-chews section one, and asks for imagination where figure two shows it.
> ✗ "In 1984, two brothers published an algorithm…" — rule 1: spends the
> Further Reading payoff; anchors in literature before mechanism.
> ✗ "A modern chip is a refrigerator for information…" — rule 7: the metaphor
> needed an audit sentence, and the audit was the paragraph's worst sentence.

The winner (Nick's pick, 2026-08-26), annotated against the shape:

> ✓ "Billions of transistors in the laptop in front of you are margined,
> clocked, and error-corrected so that almost none of them ever flips a bit by
> accident; most of the watts the machine burns go to keeping thermal noise
> out of the logic. *(move 1 — the familiar side, measured)* A chip now exists
> that runs on the noise instead: its bits flip at random on purpose, millions
> of times a second, driven by the same jitter every other chip suppresses.
> *(move 2 — the inversion, plain)* The flipping is not overhead it tolerates.
> The flipping is the computation. *(move 3 — thesis, last, fragment earned)*"

Why it beat four alternatives that were individually fine: it is the only
draft in which every sentence is about the machine (rule 3), it carries the
drama entirely in the gap between two stated facts (no metaphor to audit, no
narrative dress), and it leaves every later payoff untouched — the coin, the
comparator, the meter, and the history all still arrive as news.

## Order of application

Draft to the shape; kill by the constitution top-down; then run SLOP's four
tests sentence-by-sentence. When a candidate survives everything and still
feels flat, the fix is a sharper fact in move 1 — never a hotter word.
