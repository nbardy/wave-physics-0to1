# Basis, Cage, and Boundary

Open any mechanical CAD file and five words fall out: B-spline, NURBS, T-spline,
SubD, B-rep. They are usually introduced as a menu — pick the representation that
suits your part — and the menu is wrong. Three of them answer *how is this
function written down*. One answers *what does a designer push around*. One
answers *which side of this surface is metal*. A single part uses all of them at
once, in layers, and the layers do not compete for the job.

Below is one object. The slider does not switch between three models; there is
only one. It changes which description is drawing:

<OneObject />

At the left end you see a <C k="ctrl">cage</C> — eight points and the quads
between them, the thing a person actually edits. In the middle the cage is gone
and the <C k="curve">surface</C> it converges to is all that remains. At the
right end the surface is cut by <C k="topo">red boundaries</C> into six named
faces. Nothing about the object changed across that slide. Its cage, its surface,
and its face structure were true simultaneously the whole time.

The rest of this lesson takes those layers apart one at a time and puts a meter
on each.

## The Basis Is the Whole Story

Start with the one everybody has heard of and almost nobody has been shown. A
B-spline curve is written

$$
C(u) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} N_{i,p}(u)\, P_i ,
$$

which is a weighted average of points. The <C k="ctrl">$P_i$</C> are the
control points. The <C k="basis">$N_{i,p}$</C> are the basis functions, and they
carry every property the curve has. They are nonnegative. They sum to one at
every parameter value. And — the part that matters commercially — each of them is
zero outside a short interval.

Drag a control point in the left pane and watch what the right pane says about
it:

<BasisLocality />

The <C k="ghost">gray curve</C> is where the curve was before you touched
anything. Outside the shaded band, the live curve lies exactly on top of it — not
approximately, exactly, because the basis function belonging to the point you are
dragging is *identically zero* there. The band is that function's support, and the
meter under the left pane prints its endpoints. Move the point a mile; the far end
of the curve does not learn about it.

That is the property that makes splines usable on parts with thousands of control
points. A designer nudges a fillet and the software re-evaluates a handful of
polynomial spans, not the model.

Where do the functions come from? A recursion, due to Cox and de Boor, that
starts from indicator functions on the knot spans and climbs one degree at a
time:

$$
N_{i,p}(u) = \frac{u - u_i}{u_{i+p} - u_i} N_{i,p-1}(u) + \frac{u_{i+p+1} - u}{u_{i+p+1} - u_{i+1}} N_{i+1,p-1}(u) ,
$$

with $N_{i,0}$ equal to one on $[u_i, u_{i+1})$ and zero elsewhere, and any term
with a zero denominator read as zero. Each step of the recursion widens a
function's support by one span and raises its smoothness by one derivative. Set
the degree selector to 2, 3, 4 and count the dots on the vertical line: at degree
$p$, exactly $p+1$ basis functions are awake at a generic parameter. That is the
recursion's arithmetic, visible.

The <C k="knot">knots</C> $u_i$ are the green ticks. They are not control points
and they are not on the curve. They partition the parameter interval, and their
multiplicity sets continuity: an interior knot repeated $r$ times in a degree-$p$
spline leaves $C^{p-r}$ there. Repeat a knot $p$ times and you have manufactured
a corner out of a smooth basis, deliberately, with no special case anywhere in
the evaluator.

## Refinement Buys Coordinates, Not Shape

Here is where splines stop resembling a curve-fitting trick. You can add a knot
to a spline and get a new control point *for free* — the curve does not move.

<KnotInsert />

Press the button. The <C k="ghost">ghost polygon</C> is where the coordinates
were; the <C k="ctrl">amber polygon</C> is where they are now, and there is one
more of them. The two curves are drawn on top of each other, and the
<C k="basis">Δ meter</C> reports the largest distance between them sampled at 400
parameter values. It reads around $10^{-16}$ — floating-point noise, not
approximation error. Boehm's insertion formula makes each new control point a
convex combination of two adjacent old ones, and the algebra is exact.

Keep pressing at the same $u$ and the knot's multiplicity climbs, the local
continuity drops, and the curve grows a visible corner while still never moving.
Press once more and the figure tells you the parameter is full: at multiplicity
$p+1$ there is no coordinate left to buy there.

This is what "refinement" means in every spline-based system, and it is why the
representation survives contact with an editing session. Detail is added by
enriching the coordinate system, never by re-fitting the shape and hoping the
error stays small.

<Waypoint>
A B-spline is a finite-dimensional space of functions with a basis chosen so that
each coordinate has a short reach, the coefficients are a partition of unity, and
the space can be enlarged exactly. Everything after this is a modification to one
of those three sentences.
</Waypoint>

## The Denominator

A circle is the most-drawn shape in engineering and no B-spline can represent
one. The argument is two lines. Suppose $x(u)$ and $y(u)$ are real polynomials of
maximum degree $d \ge 1$ with $x^2 + y^2 \equiv 1$. The $u^{2d}$ coefficient of
the left side is $a_d^2 + b_d^2$, where $a_d, b_d$ are the leading coefficients,
and it must vanish. Over the reals that forces $a_d = b_d = 0$, contradicting
$d \ge 1$. So the only polynomial curves on the unit circle are the constant
ones.

The fix is to divide. Attach a positive weight $w_i$ to each control point and
normalize:

$$
C(u) = \frac{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i\, P_i}{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i} .
$$

That is a NURBS — a non-uniform rational B-spline. The basis is the same Cox–de
Boor basis; what changed is that the coefficients multiplying the control points
are now $N_i w_i / \sum_j N_j w_j$, which still sum to one, so the curve is still
an average of its control points and still lives in their convex hull.

<WeightPull />

The left pane holds one set of control points read two ways. With every weight at
1 the rational curve sits exactly on the polynomial one, one stroke; the readout
says so. Push a weight up and the curve leans toward that point. Push it toward
zero and the point loses its vote. A weight is not "importance" in any loose
sense — it is a coordinate in one dimension higher. Evaluate an ordinary
B-spline through the homogeneous points $\tilde{P_i} = (w_i P_i,\, w_i)$ in
$\mathbb{R}^3$, then divide by the last coordinate to come back down. The
division is the whole mechanism.

The right pane is the payoff. Three control points, degree two, weights
$(1, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, 1)$, and the <C k="curve">rational curve</C> lands on the
unit circle to $10^{-16}$ — the meter measures $\max\,\bigl|\,\lVert C(u)\rVert -
1\,\bigr|$ against a circle drawn from $\cos$ and $\sin$. The
<C k="hole">dashed curve</C> is the polynomial through the identical three points.
It is a parabola arc, it misses by about $6 \times 10^{-2}$, and no amount of
degree elevation will ever fix it, per the two-line argument above. Drag the
middle weight off $\sqrt2/2$ and watch the exactness go: the value is not a
tuning parameter, it is $\cos$ of the half-angle, and only that number works.

## What the Grid Charges for a Local Edit

Curves have been easy. Surfaces are where the representation starts sending
bills.

The standard way to get a spline surface is the tensor product: run one basis in
$u$, another in $v$, and lay control points on a rectangular grid,

$$
S(u,v) = \sum_i \sum_j N_{i,p}(u)\, M_{j,q}(v)\, P_{ij} .
$$

It inherits everything good — locality, exact refinement, the convex hull. It also
inherits a shape. The control points form a *grid*, and a grid has no way to hold
a new line that stops. Insert a knot in $u$ to get more control near one corner
and you have inserted a full column of control points spanning the entire patch,
most of them in places nobody wanted more control.

<RefineLocal />

Both panes receive the same edit request. The left is a tensor grid: the new
<C k="knot">knot line</C> crosses, and the counter charges you six control points.
The right is a T-mesh, which permits a line to terminate at a
<C k="topo">T-junction</C>, and the same edit costs two. Slide the strip to full
length and the right pane becomes the left pane — a tensor grid is the T-mesh in
which every line happens to run the whole way, which is why T-splines generalize
NURBS rather than replacing them.

The shaded rectangles are why this changes the mathematics and not just the
bookkeeping. A T-spline anchor infers its own knot vector by walking outward from
its position and collecting the lines it actually crosses. The
<C k="basis">violet anchor</C> sits beside the refinement strip, crosses it
immediately, and gets a small support. The <C k="hole">cyan anchor</C> is above
the T-junction, never meets that line, and reaches further. Same grid, two
different local knot vectors, inferred from the mesh.

That flexibility is not free, and this figure is honest about being a diagram of
support rather than a surface evaluator. Arbitrary T-junction arrangements do not
automatically preserve linear independence, partition of unity, or nestedness
under refinement — the properties everything above quietly depended on.
"Analysis-suitable" T-splines are the subclass where the combinatorics have been
restricted until those guarantees come back, and checking that condition is a real
part of the literature, not a footnote.

## A Surface Defined by Its Own Refinement

Everything so far has been a formula you evaluate. Subdivision surfaces take a
different route: define the surface as the *limit* of refining a mesh, and never
write down a closed form at all.

Catmull–Clark refinement replaces every face with quads. New points appear at
face centres and on edges, and every old vertex moves:

$$
P' = \frac{\bar F + 2\bar E + (n-3)\,P}{n} ,
$$

where $n$ is the vertex's valence, $\bar F$ averages the new face points of the
faces around it, and $\bar E$ averages the midpoints of its edges. Repeat.

<CageLimit />

The <C k="ctrl">cage</C> stays drawn over the <C k="curve">limit surface</C> at
every level, so you can watch the surface pull inside its own control mesh —
another partition-of-unity fact wearing different clothes. The counter multiplies
by four per level: 26 vertices and 24 quads after one step on a cube, 1,538 and
1,536 after four. Turn on the construction toggle to see one step's
<C k="basis">face points</C> and <C k="knot">edge points</C> before they are
absorbed.

The reason this exists alongside NURBS is topology. A tensor-product patch is a
rectangle; anything that is not a rectangle has to be assembled from trimmed
patches with continuity enforced across the seams by hand. A subdivision cage can
be any polygon mesh at all. The price shows in the meter: on a cube, every vertex
has valence 3, and the article's own vocabulary calls that *extraordinary*. At
valence 4 — the regular case — Catmull–Clark reproduces a uniform bicubic
B-spline surface exactly, formula and all. Away from valence 4 there is no such
formula; smoothness has to be established by studying the eigenstructure of the
subdivision matrix, and the surface is only defined as a limit. Lift the corner
and the limit follows, smoothly, with no patch layout and no seam conditions
anywhere.

That is the trade in one sentence: subdivision buys arbitrary mesh topology by
giving up the closed form.

<Waypoint>
Three bases, then. B-spline: polynomial, local, exactly refinable. NURBS: the same
basis divided by a weight sum, which buys conics. Subdivision: no basis in the
irregular regions at all, which buys arbitrary cage topology. None of the three
has said one word about which side of the surface is metal.
</Waypoint>

## Which Pieces Are Matter

A boundary representation is not a surface basis, and reading it as one is the
single most common way to misunderstand a CAD kernel. A B-rep is an oriented
topological structure. It says: this solid is bounded by this shell; the shell is
these faces; each face is a bounded region *of* some surface; each face's boundary
is a set of oriented loops of edges; each edge is a bounded interval of some
curve.

$$
\mathrm{Solid} \supset \mathrm{Shell} = \bigcup_j \mathrm{Face}_j, \qquad \partial\,\mathrm{Face} = \mathrm{Wire}_{\text{outer}} \sqcup \mathrm{Wire}_{\text{inner}} .
$$

Step through the selector:

<BrepStack />

Choose *Underlying Surface* and the figure draws the plane the top face lies on.
The plane is infinite. The face is not. Nothing about the plane knows there is a
hole in the plate — the <C k="hole">inner wire</C>, wound against the outer one,
is the only thing that says so. Geometry supplies the shape; topology selects the
region; neither is derivable from the other. Drag the hole slider and no surface
is re-fitted, because no surface changed.

The counter underneath is the Euler–Poincaré balance,

$$
V - E + F - R = 2\,(S - G) ,
$$

with $R$ the number of inner loops, $S$ the shells, $G$ the genus. This plate is a
torus, so the right side is zero, and it stays zero as you drag. The naive
$V - E + F = 2$ would miss the hole entirely: the hole does not live in the counts
of faces and edges, it lives in the loop structure, which is exactly the
information a mesh throws away.

This is also the honest answer to why boolean operations are hard. Subtracting one
solid from another means intersecting the support surfaces, constructing the
intersection curves, splitting faces and edges along them, classifying each
fragment as inside or outside, rebuilding shells from the survivors, and verifying
that the result is closed and consistently oriented. Every step is exact algebra
attempted in floating point. Tangential contacts, near-coincident faces, sliver
fragments, and features at the scale of the modelling tolerance are where kernels
fail — and they fail on the classification and stitching, not on evaluating a
basis function.

## Three Axes, Not Five Options

The durable version of all this is not a ranking.

**Basis** is how a function is written down: a finite set of coefficients against
a chosen set of functions. B-spline and NURBS live here, and the difference
between them is one division.

**Authoring** is what a person manipulates and how detail gets added: a control
polygon, a T-mesh that lets refinement stop, a polygon cage refined to a limit.
T-splines and subdivision live here, and both are answers to the tensor grid's
rigidity — one keeps the basis and loosens the mesh, the other keeps the mesh and
gives up the basis.

**Topology** is which bounded pieces of geometry bound matter. Only B-rep lives
here, and it is the only one of the five that can answer whether a point is inside
the part.

The question "should I use NURBS or SubD" is answerable. The question "should I
use NURBS or B-rep" is not a question — the part in the first figure had a cage,
a surface, and a boundary the entire time you were sliding.
