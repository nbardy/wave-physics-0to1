# Teaching a Solver to Guess

There is a neural network running a few centimeters below this paragraph. It has 809
weights, they are checked into this website's repository, and they were trained on the
fluid solver from lesson 01 — the same channel, the same disc, the same Gauss–Seidel
sweeps. It is doing real work. About two thirds of the arithmetic in a pressure solve
stops happening because of it.

It is also, by the only measurement that solver ever takes, worse than guessing zero.

<WarmStartRace height={250} />

Three panes, one moment of a fluid simulation. On the left is the defect the solver
has to fix: the amount by which the flow, at this instant, is trying to squeeze fluid
into places that are already full. Lesson 01 called that
<C k="div">divergence</C>, and it is still violet, and it is still the crime.

The middle pane and the right pane are both solving the same problem: find the
<C k="pHi">pressure</C> field whose gradient cancels that defect. The middle one
starts from nothing and sweeps. The right one starts from what the network proposed,
and the proposal wears <C k="dye">amber</C> — in this series amber marks the thing we
watch, and in this lesson the thing we watch is the network's word. Anything a
network computed also sits behind a dashed border, so even where color is busy doing
other work, the line style answers *who said this*.

In the first frame, the middle pane is blank and the right pane already holds a
nearly correct pressure field. The meters underneath disagree about which is which:
the blank pane reads a residual of 1.000, and the pane with most of the answer in it
reads 2.42 — by the number the solver steers by, the good guess is worse than an
empty grid.

Nothing is broken. The meter is honest and so is the picture, and the distance
between them is where a neural network can live inside a physics solver — and where
it had better not.

## The Seam

Lesson 01 ended by assembling the equation and then running it. One timestep of that
solver is four moves, in order — with whatever outside force there is, your finger or
a fan, added before the first of them:

$$\text{diffuse} \;\to\; \text{advect} \;\to\; \text{boundaries} \;\to\; \text{project}$$

The last one is the expensive one. *Project* is where the solver conjures the pressure
field that keeps the fluid incompressible — the silent bookkeeping from lesson 01's
last paragraph. It is expensive because, unlike the other four, it is not a formula
you evaluate. It is a linear system you have to solve:

$$\nabla^2 p = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*, \qquad \text{then} \qquad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* - \nabla p$$

Every cell's pressure depends on its neighbours', whose pressure depends on theirs,
all the way across the grid. On a 96 × 64 channel that is six thousand equations in
six thousand unknowns, and the solver has to answer them again before the next frame.

That is the seam. Three of those moves are local formulas with no notion of being
finished, and the fourth is a question with a checkable answer. You can hand the
solver a pressure field — any pressure field, from anywhere, computed by anything —
and one pass over the grid says how wrong it is. Nothing else in the timestep can be
audited that cheaply, and the audit is indifferent to where the field came from.
That indifference is the property everything below leans on.

## The Debt in the Timestep

Here is something lesson 01 did not tell you about its own solver. `project` does not
solve that system. It does forty Gauss–Seidel sweeps and stops.

Not forty sweeps *because* forty is enough. Forty because forty was the number in the
code. There is no check, no tolerance, and no report. Whatever defect is left over
after the fortieth sweep rides forward into the next timestep, and the solver has
never once mentioned it.

<SolveDebt height={250} />

The wake on the left is the lesson-01 cylinder, unchanged. The trace on the right is
the one line of instrumentation this lesson adds: after the projection has spent its
budget, how much of the defect is still there? The answer, at the shipped setting of
forty sweeps, is about a fifth of it — two hundred times the <C k="visc">gate</C> the
rest of this lesson will use, and the solver crosses that gate on precisely zero of
its timesteps.

At four sweeps the flow visibly changes character: the streaks stop mixing, the
wake thins, the fluid turns stiffer and more obedient than any real fluid is. At two
hundred, the defect falls — slowly — and the frame rate falls with it. Nowhere on
that slider does the trace touch the gate.

So the honest starting position: the simulation in lesson 01 is not converged, and
never was. It looks like a fluid because the leftover defect is smooth, small, and
partially cancelled on the next step — not because the pressure equation was solved.

## Five Ways to Be Wrong

Before asking whether learning improves a solver, name the error it is supposed to
reduce. There are five, they are genuinely different, and a technique that shrinks one
of them can be completely silent about the other four.

| the error | what it means | what a network would learn | what fixes it classically |
|---|---|---|---|
| **model-form** | the equations leave out a real stress, flux, or phase | a turbulence closure, a constitutive response, a discrepancy term | better physics |
| **discretization** | the grid and the timestep approximate the continuum badly | a stencil, a limiter, a coarse-grid correction | finer mesh, better schemes |
| **iterative** | the linear system was not solved tightly enough | a warm start, a preconditioner | more sweeps, a better solver |
| **input / state** | the geometry, the forcing, the initial condition are wrong | data assimilation, hidden-state inference | measurement |
| **tail / decision** | the mean is fine and the rare event is what mattered | error models, out-of-distribution detection | ensembles, calibration |

The trace above is the third row and only the third row. A network that helps with it
will not make the physics more correct, will not fix a coarse grid, and will not
reveal that the inflow speed was measured wrong. It will make the machine reach the
answer it was already going to reach, sooner — a much smaller claim than "deep
learning improves the simulation," and the only row whose claim a single pass over
the grid can certify.

<Waypoint>
The pressure projection is a linear system the solver never finishes, and the amount
it leaves unfinished is measurable in one pass over the grid. Everything from here is
about making that unfinished business cheaper — never about making it optional.
</Waypoint>

## Why Sweeping Is Slow

A Gauss–Seidel sweep is the simplest thing that could possibly work: walk the grid,
and set each cell's pressure to the average of its four neighbours, minus the local
defect. Repeat. It converges. It just converges at wildly different speeds depending
on *what shape* the remaining error has.

The demonstration below is that fact, isolated. The exact answer to the problem it is
solving is zero everywhere, so whatever is in the field *is* the error, and you can
watch it die. It starts as the sum of two patterns of equal size: one gentle bulge
spanning the whole box, and one fine ripple you can set with the slider.

<SlowModes height={240} />

The ripple is gone in about a dozen sweeps. The bulge is still there after six
hundred.

This is not a quirk of Gauss–Seidel; it is what local averaging *is*. Averaging a cell
with its neighbours can only move information one cell per sweep, so an error that
spans a hundred cells needs on the order of a hundred sweeps before the two ends of it
have even heard about each other — and thousands before they agree. An error that
spans three cells is negotiated away immediately.

Now put that next to the residual. The residual is $\nabla^2 p - b$, and $\nabla^2$ is
a difference of neighbours: it responds enormously to a field that wiggles between
adjacent cells and barely at all to a field that drifts gently across the whole
domain. The residual is loudest about exactly the errors that are cheapest to remove,
and quietest about the errors that cost thousands of sweeps.

So the meter the solver watches and the work the solver has left are, in the regime
that matters, almost anti-correlated. That is the whole strange arithmetic of the
opening figure, and it also says precisely what a useful guess would look like: get
the gentle, whole-domain part right, and don't waste a single parameter on the ripple.

## Learning the Smooth Half

Which is what the network is. All 809 numbers of it.

<ProposalAnatomy height={220} />

Read it left to right. The first pane is the defect — six thousand numbers. The second
pane is everything the network is permitted to look at: the same field averaged down
to 8 × 8 blocks, ninety-six numbers, ninety-eight percent of the information thrown
away before the first multiply.

That is not a compromise made for speed. It is the architecture stating what it
believes. A network that cannot see structure finer than eight cells cannot propose
structure finer than eight cells, so it can never spend effort on the ripple the
sweeps were going to erase for free — and it can never be blamed for getting the
ripple wrong.

From those ninety-six numbers it runs three 3 × 3 convolutions. Stacked, they span
seven coarse cells — seven of the twelve across the channel and seven of the eight
down it, which is most of the domain. That reach is the point: pressure is nonlocal — poke the fluid here and the pressure everywhere changes
— and three small local operations, stacked, is how you build a long-range response
out of short-range parts.

Then the coarse field is stretched back to full resolution, and that is the proposal:
the third pane. The fourth is the answer, from a conjugate-gradient solve run a
thousand times past the gate.

The two panes look almost the same, and the meter agrees: roughly nine tenths of the
field is already right. The other meter — the residual — reads 2.42, which is to say that if
you rank these two by the quantity the solver actually measures, an empty grid beats
it.

Two symmetries are built into the model rather than trained into it, because a network
that has to learn a symmetry burns capacity and still only gets it approximately.
Pressure scales linearly with the defect, so the input is divided by its own magnitude
on the way in and multiplied back on the way out; doubling the inflow speed cannot
surprise this network because it never sees a magnitude. And pressure carries a factor
of length squared, so the network's own numbers are read in units of the coarse cell's
size squared, which keeps them near one.

## Where the Data Came From

Six configurations of the lesson-01 channel — different inflow speeds, viscosities,
disc positions and sizes, some of them stirred with random impulses. Each one warmed
up and then sampled forty times, giving 240 divergence fields.

Those fields are not simulated stand-ins for what a solver sees. They are read
directly out of `solver.div` after a real timestep, which is the array `project`
itself writes before it starts sweeping. The network is trained on the exact defect
the exact solver hands it.

The targets are the converged pressure fields, from conjugate gradients run to a
relative residual of $10^{-5}$ — a hundred times tighter than the gate anything in
this lesson is judged against. Training is 8,000 steps of Adam on batches of twelve,
about a minute on a laptop, with the analytic gradient checked against finite
differences before the first step is taken.

And the loss is mean-squared error on the pressure field. Not on the residual.

That choice is the entire preceding section, cashed out. The residual is the quantity
you can measure without knowing the answer, which makes it the tempting thing to
train on, and it is the wrong thing to train on: minimizing it would push the network
to chase the high-frequency modes it cannot represent and does not need to, and would
teach it to ignore the smooth error that is the only thing it is there for. Train on
the error you are trying to remove, even when the error is the expensive quantity to
compute. You only have to compute it once, offline, in a script.

## The Race

<WarmStartRace height={250} knobs />

Same figure as the top of the page, now with its knobs exposed.

The first two cases are held-out fields — same family as the training set, parameters
the network never saw. Averaged over sixteen of them, the sweep count to reach a
relative residual of $10^{-3}$ falls from 2330 to 840. The network saves 64% of the
work, and the two paths agree on the answer to about a quarter of a percent of peak
pressure.

The gate buttons show where the headline number comes from. At a gate of $10^{-2}$
the speedup climbs above eight times; at $10^{-4}$ it falls to about two. The reason
is not subtle: a warm start is a head start, and the further away the finish line,
the less a head start is worth. A speedup only means something next to the tolerance
it stopped at.

The agreement number moves with the gate too. At $10^{-3}$ the two accepted answers
differ by 0.24% of peak pressure; at $10^{-2}$, by 6%. The gate is the contract
between the two paths, and its number is the contract's strength.

The last three cases are out of distribution — an airfoil at 14°, two discs, an open
channel with no obstacle at all. The speedup drops to about 1.6×. Notice what does
not drop.

## Name the Baseline

Everything so far is true and everything so far is incomplete, because a speedup is a
ratio and a ratio needs a denominator. Gauss–Seidel is what this series ships. It is
also, as solvers of this system go, a soft target — and an accelerator measured only
against a soft target has proved nothing except that the target was soft.

So: conjugate gradients. Same matrix, same right-hand side, same gate, no learning
anywhere, and published in 1952.

The network's 3.4× is real. It is also smaller than the 6.4× you get by changing
nothing about the machine learning and everything about the linear algebra — and the
two are not additive, because they are competing to remove the same smooth error.
Applied to the better solver, the network is worth 1.1×.

None of that makes the 3.4× a lie. It makes it an answer to a question about
Gauss–Seidel. "Our neural accelerator makes this solver 3.4× faster" and "a 1952
algorithm makes it 6.4× faster, and the accelerator then adds 11% on top" are the
same afternoon of measurements on the same field to the same tolerance — and only the
second one names the solver worth building on.

<Waypoint>
The network genuinely removes most of the smooth error, and so does a good classical
solver, and there is only one smooth error to remove. Whatever a learned component is
worth, it is worth it against the best baseline you were willing to implement — never
against the one you happened to have.
</Waypoint>

## What It Actually Learned

There is a temptation, once something works, to describe it as having learned the
operator: *the network learned to solve Poisson's equation*. The claim is checkable.
Poke the divergence field in exactly one place, and the response says what was
actually learned.

<ImpulseResponse height={330} />

The true response is the Green's function of the discrete Laplacian: a symmetric well,
deepest at the poke, falling away logarithmically, and — this is the part that
matters — the same shape wherever you put it. Drag the slider and the left pane's well
slides along with you, unchanged.

The right pane does not do that. It produces a blurred, blocky approximation, missing
the sharp centre entirely, with visible eight-cell seams from the grid it was built
on, and it is 40 to 45% wrong depending on where you put the poke — against 11% on the
wake fields it was trained on.

So the honest description is narrower and more useful: it learned what the pressure
around a wake in this channel tends to look like. That is a prior, not an operator —
and the sentence predicts the out-of-distribution numbers from the previous section
before the cases run.

## The Gate Is the Architecture

Which brings us back to the strange arithmetic at the top of the page, and to the
reason any of this is allowed near a simulation.

The network is 89% right about the pressure field. Suppose we simply believed it.

<UngatedRollout height={300} />

Both channels start from the same flow and run the same solver with the same network
in the same place. On the left, the proposal is the pressure field and nothing checks
it. On the right, it is a starting point and the solver sweeps from there — as many
times as you like.

The left channel does not drift. It comes apart in about eight timesteps, and the
wreckage names its own cause: those are eight-cell blocks, the seams of the coarse
grid the proposal was stretched from. The gradient of that proposal has a small step
at every seam, each step is a velocity error injected on every timestep at exactly the
wavelength the flow cannot dissipate, and the next step sees a bigger defect and
proposes a bigger pressure. The loop closes on itself.

Slide the correction sweeps down and you can find the edge yourself. Somewhere between
two sweeps and forty, "mostly right" stops being survivable.

None of this is a failure of the network. One pane over, the same weights are saving
two thirds of the work. It is a failure of a component with no check on its output
being asked to carry load.

So put the check back, and then attack the network directly. The slider below adds
noise to all 809 weights, from the trained model at the left end to a random function
of the divergence field at the right.

<SabotageGate height={300} />

Three numbers move as you drag, and they do not move together. The proposal's error
climbs and keeps climbing. The sweeps to the gate climb, cross the cold-start line
somewhere in the middle, and keep going — the accelerator becomes a decelerator, and
at full damage the solver would have been better off with no guess at all.

And the accepted answer does not move. At every position of that slider it agrees with
the pure cold-start solution to about a percent — and that number is not a property of
the network at all. It is set by the gate. Both fields were swept until the same
residual test passed, so the most they can differ by is what a residual of $10^{-3}$
permits, whatever put them in the neighbourhood.

That is the trade. **Behind a verifiable gate, a learned component can spend your
time and cannot spend your correctness.** Every number in the pressure story — the
2.8×, the 1.1×, the out-of-distribution slowdown, the failed impulse response — is a
claim about the clock, because the answer was never delegated.

## The Map of the Machine

The projection is one move out of four, and everything that made it safe — the
linear system, the residual, the one-pass audit — belongs to the move, not to the
machine. Here is the rest of the machine at the same magnification: what a network
would hold at each seam, and what would catch it lying.

| seam | a network would learn | error row | what checks it, in the loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| the state coming in | fill the gaps between sensors; infer what wasn't measured | input / state | held-out sensors — real, but outside the loop |
| the forces | unresolved forcing, roughness, control response | input / state | nothing local |
| diffuse | the drag of eddies the grid can't hold — a turbulence closure | model-form | nothing in the loop; hours against a finer run |
| advect | a flux, a limiter, a correction for what the grid loses | discretization | conservation — one pass, and it can only convict |
| boundaries | where the interface sits, how it breaks | discretization, model-form | geometry invariants, partial |
| **project** | a warm start, a preconditioner | iterative | **the residual — one pass, and it can acquit** |
| the whole step at once | a surrogate for the entire solver | every row at once | nothing — the checker was replaced with everything else |

None of these seams exist in Navier–Stokes. The equation has no advection step, no
sweep budget, no grid; refine the mesh and the equation sits unchanged while every
seam moves closer to it. A seam is a property of the machine, not of the physics —
which is why each one has its own error, its own learnable piece, and its own kind
of check.

The last column is the load-bearing one, and it is a ladder. The residual, on the
top rung, is an audit in the strict sense: pass it and the answer is right to
tolerance no matter what produced it — the fact the sabotage slider spent all of
last section demonstrating. Conservation, one rung down, is a law rather than an
audit. True motion always obeys it, but so do infinitely many wrong motions, so it
can convict a scheme of inventing mass and can never acquit one of being wrong. And
from the closure seam on down there is no meter in the loop at all — a learned
closure is graded by running the whole simulation against a finer one, hours per
verdict, and a whole-solver surrogate absorbs the very machinery that would have
done the grading.

The pressure seam sat on the top rung, which is why its story stayed safe even with
its network sabotaged into noise. One rung down: advection.

## What Averaging Steals

<SmearRace height={260} />

Two runs of the scheme lesson 01 taught — trace backward, interpolate — in the same
swirl, seeded with the same <C k="dye">dye</C>. The left pane spends 6,144 cells.
The right one spends what the buttons allow, and its meter reads the distance from
the standard, measured on its own grid, so blockiness alone scores nothing. What it
is charged for is the smear.

The thief is the interpolation. Every backtrace lands between cells; every landing
blends four neighbours; every blend is a small blur — the price of unconditional
stability, disclosed when lesson 01 bought it. On the fine grid the blur is a
fraction of a cell and the dye can afford it. At 24 × 16 each blur is four times
wider, and a few hundred steps dissolve the disc into fog. Nothing is unstable,
nothing explodes; the coarse lane is just steadily less true. That is the
discretization row of the error table, running live.

The classical exit is to pay: sixteen times the cells, sixteen times the arithmetic
per step, and on most schemes a finer timestep on top. The learned lane on this row
proposes the opposite trade — keep the budget, learn what the averaging steals.

## A Correction That Cannot Cheat

The network for this seam is 954 parameters, and its architecture is mostly a list
of things it is forbidden to do.

It does not output dye. It outputs *fluxes* — one number per face between
neighbouring cells, how much dye to slide across — and the correction is what the
slides add up to. Whatever leaves a cell lands in its neighbour, the wall faces are
pinned at zero, and the total is untouched by any setting of any weight.
Conservation is not in the training data; it is in the plumbing — the same move the
pressure net made with scale symmetry, applied to a law instead. Each flux is
capped at a fraction of the local dye scale, so one step can move at most a sliver
of a cell's contents, and the input is normalized by the dye's own magnitude, so a
brighter blob cannot surprise it.

The data is the same recipe as before. The fine solver manufactures truth — six
swirls, 240 steps each, every state averaged down to 24 × 16, 1,440 pairs — and the
loss asks the corrected coarse step to land on the next true state. Adam, six
thousand steps, the gradient checked against finite differences before the first
one. The training loss settles around 10⁻⁶; the corrected step matches the true
next state almost to the pixel. By every number the training run can see, the
problem is solved.

<OneStepDrift mode="failure" height={300} />

Run free, it wrecks the flow inside two hundred steps.

The cause is bookkeeping, not bad luck. Every pair in that training set begins from
truth — a restricted fine state. The model has never once seen its own work. Run
free, its input is its previous output carrying its previous error, a distribution
no training pair ever drew from, and its response to that unfamiliar error is, on
average, a slightly larger error. Recall the ungated pressure rollout: mostly
right, every step, compounding. Same disease — except this seam has its meter, and
the meter has nothing to say. The correction's mass ledger reads zero on every step
of the detonation, because the flux form cannot do otherwise; the one part of the
lane the check could ever have convicted is the one part provably innocent.

The loss was never allowed to watch the machine it lives in. So let it watch the
machine.

## The Solver in the Loss

The repair is to make training look like deployment. Unroll: the model takes
sixteen consecutive steps riding its own outputs, every step is scored against
truth, and the gradient is carried backward through the whole chain — through the
network sixteen times, and through the *solver* sixteen times. That last part
sounds expensive and is not: with the swirl frozen, a semi-Lagrangian step is a
fixed table of gathers, and a fixed gather has an exact adjoint. The literature
calls this *solver-in-the-loop* training, and the paper that named it is in Further
Reading. One further nudge: each training window starts from a slightly perturbed
state, so the model earns points only by pulling errors in — including errors it
did not make.

<OneStepDrift mode="both" height={330} />

Same 954 parameters, same data, same optimizer; the only change is what the loss
could see. By the end of the window the
one-step lane's error is measured in multiples of the entire field; the in-the-loop
lane sits at 50% against the plain solver's 47% — an identical opportunity to
detonate, declined at every step. And the number that should worry every benchmark
table: rescored on the one-step test, the very exam the first model was trained to
ace, the two models come out nearly tied — 1.4× apart. The property that separates
wreckage from an instrument is invisible at one step. It lives a few hundred steps
deep, and only a rollout can see it.

<FluxRollout height={330} />

Across held-out swirls the corrected lane runs ahead of plain for its first two to
three hundred steps — cutting the error by a third around step 100 on the ring —
and then the lanes trade places, case by case, with nothing detonating. On the swirl
half again faster than anything in training, the lead holds through the whole
window: the faster the flow, the more each backtrace smears, and the more a
smear-repairer has to do. The ceiling deserves equal billing. A plain run at
48 × 32 still beats every 24 × 16 lane on this page — 954 parameters buy back
roughly a third of the distance to a grid twice as fine, early, and less of it
late. The serious version of this seam, Kochkov's in Further Reading, spends far
more network on far more solver for its eight-to-ten-fold claims; what scales is
the shape of the trade, not this toy's numbers.

The strength slider is where this seam's meter finally shows its grade. A sharpener
overshoots — flecks of impossible <C k="div">violet</C> ink are the standing price
of anti-diffusion, and at 100% the error curves say the price is worth paying. Past
100% the overshoot compounds: the ringing deepens, the violet spreads, and the
error curve climbs over the lane with no correction at all. Through every bit of
it, the correction's mass ledger holds at zero. Wrong fluid, clean ledger.

The mass meters under the other two panes complete the indictment from the other
side. Both lanes *leak* — a third to nearly half of the dye over this window — because
backtrace-and-interpolate was never a conservative scheme; it blurs mass into
walls and clamps it at edges, a defect lesson 01 never mentioned because nothing
there measured it. So on this seam the one-pass meter convicts the honest
workhorse and has nothing on the vandal. That is what a necessary check is: real,
cheap, and one full rung below sufficient.

One more honesty item, and it is the one that matters. Every error curve in these
figures leans on the ghost, and the ghost costs sixteen times the lane it grades.
In production, the ghost is the run you were trying to avoid. What you can afford
on every step is the conservation meter — and the conservation meter cannot acquit.
On this seam, a clean bill of health means only that nothing has been caught yet.

## The Rungs Below

Below advection, the meters run out entirely.

The closure seam is the biggest prize on the map and the most exposed. A coarse
grid cannot resolve small eddies, so their drag on the resolved flow has to be
supplied from outside — the model-form row, the row where learning has genuinely
unknown physics to offer. It is also the row where the ghost stops being a teaching
aid and becomes the only judge there is: a closure is graded by running the whole
simulation against a finer one, hours per verdict, and its failure modes are
rollout-shaped — exactly the kind the one-step story just rehearsed.

The whole-step surrogate keeps the geometry and the forcing and replaces everything
else. The speedups are the largest on offer and the exposure is total: when the
entire solver is learned, there is no residual left to gate on, because the thing
that computed residuals was replaced too. The ungated pressure pane — which wrecked
a flow using a model that was 89% right — was the mild version of this, contained
to one stage of one timestep.

And above the whole map sits a quieter seam, the controller: which mesh, which
timestep, which fidelity, when to escalate to the expensive model. Decisions rather
than fields, graded only in calibration across many runs — the last row of the
error table.

The same map is being drawn in every field that simulates:

| field | structure kept fixed | the map that is learned | what verifies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| fluids | mass, momentum, the pressure projection | subgrid stress, or a solver accelerator | conservation, residual, measured forces |
| weather | resolved atmospheric dynamics | cloud and convection parameterization, or the whole forecast operator | forecast skill, extremes, ensemble calibration |
| molecules | Newtonian time integration | the potential-energy surface and its forces | energies, stability, rare chemistry |
| solids | balance laws, finite-element equilibrium | the constitutive law, plasticity, damage | thermodynamic admissibility, load paths, failure tests |

Every row keeps a different thing fixed and learns a different missing map. What
sorts the quiet wins from the loud headlines is the seam table's last column,
applied field by field: whether the check runs in the loop, or in a paper.

## Further Reading

Jonathan Tompson, Kristofer Schlachter, Pablo Sprechmann and Ken Perlin's
[*Accelerating Eulerian Fluid Simulation With Convolutional Networks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03597)
(ICML 2017) is the paper the pressure demonstration is a small, honest
descendant of — a network trained to supply the pressure field, with the
divergence and long-rollout discussion that motivated the ungated pane above.

Dmitrii Kochkov and colleagues'
[*Machine Learning–Accelerated Computational Fluid Dynamics*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2101784118)
(PNAS 2021) is the grown-up version of this article's second seam: learned
corrections inside a classical Navier–Stokes solver, matching a grid roughly eight
to ten times finer in each direction — with conservation carried, as here, by the
flux form rather than by hope.

Ayano Kaneda and colleagues' [*A Deep Conjugate Direction Method for Iteratively
Solving Linear Systems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10763) (ICML 2023) is the
version of this idea that takes the baseline question seriously: the network
supplies search directions *inside* a Krylov method, so it has to compete with
conjugate gradients rather than with a sweep counter.

Kiwon Um and colleagues' [*Solver-in-the-Loop*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00016)
(NeurIPS 2020) is the paper whose central experiment the second seam re-runs at toy
scale: models trained on single steps drift when rolled out, and letting the
differentiable solver into the loss is the repair. Everything about the in-the-loop
model above is their move, shrunk until it fits in a browser tab.

Jonathan Shewchuk's [*An Introduction to the Conjugate Gradient Method Without the
Agonizing Pain*](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~quake-papers/painless-conjugate-gradient.pdf)
(1994) is the friendliest possible route to the algorithm in the blue curve, and
it is where the spectral picture in *Why Sweeping Is Slow* comes from.

And for the classical side of the seam, William Briggs' *A Multigrid Tutorial* is
the book-length version of this lesson's central observation — that error has a
shape, that smooth error is the expensive kind, and that the fix is to look at it
on a coarser grid. Achi Brandt's
[*Multi-Level Adaptive Solutions to Boundary-Value Problems*](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~achi/LOP16.pdf)
got there in 1977 without a training set, and any learned accelerator is, whether
or not it says so, competing with it.

## Final Words

Two networks live in this lesson — 809 parameters and 954 — and between them they
know nothing about fluids. One holds an opinion about what pressure near a wake
looks like, the other about what averaging steals from a moving edge, and every
true thing either one produces is made true by the machinery around it: sweeps that
finish a guess, a flux form that cannot mint dye, a residual that defines done, a
fine run that defined true.

I keep coming back to how old the load-bearing parts are. The gate that held while
a network was sabotaged into noise is a sum and a comparison. The repair that saved
the second model was not a bigger network — it was a solver from 1999 allowed into
the loss function. The newest instruments in computing, held honest by some of the
oldest.

The next time a headline says a neural network simulates physics a thousand times
faster, the number is probably real. Three questions give it its meaning: which of
the five errors it reduces, which baseline it beat, and what checks the answer once
the inputs drift from home. Fields with a cheap third answer are about to get very
fast. The rest are waiting for something better than a ghost.
