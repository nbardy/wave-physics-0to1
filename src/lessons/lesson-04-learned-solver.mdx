# Teaching a Solver to Guess

There is a neural network running a few centimeters below this paragraph. It has 809
weights, they are checked into this website's repository, and they were trained on the
fluid solver from lesson 01 — the same channel, the same disc, the same Gauss–Seidel
sweeps. It is doing real work. About two thirds of the arithmetic in a pressure solve
stops happening because of it.

It is also, by the only measurement that solver ever takes, worse than guessing zero.

<WarmStartRace height={250} />

Three panes, one moment of a fluid simulation. On the left is the defect the solver
has to fix: the amount by which the flow, at this instant, is trying to squeeze fluid
into places that are already full. Lesson 01 called that
<C k="div">divergence</C>, and it is still violet, and it is still the crime.

The middle pane and the right pane are both solving the same problem: find the
<C k="pHi">pressure</C> field whose gradient cancels that defect. The middle one
starts from nothing and sweeps. The right one starts from what the network proposed —
and I will use <C k="dye">amber</C> for that proposal throughout this lesson and for
nothing else, so a color always answers *who said this*. In every other lesson in this
series amber is the thing we watch. Here it is the thing we do not trust.

Watch the first frame. The middle pane is blank; the right pane already has a
pressure field in it, and the field is nearly correct. Now read the two meters
underneath. The blank pane has a residual of 1.000. The pane with a nearly correct
answer in it has a residual of 2.42.

Nothing is broken. The residual is telling the truth. So is the picture. Getting those
two facts to sit in the same head is most of what there is to learn here, and once
they do, the question *where should a neural network go inside a physics simulation*
answers itself.

## The Seam

Lesson 01 ended by assembling the equation and then running it. One timestep of that
solver is four moves, in order — with whatever outside force there is, your finger or
a fan, added before the first of them:

$$\text{diffuse} \;\to\; \text{advect} \;\to\; \text{boundaries} \;\to\; \text{project}$$

The last one is the expensive one. *Project* is where the solver conjures the pressure
field that keeps the fluid incompressible — the silent bookkeeping from lesson 01's
last paragraph. It is expensive because, unlike the other four, it is not a formula
you evaluate. It is a linear system you have to solve:

$$\nabla^2 p = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*, \qquad \text{then} \qquad \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{u}^* - \nabla p$$

Every cell's pressure depends on its neighbours', whose pressure depends on theirs,
all the way across the grid. On a 96 × 64 channel that is six thousand equations in
six thousand unknowns, and the solver has to answer them again before the next frame.

That is the seam. Three of those moves are local formulas with no notion of being
finished, and the fourth is a question with a checkable answer. You can hand the solver a
pressure field — any pressure field, from anywhere, computed by anything — and it can
tell you in one pass over the grid how wrong that field is. Nothing else in the
timestep has that property.

Hold onto it. It is going to turn out to be the entire safety argument.

## The Debt in the Timestep

Here is something lesson 01 did not tell you about its own solver. `project` does not
solve that system. It does forty Gauss–Seidel sweeps and stops.

Not forty sweeps *because* forty is enough. Forty because forty was the number in the
code. There is no check, no tolerance, and no report. Whatever defect is left over
after the fortieth sweep rides forward into the next timestep, and the solver has
never once mentioned it.

<SolveDebt height={250} />

The wake on the left is the lesson-01 cylinder, unchanged. The trace on the right is
the one line of instrumentation this lesson adds: after the projection has spent its
budget, how much of the defect is still there? The answer, at the shipped setting of
forty sweeps, is about a fifth of it — two hundred times the <C k="visc">gate</C> the
rest of this lesson will use, and the solver crosses that gate on precisely zero of
its timesteps.

Drag the budget down to four sweeps and the flow visibly changes: the streaks stop
mixing, the wake thins, the fluid gets stiffer and more obedient than any real fluid
is. Drag it up to two hundred and the defect falls, slowly, and the frame rate falls
with it. Nowhere on that slider does the trace touch the gate.

This is worth sitting with, because it is the honest starting position for everything
that follows. The simulation in lesson 01 is not converged. It was never converged.
It looks like a fluid because the leftover defect is smooth and small and gets
partially cancelled next step, not because the pressure equation was solved.

## Five Ways to Be Wrong

Before asking whether learning improves a solver, name the error it is supposed to
reduce. There are five, they are genuinely different, and a technique that shrinks one
of them can be completely silent about the other four.

| the error | what it means | what fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **model-form** | the equations leave out a real stress, flux, or phase | better physics; a closure that supplies the missing term |
| **discretization** | the grid and the timestep approximate the continuum badly | finer mesh, better stencils, higher-order fluxes |
| **iterative** | the linear system was not solved tightly enough | more sweeps, a better solver, a better initial guess |
| **input / state** | the geometry, the forcing, the initial condition are wrong | measurement, data assimilation, inverse modelling |
| **tail / decision** | the mean is fine and the rare event is what mattered | ensembles, uncertainty, calibration |

The trace you just watched is the third row and only the third row. A neural network
that helps with it will not make the physics more correct, will not fix a coarse
grid, and will not tell you that your inflow speed was measured wrong. It will make
the machine reach the answer it was already going to reach, sooner.

That is a much smaller claim than "deep learning improves the simulation." It is also
the only one in the table with a meter attached, which is why it is the one this
lesson can actually settle.

<Waypoint>
The pressure projection is a linear system the solver never finishes, and the amount
it leaves unfinished is measurable in one pass over the grid. Everything from here is
about making that unfinished business cheaper — never about making it optional.
</Waypoint>

## Why Sweeping Is Slow

A Gauss–Seidel sweep is the simplest thing that could possibly work: walk the grid,
and set each cell's pressure to the average of its four neighbours, minus the local
defect. Repeat. It converges. It just converges at wildly different speeds depending
on *what shape* the remaining error has.

The demonstration below is that fact, isolated. The exact answer to the problem it is
solving is zero everywhere, so whatever is in the field *is* the error, and you can
watch it die. It starts as the sum of two patterns of equal size: one gentle bulge
spanning the whole box, and one fine ripple you can set with the slider.

<SlowModes height={240} />

The ripple is gone in about a dozen sweeps. The bulge is still there after six
hundred.

This is not a quirk of Gauss–Seidel; it is what local averaging *is*. Averaging a cell
with its neighbours can only move information one cell per sweep, so an error that
spans a hundred cells needs on the order of a hundred sweeps before the two ends of it
have even heard about each other — and thousands before they agree. An error that
spans three cells is negotiated away immediately.

Now put that next to the residual. The residual is $\nabla^2 p - b$, and $\nabla^2$ is
a difference of neighbours: it responds enormously to a field that wiggles between
adjacent cells and barely at all to a field that drifts gently across the whole
domain. The residual is loudest about exactly the errors that are cheapest to remove,
and quietest about the errors that cost thousands of sweeps.

So the meter the solver watches and the work the solver has left are, in the regime
that matters, almost anti-correlated. That is the whole strange arithmetic of the
opening figure, and it also says precisely what a useful guess would look like: get
the gentle, whole-domain part right, and don't waste a single parameter on the ripple.

## Learning the Smooth Half

Which is what the network is. All 809 numbers of it.

<ProposalAnatomy height={220} />

Read it left to right. The first pane is the defect — six thousand numbers. The second
pane is everything the network is permitted to look at: the same field averaged down
to 8 × 8 blocks, ninety-six numbers, ninety-eight percent of the information thrown
away before the first multiply.

That is not a compromise made for speed. It is the architecture stating what it
believes. A network that cannot see structure finer than eight cells cannot propose
structure finer than eight cells, so it can never spend effort on the ripple the
sweeps were going to erase for free — and it can never be blamed for getting the
ripple wrong.

From those ninety-six numbers it runs three 3 × 3 convolutions. Stacked, they span
seven coarse cells — seven of the twelve across the channel and seven of the eight
down it, which is most of the domain. That reach is the point: pressure is nonlocal — poke the fluid here and the pressure everywhere changes
— and three small local operations, stacked, is how you build a long-range response
out of short-range parts.

Then the coarse field is stretched back to full resolution, and that is the proposal:
the third pane. The fourth is the answer, from a conjugate-gradient solve run a
thousand times past the gate.

The two panes look almost the same, and the meter agrees: roughly nine tenths of the
field is already right. The other meter — the residual — reads 2.42, which is to say that if
you rank these two by the quantity the solver actually measures, an empty grid beats
it.

Two symmetries are built into the model rather than trained into it, because a network
that has to learn a symmetry burns capacity and still only gets it approximately.
Pressure scales linearly with the defect, so the input is divided by its own magnitude
on the way in and multiplied back on the way out; doubling the inflow speed cannot
surprise this network because it never sees a magnitude. And pressure carries a factor
of length squared, so the network's own numbers are read in units of the coarse cell's
size squared, which keeps them near one.

## Where the Data Came From

Six configurations of the lesson-01 channel — different inflow speeds, viscosities,
disc positions and sizes, some of them stirred with random impulses. Each one warmed
up and then sampled forty times, giving 240 divergence fields.

Those fields are not simulated stand-ins for what a solver sees. They are read
directly out of `solver.div` after a real timestep, which is the array `project`
itself writes before it starts sweeping. The network is trained on the exact defect
the exact solver hands it.

The targets are the converged pressure fields, from conjugate gradients run to a
relative residual of $10^{-5}$ — a hundred times tighter than the gate anything in
this lesson is judged against. Training is 8,000 steps of Adam on batches of twelve,
about a minute on a laptop, with the analytic gradient checked against finite
differences before the first step is taken.

And the loss is mean-squared error on the pressure field. Not on the residual.

That choice is the entire preceding section, cashed out. The residual is the quantity
you can measure without knowing the answer, which makes it the tempting thing to
train on, and it is the wrong thing to train on: minimizing it would push the network
to chase the high-frequency modes it cannot represent and does not need to, and would
teach it to ignore the smooth error that is the only thing it is there for. Train on
the error you are trying to remove, even when the error is the expensive quantity to
compute. You only have to compute it once, offline, in a script.

## The Race

<WarmStartRace height={250} knobs />

Same figure as the top of the page, now with its knobs exposed.

The first two cases are held-out fields — same family as the training set, parameters
the network never saw. Averaged over sixteen of them, the sweep count to reach a
relative residual of $10^{-3}$ falls from 2330 to 840. The network saves 64% of the
work, and the two paths agree on the answer to about a quarter of a percent of peak
pressure.

The gate buttons are worth a minute on their own, because they show where the headline
number comes from. Loosen the gate to $10^{-2}$ and the speedup climbs above eight
times. Tighten it to $10^{-4}$ and it falls to about two. The reason is not subtle: a
warm start is a head start, and the further away the finish line is, the less a head
start is worth. Any speedup quoted for a technique like this is a function of how much
correctness you were willing to stop short of, and quoting it without the tolerance is
quoting nothing.

Watch the agreement number as you loosen the gate too. At $10^{-3}$ the two accepted
answers differ by 0.24% of peak pressure; at $10^{-2}$ they differ by 6%. The gate is
not a formality attached to the end of the pipeline. It *is* the contract, and its
number is the strength of the contract.

The last three cases are out of distribution — an airfoil at 14°, two discs, an open
channel with no obstacle at all. The speedup drops to about 1.6×. Notice what does
not drop.

## Name the Baseline

Everything so far is true and everything so far is incomplete, because a speedup is a
ratio and a ratio needs a denominator. Gauss–Seidel is what this series ships. It is
also, as solvers of this system go, a soft target — and an accelerator measured only
against a soft target has proved nothing except that the target was soft.

So: conjugate gradients. Same matrix, same right-hand side, same gate, no learning
anywhere, and published in 1952.

The horizontal axis is passes over the grid rather than iterations, because iterations

| | passes to the gate |
|---|---|
| sweeps, from zero | 2679 |
| sweeps, from the network | 792 |
| conjugate gradients, from zero | 420 |
| conjugate gradients, from the network | 378 |

The network's 3.4× is real. It is also smaller than the 6.4× you get by changing
nothing about the machine learning and everything about the linear algebra — and the
two are not additive, because they are competing to remove the same smooth error.
Applied to the better solver, the network is worth 1.1×.

None of that makes the 3.4× a lie. It makes it an answer to a question about
Gauss–Seidel. "Our neural accelerator makes this solver 3.4× faster" and "a 1952
algorithm makes it 6.4× faster, and the accelerator then adds 11% on top of that" are
the same afternoon of measurements, on the same field, to the same tolerance. Only one
of them tells you what to do on Monday.

<Waypoint>
The network genuinely removes most of the smooth error, and so does a good classical
solver, and there is only one smooth error to remove. Whatever a learned component is
worth, it is worth it against the best baseline you were willing to implement — never
against the one you happened to have.
</Waypoint>

## What It Actually Learned

There is a temptation, once something works, to describe it as having learned the
operator: *the network learned to solve Poisson's equation*. It is easy to check
whether that is true. Poke the divergence field in one place and look at the response.

<ImpulseResponse height={330} />

The true response is the Green's function of the discrete Laplacian: a symmetric well,
deepest at the poke, falling away logarithmically, and — this is the part that
matters — the same shape wherever you put it. Drag the slider and the left pane's well
slides along with you, unchanged.

The right pane does not do that. It produces a blurred, blocky approximation, missing
the sharp centre entirely, with visible eight-cell seams from the grid it was built
on, and it is 40 to 45% wrong depending on where you put the poke — against 11% on the
wake fields it was trained on.

So the honest description is narrower and more useful: it learned what the pressure
around a wake in this channel tends to look like. It is a very good prior and it is
not an operator. That single sentence predicts the out-of-distribution numbers from
the previous section before you measure them, which is the test of whether a
description of a model is worth anything.

## The Gate Is the Architecture

Which brings us back to the strange arithmetic at the top of the page, and to the
reason any of this is allowed near a simulation.

The network is 89% right about the pressure field. Suppose we simply believed it.

<UngatedRollout height={300} />

Both channels start from the same flow and run the same solver with the same network
in the same place. On the left, the proposal is the pressure field and nothing checks
it. On the right, it is a starting point and the solver sweeps from there — as many
times as you like.

The left channel does not drift. It comes apart in about eight timesteps, and the
wreckage names its own cause: those are eight-cell blocks, the seams of the coarse
grid the proposal was stretched from. The gradient of that proposal has a small step
at every seam, each step is a velocity error injected on every timestep at exactly the
wavelength the flow cannot dissipate, and the next step sees a bigger defect and
proposes a bigger pressure. The loop closes on itself.

Slide the correction sweeps down and you can find the edge yourself. Somewhere between
two sweeps and forty, "mostly right" stops being survivable.

None of this is a failure of the network. One pane over, the same weights are saving
two thirds of the work. It is a failure of a component with no check on its output
being asked to carry load.

So put the check back, and then attack the network directly. The slider below adds
noise to all 809 weights, from the trained model at the left end to a random function
of the divergence field at the right.

<SabotageGate height={300} />

Three numbers move as you drag, and they do not move together. The proposal's error
climbs and keeps climbing. The sweeps to the gate climb, cross the cold-start line
somewhere in the middle, and keep going — the accelerator becomes a decelerator, and
at full damage the solver would have been better off with no guess at all.

And the accepted answer does not move. At every position of that slider it agrees with
the pure cold-start solution to about a percent — and that number is not a property of
the network at all. It is set by the gate. Both fields were swept until the same
residual test passed, so the most they can differ by is what a residual of $10^{-3}$
permits, whatever put them in the neighbourhood.

That is the trade, stated as sharply as this article knows how to state it. **Behind a
verifiable gate, a learned component can spend your time and cannot spend your
correctness.** Every claim in this lesson — the 2.8×, the 1.1×, the out-of-distribution
degradation, the failure of the impulse response — is a claim about the clock. Not one
of them is a claim about whether the answer is right, because the answer's correctness
was never delegated.

## The Seams Without Gates

The pressure solve is the friendliest seam in a fluid solver, and it is fair to ask
what happens at the others. Two are worth naming, because they are where most of the
field's real work is, and because both give up the property this whole article rests
on.

**Learned closure.** A coarse grid cannot resolve small eddies, so their effect on the
resolved flow — a stress, a flux, a drag — has to be supplied from outside. That is
the *model-form* row of the table, the first row, and it is the row where learning has
the most to offer: the missing term is genuinely unknown, and there is data. It is
also a row with no residual. You cannot hand a closure's output to a one-pass check
and ask whether it is right; you find out by running the simulation and comparing
against a finer one, which is a verification loop measured in hours rather than
microseconds.

**Whole-solver surrogates.** Wrap the entire timestep — geometry and forcing in, future
flow field out. The speedups are enormous and so is the exposure, because now nothing
in the pipeline is classical and there is no residual left to gate on. What you saw in
the ungated pane is the mildest possible version of this failure mode, produced by a
model that was 89% right.

The same shape appears in four fields, which is the argument that this is
architecture rather than a fluids trick:

| field | structure kept fixed | the map that is learned | what verifies it |
|---|---|---|---|
| fluids | mass, momentum, the pressure projection | subgrid stress, or a solver accelerator | conservation, residual, measured forces |
| weather | resolved atmospheric dynamics | cloud and convection parameterization, or the whole forecast operator | forecast skill, extremes, ensemble calibration |
| molecules | Newtonian time integration | the potential-energy surface and its forces | energies, stability, rare chemistry |
| solids | balance laws, finite-element equilibrium | the constitutive law, plasticity, damage | thermodynamic admissibility, load paths, failure tests |

Every row keeps a different thing fixed and learns a different missing map. What
separates the good rows from the exciting ones is whether the fourth column can be
evaluated in the loop or only in a paper.

## Further Reading

Jonathan Tompson, Kristofer Schlachter, Pablo Sprechmann and Ken Perlin's
[*Accelerating Eulerian Fluid Simulation With Convolutional Networks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03597)
(ICML 2017) is the paper this article's demonstration is a small, honest
descendant of — a network trained to supply the pressure field, with the
divergence and long-rollout discussion that motivated the ungated pane above.

Ayano Kaneda and colleagues' [*A Deep Conjugate Direction Method for Iteratively
Solving Linear Systems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10763) (ICML 2023) is the
version of this idea that takes the baseline question seriously: the network
supplies search directions *inside* a Krylov method, so it has to compete with
conjugate gradients rather than with a sweep counter.

Kiwon Um and colleagues' [*Solver-in-the-Loop*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00016)
(NeurIPS 2020) is the cleanest statement of why a model trained on single steps
behaves badly over a rollout, and what training it inside the solver does about it.

Jonathan Shewchuk's [*An Introduction to the Conjugate Gradient Method Without the
Agonizing Pain*](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~quake-papers/painless-conjugate-gradient.pdf)
(1994) is the friendliest possible route to the algorithm in the blue curve, and
it is where the spectral picture in *Why Sweeping Is Slow* comes from.

And for the classical side of the seam, William Briggs' *A Multigrid Tutorial* is
the book-length version of this lesson's central observation — that error has a
shape, that smooth error is the expensive kind, and that the fix is to look at it
on a coarser grid. Achi Brandt's
[*Multi-Level Adaptive Solutions to Boundary-Value Problems*](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~achi/LOP16.pdf)
got there in 1977 without a training set, and any learned accelerator is, whether
or not it says so, competing with it.

## Final Words

The interesting thing about the network in this article is not that it works. Small
networks trained on their own solver's output have been supplying warm starts for the
better part of a decade, and 809 parameters buying 64% of a Poisson solve is a
pleasant result, not a surprising one.

The interesting thing is the shape of what it is allowed to do. It never touches the
equation. It never touches the answer. It offers an opinion, the opinion is checked
by arithmetic that predates it by seventy years, and the check is cheap enough to run
every single time. Break the network and you get a slower simulation of the same
fluid. There is no setting of that slider, no field it has never seen, no failure of
generalization that turns into a wrong flow — because the part that decides what is
right was never the part that learned.

I do not think that is a limitation to be engineered away. I think it is the design.
The seams in a physics solver worth putting a model into are the ones where being
wrong is expensive and *detectable*, and the field's hard problem is not building
bigger models for the other seams. It is finding, for each of them, something a
solver can check in one pass over the grid.
