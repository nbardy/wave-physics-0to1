# The Jacobian and the Hessian

Below, a plane has been stirred. Every line of the grid was straight before the
stirring; none of them is straight now. The ring is a loupe — you can drag it
anywhere on the warped plane, and the right pane shows what the loupe sees. The
slider is its magnification:

<WarpLoupe mode="plant" />

Push the slider all the way toward *close*, then drag the ring around. The swerving
drains out of the picture, and what stands at the bottom of every zoom is the same
kind of thing: a lattice of identical parallelograms — tilted one way here, sheared
another way there, but always a lattice, always perfect. The stirring never manages
to be complicated up close. The amber patch riding the loupe is a small square
inked onto the plane before the stirring; I re-ink it to match each magnification,
since a real square would shrink out of sight. It lands as one parallelogram of
the same lattice.

A parallelogram lattice is a plain thing. Four numbers pin it down completely, and
those four numbers are different at every point of the plane. That little box of
four is the Jacobian. Inside it hide two more things: the area of the
parallelogram, and — much later — an entire second matrix wearing the first one's
structure. The Hessian never needs its own theory; it is this same picture, aimed
at a different target.

Every quantity here keeps one color throughout: the
<C k="stamp">stamp we watch</C> is always amber, the
<C k="ex">east arrow</C> is always blue, the
<C k="ey">north arrow</C> is always green, every
<C k="area">measured meter</C> — areas, curvatures — is violet, and on the
landscapes of the second half, <C k="hi">high ground</C> is red,
<C k="lo">low ground</C> cyan, and the <C k="grad">downhill arrows</C> pink. None
of the names need memorizing; the colors do the remembering.

## One Number Was Enough

The zoom is not a new instrument. It is the whole of first-year calculus, run
backwards. Below is an ordinary curve; drag left or right to move the marked point,
and push the magnification:

<ZoomLine />

Notice the meter. It reports the slope of whatever is actually in the window —
rise over run between the two visible ends of the curve — and as the window
shrinks, that measured slope stops changing. The curve has committed to a line.
*Differentiable at a point* means exactly this and nothing more: zoom in far enough
and the wiggling gives up. The derivative is the number the meter converges to,

$$
f(a+h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h ,
$$

good for small $h$ and better for smaller. One number suffices because the thing at
the bottom of the zoom is a line through a point, and a line through a point has
one degree of freedom: its slope.

The hero figure already showed us the same convergence with a different ending.
Zoom into the stirred plane and the picture also commits — but it commits to a
*lattice*, and a lattice through a point does not have one degree of freedom. The
next two sections earn its four.

## Maps, Not Graphs

First, what kind of function stirs a plane. The curve
above eats one number and returns one number, so its graph fits on a screen. The
stirring eats a point of the plane and returns a point of the plane — written out,

$$
F(x, y) = \bigl(f_1(x,y),\; f_2(x,y)\bigr),
$$

two inputs, two outputs. Its graph lives in four dimensions, and no amount of
cleverness fits four dimensions on a screen. So we stop graphing and watch the
plane itself: draw a grid, apply $F$ to every point, and look at what the grid
becomes. That is the picture the hero has been showing all along — not a graph
*of* the stirring but the stirred plane itself.

To measure a stirring, we need a probe. Ours is the amber square the hero's loupe
has been carrying, and it finally gets its name: the <C k="stamp">stamp</C> — inked
onto the plane before the map acts, with a dot on one corner so we can tell
whether it lands right-side out. Where the map is gentle the
stamp lands almost square; where the map is violent it lands as a bent little rag.
One stamp is draggable below; two more are pinned for comparison, and each landed
stamp carries a violet meter reading the one number a first instinct asks for —
how much the map *scaled* it:

<WarpStamp />

Drag the bright stamp anywhere and watch the three meters. They agree, everywhere,
to two decimal places: ×1.00. This particular stirring happens to preserve every
area exactly — and yet the three landed shapes could hardly look more different:
one nearly square, one sheared into a lozenge, one bent around the vortex. The
scale-factor meter is not wrong; it is blind. Whatever "the local behavior of the
map" is, it is not one number. A number cannot say *which way*.

## The Landing Report

Arrows can. Plant two of them on the stamp — <C k="ex">east</C> and
<C k="ey">north</C>, each one grid-cell long — and ask the only question that
survives the previous figure: where does each arrow *land*? Below, the loupe from
the hero returns with the arrows riding it. Dashed ghosts mark where each arrow
would land if the map did nothing, so the map's opinion is visible as the gap
between ghost and arrow. The selector switches between four maps; the fourth is
our stirring:

<WarpLoupe mode="arrows" />

The box in the corner is nothing but the landing report, filed in columns: the
first column holds the two coordinates of where <C k="ex">east</C> landed, the
second column where <C k="ey">north</C> landed. Read the report while switching
maps. *Do nothing* files ones and zeros — each arrow lands on its own ghost.
*Rotate 30°* files cosines and sines. *Shear* moves only one arrow. And the swirl
files a report that changes as you drag, because a stirring treats every
neighborhood differently.

That box is the Jacobian of the map at that point. The symbols, now that the thing
itself has been seen: writing $\partial f_1/\partial x$ for "how fast the first
output coordinate changes per step east," the landing of the east arrow is
$(\partial f_1/\partial x,\; \partial f_2/\partial x)$, the landing of the north
arrow is the $y$-partials, and the report reads

$$
J \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\\[10pt]
\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

columns as landings. The partial-derivative grid is a filing system for a picture:
this is the fact the standard treatments state last, if at all, and it is why the
matrix multiplies vectors the way it does — $J$ applied to any small displacement
$h$ just resolves $h$ into its east and north parts and sends each part to its
landing,

$$
F(p + h) \;\approx\; F(p) + J\,h .
$$

This is the same sentence as the one-dimensional one, with the multiplication
promoted to a matrix acting on a vector. Sanity checks, straight from the
selector: for *do nothing*, $J$ is the identity and the approximation is exact.
For *rotate 30°*, $J$ is the rotation matrix, exactly, everywhere — a rotation is
already linear, so the zoom has nothing left to discard. Only the swirl needs the
$\approx$, and the loupe shows what the $\approx$ costs: back the magnification
off and the lattice picks its curvature back up.

## The Area Receipt

Two of the four numbers were promised early: the parallelogram's area, hiding in
the report. The stamp's landed copy *is* the parallelogram with
<C k="ex">east's landing</C> and <C k="ey">north's landing</C> as its sides, and
the area of that parallelogram is the cross-product combination

$$
\det J \;=\;
\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x}\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial y}
-
\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x},
$$

the *determinant* — the map's local area factor. The swirl scored ×1.00 on every
meter because a swirl only rotates each ring of the plane, and rotations spend no
area; $\det J = 1$ there identically, which is the honest content of the meters
agreeing. The meter below watches a different map, one built to misbehave: the
slider folds the plane toward itself like a sheet of paper being pushed from both
ends.

The meter crosses. Watch the stamp's corner dot on the way through: as the fold
passes, the stamp thins to a needle — that is the instant of $\det J = 0$, the
whole square crushed onto a line, two dimensions of ink surviving as one — and
what unfolds on the far side is the stamp *mirrored*, dot on the wrong side. The
negative sign is not bookkeeping; it is the picture of the plane locally turned
over. And the needle instant explains a fact usually delivered as a decree: a map
can be undone only where $\det J \neq 0$. At the needle, two different points of
the stamp got sent to one landing point, and no formula recovers information that
was never delivered.

The determinant is also the resolution of a small scandal from integration. Every
change of variables drags a mysterious factor into the integral — the $r$ in
$r\,dr\,d\theta$ being the one everybody meets first. That $r$ is a determinant:
the polar-coordinates map $(r, \theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta,\, r\sin\theta)$ has
$\det J = r$, so a stamp inked near the origin lands tiny while a stamp at radius
ten lands enormous, and the integral must pay each patch by its landed area:

$$
\iint g\,dx\,dy \;=\; \iint g \; \lvert\det J\rvert \;dr\,d\theta .
$$

The absolute value is there because integration pays for area regardless of
whether the stamp came out mirrored.

<Waypoint>
You can now read any smooth map of the plane the way the loupe does: at each
point, a lattice; the lattice, four numbers filed as two landings; the landings'
parallelogram area, one signed number that prices integrals and prophesies where
the map can be undone. All of it earned by zooming.
</Waypoint>

## A Field of Arrows

The object below looks like a change of subject and is not. It is a landscape: one height at every point,
$f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, drawn as <C k="hi">high ground</C> in red and
<C k="lo">low ground</C> in cyan. On it stands the gradient — at every point, the
<C k="grad">arrow</C> $\nabla f$ pointing straight uphill, longer where the ground
is steeper. Drag the ring and the right pane isolates your point's arrow:

<GradField />

*Point in, arrow out* is a map from the plane to the plane — the gradient of a
landscape is exactly the kind of object we spent four sections learning to read. So it has a Jacobian: a landing report describing
how the arrow turns and stretches per step <C k="ex">east</C> and per step
<C k="ey">north</C>. In the right pane, the blue and green vectors perched on the
arrowhead show precisely that — step east and the arrowhead moves along blue,
step north and it moves along green. Four numbers, filed in the same columns as
ever.

That box — the Jacobian of the gradient — is the Hessian of the landscape:

$$
H \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}\\[10pt]
\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

second derivatives because the gradient was already made of first ones. One gift
comes free and is visible in the meter: the two off-diagonal entries are always
equal. For any twice-continuously-differentiable landscape the mixed partials
agree — east-then-north measures the same tilt as north-then-east — so the
Hessian is symmetric, always. Hold that; it pays for the geometry of the next
section.

## The Shape Left Behind

The landscape has three special points — the bottom of the cyan basin, the top of
the red hill, and between them a mountain pass. At each one the
<C k="grad">arrow</C> dies: $\nabla f = 0$. There the linear story has nothing to
say — "to first order, the ground is flat" is true at all three points and
distinguishes none of them, though a hiker standing at a summit and a hiker
standing at a pass are in very different situations. When the first-order term
goes silent, zoom again and listen to the second:

$$
f(c + h) \;\approx\; f(c) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, h^{\!\top} H\, h ,
$$

the quadratic stand-in, with the Hessian in the seat the slope used to occupy.
(Boundary check: on a genuinely flat plane $H$ is the zero matrix, the correction
vanishes, and flat means flat.) The figure below zooms into each special point in
turn. I draw the contour lines from the true landscape, never from the quadratic —
whatever shape emerges under magnification is $f$'s own doing, not an installation.

At the pit and the peak: nested ellipses, ever more perfect as you push the
magnification. At the pass: two contour lines *crossing* at the point itself,
cutting the neighborhood into four quadrants — <C k="hi">up</C>, <C k="lo">down</C>,
up, down, in alternation. Rings and crossings are the only two smooth options,
and which one you get is decided entirely by the four numbers in $H$.

The violet axes overlaid on the figure decode them. A symmetric matrix — and the
previous section guaranteed symmetry — always owns two perpendicular
directions it does not shear, its *eigenvectors*: step exactly along one and the
arrow-change points exactly back along it, $Hv = \lambda v$. Along each axis the
landscape is a one-dimensional parabola, and the number $\lambda$ is its
curvature, signed. Read the figure's meters as you switch points: the pit files
$(+2.0, +0.9)$ — up both ways, but not equally, which is why the rings are
ellipses and why their long axis lies along the dashed, gentler direction. The
peak files $(-2.0, -0.9)$. The pass files one of each — up along the solid
axis, down along the dashed one — and contours can only cross where the
quadratic changes sign quadrant by quadrant.

That reading *is* the second-derivative test, usually taught as an incantation:
both curvatures positive, a minimum; both negative, a maximum; mixed, a saddle.
And the incantation's strange gatekeeper, "check the sign of
$f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2$," is an old acquaintance — that expression is
$\det H = \lambda_1 \lambda_2$, the area receipt of the gradient map, negative
exactly when the curvatures disagree. The determinant caught folds in the first
half; here it catches saddles, and it is doing the same job both times.

One warning the axes make visible: the tilt of the ellipses on screen comes from
the off-diagonal entries. A rotated valley *needs* its mixed partials — zero them
by fiat and you have silently decreed that every bowl in the world sits square to
your coordinate axes. The mixed term is not bookkeeping; it is the freedom to
tilt.

## Stepping Downhill

Everything so far has been anatomy. Both boxes go to work in the same arena — the
problem of walking to the bottom of a valley you cannot see, guided only by local
measurements. This is the daily bread of optimization: the landscape is a loss,
the walker is an algorithm, and the question is what to measure at each step.

The obvious walker measures the <C k="grad">arrow</C> and steps against it —
gradient descent. The valley below is a quadratic bowl, tilted 25° and much
steeper across than along; both walkers start at the amber dot and step on a
fixed clock, pink measuring only the arrow, violet measuring the arrow *and the
Hessian* and taking the step $-H^{-1}\nabla f$ instead. Two knobs this once —
valley narrowness and step boldness — and the regimes where each walker breaks
are reachable on purpose:

<NewtonRace />

The pink walker zig-zags. It must: across the narrow valley the arrow is huge, so
a fixed step overshoots side to side, while along the valley the arrow is tiny,
so progress toward the bottom crawls. Narrow the valley and the zig-zag worsens;
push boldness past the tipping point and pink flies out of the map entirely —
each overshoot larger than the last. The arrow alone cannot distinguish "steep
because far from the bottom" from "steep because the valley is narrow."

The violet walker knows the difference, because $H$ *is* the record of how the
arrow changes — multiplying by $H^{-1}$ un-stretches the valley, turning the
tilted narrow bowl into a round one where straight downhill genuinely points at
the bottom. At full boldness it lands there in one step, for any narrowness, at
any tilt: on a true quadratic, $-H^{-1}\nabla f$ is not a good direction but the
exact displacement to the minimum. That one-step landing is Newton's method, and
its indifference to the narrowness slider is the practical meaning of everything
the eigen-axes section built.

Honesty about the arena: real landscapes are quadratic only near the bottom, so
real Newton methods walk cautiously until the quadratic stand-in becomes
trustworthy, then pounce. And nothing on this page integrates an equation — every
map, gradient, and Hessian here is closed-form; the only thing that steps is the
race itself, one walker-move per fixed tick of a clock, so your frame rate never
changes who wins.

<Waypoint>
You now hold both boxes. The Jacobian: where the two arrows land — the lattice at
the bottom of the zoom, its determinant pricing area, flips, and invertibility.
The Hessian: the same report filed on the gradient — the curvature box whose
eigen-axes name the bowl's directions, whose signs call pit, peak, or pass, and
whose inverse is the lens that makes crooked valleys round.
</Waypoint>

## The Flow Map

One confession was left standing at the top of the article: where the stirring
came from. It is not an arbitrary warp. The hero's swirl is a *flow map* — take
the vortex from the first lesson's fluid, let every point of the plane ride it
for $t$ seconds, and call the start-to-finish assignment $F_t$. Below, the hero
returns with the loupe intact and its meters finally unlocked, and the flow time
now actually flows:

<WarpLoupe mode="return" />

The grid winds tighter and tighter; the landing report at your probe point churns;
the parallelograms tilt and shear without rest. Through all of it, the violet
meter does not move: area ×1.000, at every point, at every instant. That frozen
meter is a sentence from the first lesson translated into this article's language
— *incompressible* means the flow map of the fluid satisfies $\det J = 1$
everywhere, forever. Watching dye swirl without ever bunching up and watching
this determinant sit at one are the same observation made in two vocabularies.

Every simulation on this site drags these boxes along invisibly. The solver's
advection step asks where a parcel came from — a question about undoing a flow
map, legal only while $\det J \neq 0$. The pressure projection is the machine
that polices $\det J = 1$. And every training loop that has ever fit a model
walked a loss landscape as one of the two walkers above.

## Further Reading

Grant Sanderson's *Essence of Linear Algebra* (3Blue1Brown) is the finest
existing treatment of matrices as things that move a plane — determinant as area
factor, columns as landed basis vectors — and its chapter thirteen was the first
place many people ever *saw* a matrix. This article's first half is, knowingly, a
calculus-flavored companion to it: his matrices act globally; the Jacobian is the
same picture rationed out one zoom at a time.

Michael Spivak's *Calculus on Manifolds* delivers, in its first forty pages, the
theorem this article drew: the derivative of a map is a linear map, defined by
the quality of its impersonation, with the partial-derivative matrix demoted to a
representation of it. The book is famously terse — every sentence has been
sanded — but read after the figures above, its austerity turns hospitable.

Boyd and Vandenberghe's *Convex Optimization* (free from Stanford's site) is
where the second half grows teeth. Chapter 9's analysis of gradient descent
zig-zag and Newton's affine invariance — the formal name for "indifferent to the
narrowness slider" — is exactly the race figure, run with proofs, and their
condition-number bounds explain quantitatively what the pink walker suffers.

## Final Words

The two boxes at the top of this article spent years, for me and for most people
who use them, as filing cabinets — grids of partials, memorized, differentiated
by which exam they appeared on. The whole distance between that and understanding
turned out to be one instrument. Zoom into a curve: a line, one number. Zoom into
a map of the plane: a lattice, four numbers, filed as two landings. Zoom into a
landscape where its arrow has died: a quadric, and the four numbers are curvature
now, because the map you zoomed was the gradient itself. The Hessian was never a
second idea. It is the Jacobian, pointed inward.

What stays with you is the lattice. Smoothness is a strong word — it promises
that under enough magnification *every* map alive, however violently it stirs,
must flatten into the same parallelogram monotony — and every use of these
matrices anywhere is a way of cashing that promise: an integral repriced patch by
patch, a fold located by a vanishing area, a saddle called by disagreeing
curvatures, a crooked valley made round. The mathematics of change runs on the
fact that up close, change runs out of imagination.
