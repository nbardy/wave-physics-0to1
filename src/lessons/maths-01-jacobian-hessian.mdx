# Every Map Lies: the Jacobian and the Hessian

On the wall map of every classroom, Greenland and Africa are roughly the same
size. Africa is fourteen times larger. The map below is that map — Mercator's,
the one the wall and half the internet still use — and its Greenland comes
loose. Drag it south:

<MapLies mode="plant" />

By the equator the giant has shrunk to a modest island, and the violet receipt
riding it has fallen from fifteen to one. Nothing about Greenland changed on
the way down — the copy you dragged is moved by a rigid rotation of the globe
itself, the same everywhere-honest motion a ship would make. What changed is
how much the *map* inflates whatever stands at that latitude. The receipt is
measuring the map, not the island.

This is not a defect of one lazy mapmaker. A sphere and a flat sheet disagree
about distances — peel an orange and try to flatten the peel without tearing
or stretching it — and Gauss proved in 1827 that no cleverness ever closes the
gap: flattening the earth *must* distort it. He called it the Theorema
Egregium, "the remarkable theorem." So the honest question about any flat map
is never *whether* it lies, but *which lie it chose, and by how much, where* —
and it turns out cartographers answer that question with an instrument they
print right on the map. That instrument, and the box of four numbers behind
it, is this article's subject. Its sibling — the same box, aimed at mountains
instead of oceans — is the second half.

Every quantity keeps one color throughout: the <C k="stamp">probe we watch</C>
is always amber, the <C k="ex">east arrow</C> blue, the
<C k="ey">north arrow</C> green, every <C k="area">measured meter</C> —
areas, curvatures — violet, and on the terrain of the second half the
<C k="grad">uphill arrows</C> are pink. None of the names need memorizing;
the colors do the remembering.

## The Circle That Confesses

To measure a lie you need a witness of known shape. Ours is a circle drawn on
the ground itself — on the actual globe, a ring of points all the same true
distance from a center, the shape a surveyor would stake out with a rope. The
map then prints that circle however it must. Below, the left pane is the
world under the naive grid — longitude straight across, latitude straight up,
the projection every spreadsheet of coordinates secretly assumes — with the
circle drawn on it; the right pane shows the printed shape magnified to a
standard size, next to a dashed ring marking what the ground knows it to be.
The slider sets how much ground the circle spans:

<TissotLoupe mode="blob" />

At continent scale the printed shape is a lopsided egg, visibly fatter at
its northern edge than its southern, because the top of the circle sits at a
more stretched latitude than the bottom. No single description covers it.
Now pull the slider toward *county* and watch the right pane: the egg
settles into a clean ellipse — wide, but a true ellipse, symmetric about its
axes — and further shrinking changes nothing but the label. Drag the circle
anywhere on the map and it happens there too: a different ellipse at every
latitude, but always an ellipse.

That emergence is the whole idea of differentiability, met in the wild.
Up close, every smooth map's lie simplifies into the one shape a linear
distortion makes of a circle. The lopsided egg needed a paragraph; the
ellipse needs four numbers. The next section reads them off.

## The Landing Report

One fact about the globe first, because everything rests on it — including
why the egg was fat side north: a degree of longitude is not a fixed
distance. At the equator it matches a degree of latitude; at 60° north it is
half as long; approaching the pole it shrinks to nothing. The naive grid
prints every degree the same width anyway, so the farther north, the more
stretched the print. The honest unit steps at any point are therefore steps
of *ground* — one stride <C k="ex">due east</C>, one stride
<C k="ey">due north</C>, equal true lengths. The figure below plants both
strides at the probe and shows where the map prints them, next to dashed
ghosts marking a print with no opinion. The selector adds Mercator and a
third map we'll fight with later:

<TissotLoupe mode="matrix" />

The box in the corner is the whole local story, filed in columns: the first
column is where the <C k="ex">east stride</C> landed, the second where the
<C k="ey">north stride</C> landed. The ellipse you met in the last section is
nothing separate — it is where the *whole family* of unit strides lands, and
the two columns pin it down. This box is the Jacobian of the map at that
point. Written in the calculus the reader already owns, with $x$ and $y$ the
printed coordinates and the strides taken along the ground,

$$
J \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial e} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial n}\\[10pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial e} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial n}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

columns as landings, and the map's local behavior is the linear impersonation

$$
F(p + h) \;\approx\; F(p) + J\,h
$$

for small ground displacements $h$ — the ellipse being this formula applied to
the circle of unit $h$'s. Boundary check, visible in the figure: put the probe
on the equator under the naive grid. Both strides land exactly on their
ghosts, $J$ files ones and zeros, and the circle prints as a circle — at that
one happy spot, the map isn't lying at all.

The instrument has a name and a pedigree. Nicolas Auguste Tissot, a French
cartographer, published exactly this ellipse-field in 1859, and serious
atlases have printed *Tissot's indicatrix* over their projections ever since.
You have been looking at drawings of the Jacobian since your first atlas;
nobody said so.

## The Area Receipt

The hero's receipt is hiding in the box. The landed strides span a
parallelogram — the printed copy of a small square of ground — and its area is
the cross-combination of the columns,

$$
\det J \;=\;
\frac{\partial x}{\partial e}\frac{\partial y}{\partial n}
-
\frac{\partial x}{\partial n}\frac{\partial y}{\partial e},
$$

the *determinant*: the map's local area magnification. On Mercator it works
out to $\sec^2(\mathrm{lat})$ — the two strides each stretch by $\sec$, and
area pays twice. At Greenland's central latitude, 72°, that is a factor of
ten and a half — yet the hero's receipt read fifteen, and the gap is the
point: $\det J$ is a rate at a *point*, and a whole coastline pays the rate
prevailing at each of its pieces, Greenland's arctic reaches dearest of all.
Below, the one-frame version: three circles of identical
ground size, three violet receipts. Drag the bright one along a meridian and
watch its receipt breathe while its ground size never changes:

<TissotTrio />

The receipt also settles an old classroom debt. Every change of variables in
an integral drags in a mysterious factor — the $r$ in $r\,dr\,d\theta$ — and
that factor is this same determinant: polar coordinates are a map, the map
inflates area by $r$, and the integral must pay each patch by its printed
area,

$$
\iint g \,dx\,dy \;=\; \iint g \;\lvert\det J\rvert \,du\,dv .
$$

The absolute value is doing quiet work in that formula, and a projection
can't show why, because projections never fold. So here is one figure on
loan from the abstract plane — a map that folds it like a sheet of paper
pushed from both ends, with the probe square caught in the crease.

The meter crosses. On the way it touches zero exactly when the square is
crushed to a needle — two dimensions of ink surviving as one — and what
unfolds on the far side is the square *mirrored*, its corner dot on the wrong
side. A negative determinant is the picture of a patch turned over, and
$\det J = 0$ is the moment a map destroys information: two ground points
printed on one spot, which no formula can afterwards tell apart. That is why
"invertible where $\det J \neq 0$" is a theorem and not a slogan — and why
integration keeps the absolute value: ink counts the same on either face.

## Choose Your Lie

Look back at Mercator's Jacobian: $\sec\phi$ down both diagonal slots, zeros
off it. Both strides stretched *equally*, no shear — which is why every
Tissot ellipse on Mercator is a perfect circle, just inflated. Mercator lies
enormously about size and not at all about shape. Could a map do the reverse?
Below, the same ground circle on two maps at once. The right one is the
*sinusoidal* projection, built by forcing east-west distances true at every
latitude:

<ProjectionDuel />

Read the meters. Mercator: round to a hair, area inflated. Sinusoidal: area
×1.00 — exactly, everywhere, its determinant is pinned to one by
construction — but the circle lands sheared several times longer than wide,
and the shear worsens as you drag away from the central meridian. So which should a map keep, shape or
size? Both, is the natural answer, and Gauss's theorem is precisely the
sentence that forbids it: keeping both everywhere is flattening the orange
peel. Every projection ever printed is a settlement. Mercator settled for
shape because sailors steer by angles — a compass course crosses its map as
a straight line, which is worth any amount of Greenland to a navigator.
Equal-area maps settle for size because censuses and rainforests are counted
in area. The Jacobian is the ledger where each map's settlement is written
down, point by point: its ellipse-shape says what happened to angles, its
determinant what happened to area.

<Waypoint>
You can now read a projection the way a cartographer does: at any point, two
ground strides and their landings — four numbers; the ellipse those landings
sweep is Tissot's indicatrix; the determinant is the area receipt, zero at a
fold, negative through a mirror, and the reason $r$ haunts every polar
integral. And no map escapes the choice the ledger records.
</Waypoint>

## The Other Map in the Atlas

Turn the atlas page from the oceans to the mountains. A terrain sheet — this
one is an invented stretch of backcountry, drawn from a formula so that every
claim about it can be checked exactly — shows height by tint and by contour
lines. On it stands a field of <C k="grad">arrows</C>: at each point, the
direction a ball would refuse to roll — straight uphill, crossing each
contour at a right angle, longer where the contours crowd. Drag the ring; the
right pane isolates your point's arrow, together with the same arrow one
step east and one step north:

<TerrainField />

Watch the right pane while you drag. The dashed ghosts are the arrow's own
near future: <C k="ex">blue</C> joins its head to where the head sits one
stride east, <C k="ey">green</C> to one stride north. Point in, arrow out is
a map from the plane to the plane — precisely the kind of object the first
half taught us to read — and so it has a landing report of its own: how much
the arrow changes per stride, filed in the same columns as ever. That report
has its own name: the Hessian of the terrain,

$$
H \;=\;
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\,\partial y}\\[10pt]
\dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}
\end{pmatrix},
$$

second derivatives because the arrows were already made of first ones. The
meter shows one gift the projection half never gave us: the two off-diagonal
entries agree, always — stepping east then measuring north-tilt equals
stepping north and measuring east-tilt, for any twice-smooth terrain. The
Hessian is symmetric. Hold that; it buys the geometry of the next section.

## Summit, Basin, Pass

The sheet has three marked spots where the arrow dies entirely: the bottom of
a basin, the top of a summit, and — between the high ground and the low — a
mountain pass. Hikers cross passes; topo maps have a reading for them.

  a="Small closed rings, like a summit's"
  b="Two lines crossing at the pass itself, four open quadrants"
>
  <CriticalZoom />
</Predict>

At the basin and the summit: nested ellipses, cleaner the closer you look. At
the pass: two contours *crossing* at the point, cutting the neighborhood into
four alternating quadrants — <C k="hi">up</C>, <C k="lo">down</C>, up, down.
The zoom is honest: those contours are drawn from the terrain itself, never
from any quadratic stand-in, and yet a quadratic is exactly what emerges,
because with the arrow dead the terrain's local truth is the next term of the
impersonation:

$$
f(c + h) \;\approx\; f(c) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, h^{\!\top} H\, h .
$$

(Boundary check: on flat ground $H$ is the zero box and the formula says
flat.) The violet axes decode the shape. A symmetric box always owns two
perpendicular directions it does not shear — step along one and the arrow
change points straight back along it, $Hv = \lambda v$ — and along each the
terrain is a plain one-dimensional parabola with signed curvature $\lambda$.
The basin files two positive curvatures, unequal, which is why its rings are
ellipses with a long axis down the gentler direction. The summit files two
negatives. The pass files one of each — the ground genuinely curves *up*
along one axis and *down* along the other, which no single number could ever
say, and contours can only cross where that sign change cuts the quadrants.

Reading the signs is the second-derivative test the reader memorized once as
an incantation — and the incantation's gatekeeper,
$f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2$, is an old acquaintance: it is $\det H$, the area
receipt of the *arrow map*, $\lambda_1\lambda_2$. Negative exactly when the
curvatures disagree. The determinant caught folds on the projection; it
catches passes on the terrain; it is the same bookkeeper both times.

## The Automatic Surveyor

If the signs of two curvatures can call a pass, nobody needs to point at the
special spots by hand — the sheet can be surveyed unattended. The recipe is
the two boxes working in sequence: glow wherever the
<C k="grad">arrow</C> is short (a summit candidate must first be nearly
level), then color the glow by the Hessian's two signs —
<C k="hi">domes</C> where both curve down, <C k="lo">bowls</C> where both
curve up, <C k="area">saddle-shaped ground</C> where they disagree. Slide
from *terrain* to *survey*:

<CurvatureMap />

The summit glows inside red, the basin inside cyan, and violet turns up
*two* passes — the one the zoom visited and a twin on the far shoulder the
prose never mentioned, found by arithmetic, not by eyes. Fainter glows trace
the nearly-level shelves between them. This is not a
classroom exercise dressed as software: elevation models are mined for
peaks, sinks, and cols by exactly this eigenvalue-sign sweep, and computer
vision's blob detectors run the same $\det H$ test on images — SIFT, the
workhorse feature-finder, rejects a candidate point when the Hessian's
determinant test smells an edge instead of a blob. The incantation from the
calculus final runs inside mapping software and camera pipelines, all day,
at scale.

## Downhill in Fog

One more use of the second box, and it is the one that runs the modern world.
Put a walker on the terrain in fog: it cannot see the basin, only measure the
ground under its feet. Measuring the <C k="grad">arrow</C> and stepping
against it is gradient descent — the algorithm that trains every neural
network — and on kind terrain it works. The valley below is unkind: tilted,
and far steeper across than along. The pink walker steps against the arrow.
The violet walker also measures the *Hessian* and steps by $-H^{-1}\nabla f$
instead. Two knobs this once — valley narrowness and step boldness — and the
regimes where each walker breaks are reachable on purpose:

<NewtonRace />

The pink walker zig-zags; it must. Across the narrow valley the arrow is
huge, so a fixed step overshoots wall to wall, while along the valley the
arrow is tiny and progress crawls; the arrow alone cannot distinguish "steep
because far from the bottom" from "steep because the valley is narrow." Push
boldness past the edge and pink leaves the map, each overshoot larger than
the last. The violet walker is immune, because $H$ is the record of how the
arrow changes: multiplying by $H^{-1}$ un-stretches the valley into a round
bowl where downhill points at the bottom. At full boldness it lands there in
one step, at any narrowness — that is Newton's method, and its indifference
to the narrowness knob is the cash value of everything the eigen-axes
taught. Honesty about the arena: real terrain is quadratic only near the
bottom, so real Newton methods walk warily until the impersonation becomes
trustworthy, then pounce. And nothing on this page integrates an equation —
maps, gradients, and Hessians here are closed-form; the only thing that
steps is the race, one walker-move per fixed tick, so your frame rate never
changes who wins.

<Waypoint>
Both boxes are now working instruments. The Jacobian: stride-landings,
Tissot's ellipse, the determinant pricing area and calling folds. The
Hessian: the same report filed on the uphill arrows — curvatures on its
axes, passes on its determinant, and, inverted, the lens that makes crooked
valleys round.
</Waypoint>

## The Confession, Everywhere

The hero returns wearing the full instrument. Every circle below is the same
six degrees of ground; the printed sizes are the map confessing its area lie
at every latitude at once, exactly as Tissot intended. Greenland still comes
loose:

<MapLies mode="return" />

Read it like a cartographer now: circles fat at the top, honest at the
equator, every one of them round because Mercator's settlement kept shape
and sold size. Fourteen times — the true Africa-to-Greenland ratio — was
never hiding anywhere but in a determinant.

One more map deserves its reading. The fluid simulations elsewhere on this
site push a velocity field's particles around the screen; the start-to-now
assignment of each particle is a map of the plane, and it drags a Jacobian
along every path. Incompressibility — the property the solver spends most of
its effort enforcing — is the sentence $\det J = 1$, forever, at every
particle: a fluid is a map that never writes a false area receipt. The
projection page and the physics engine are keeping the same books.

## Further Reading

John P. Snyder's *Map Projections: A Working Manual* (USGS Professional
Paper 1395, free online) is the projection bible — every chart in it comes
with exactly the partial derivatives this article called landing reports,
and Snyder's own life is the subject's best anecdote: a chemist who took up
projections as a hobby and ended up writing NASA's. The Tissot machinery
appears there as "distortion analysis," worked to numbers.

Grant Sanderson's *Essence of Linear Algebra* (3Blue1Brown) is the finest
treatment anywhere of matrices as machines that move a plane — columns as
landed basis vectors, determinant as area factor. This article's ellipses
are his pictures made local; if the linear half felt fast, his chapters are
the unhurried version.

Boyd and Vandenberghe's *Convex Optimization* (free from Stanford) gives the
fog-walking finale its rigor: chapter 9 proves how badly gradient descent
zig-zags as conditioning worsens and why Newton's step is indifferent to it
— the formal name is affine invariance — with bounds where this article has
a race.

## Final Words

Tissot's ellipses were on the schoolroom wall the whole time; so was the
fourteen-fold lie they measure. What was missing was only the reading: that
the little box of partial derivatives everyone computes and nobody sees *is*
the ellipse, that its determinant is the receipt, and that pointing the same
box at a terrain's own arrows turns it into the curvature that calls every
summit and pass on Earth. Two objects from a calculus course, and between
them they run the atlas, the census, the feature-detector, and the training
loop.

None of the maps got fixed. The theorem stands; the wall map lies tonight
exactly as it did when you were nine. What changed hands is custody of the
lie — it now has a size you can read and a receipt it must sign at every
point of the sheet. A map that cannot help lying, and will tell you exactly
how much if you know its four-number language, is a stranger and finer thing
than an honest one could ever have been.
