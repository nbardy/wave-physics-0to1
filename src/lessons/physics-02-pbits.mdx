# A Computer Made of Noise

{/* STAGE 3 SCAFFOLD — blocked prose; the voice pass is Stage 4.
    F1 HERO lands here, built last per PLAN (needs the §8 trainer). */}

Every computer you own is at war with noise. Billions of transistors are margined,
clocked, and error-corrected so that not one of them ever flips a bit by accident;
most of the watts your laptop burns are spent winning that war. A chip exists now
that surrenders it instead. Its bits flip at random on purpose, millions of times
a second, driven by the same thermal jitter every other chip suppresses — and it
computes *with* the flipping, on less power than a nightlight.

This lesson builds that machine's whole idea from one noisy coin — and builds,
alongside it, the instrument that can tell when such a machine is lying. Both
halves matter. Randomness is the one output your eye cannot audit, and by the end
you will have caught a fast, plausible-looking sampler producing an entirely wrong
answer that no energy readout would ever have revealed.

One amnesty up front: there is no quantum mechanics anywhere in this article.
These are classical coins.

## A bit that leans

A fair coin flipped forever computes nothing — its histogram is flat and stays
flat no matter what you wish it said. The smallest useful move is to give the
coin an opinion. The cell below flickers between its two states, <C k="sUp">up</C>
and <C k="sDn">down</C>, forty times a second. The knob is its *bias*. Drag it and
watch the histogram lean:

<BitFlicker />

The coin still never obeys — at any bias short of the ends it sometimes lands
against its preference. What the knob sets is not the outcome but the *lean*, and
the histogram measures it. This flickering, opinionated coin is called a p-bit, a
probabilistic bit, and it is the only component the machine in this lesson is
made of.

Now sweep the knob slowly from one end to the other and let it rest a moment at
each stop. Every bias you visit leaves a dot behind — its measured mean — and the
dots trace a curve:

<BitFlicker trace />

That S-shape is yours; the figure only recorded it. Written down, the rule you
just drew is

$$P(s = +1) = \sigma(2h), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$$

where $s$ is the coin's state, $h$ is the bias knob, and $\sigma$ is the S-curve
itself. Check it at the edges: push $h$ hard positive and $\sigma(2h)$ saturates
at 1 — the coin pins <C k="sUp">up</C> and the flicker dies. At $h = 0$ it gives
exactly ½, the fair coin we started with — uselessness recovered as a special
case.

## Reading the display

Before anything else happens, the display itself. A cell filled <C k="sUp">amber</C>
points up; filled <C k="sDn">blue</C>, down. A wire between two cells means their
leans are linked: a <C k="ferro">warm wire</C> wants its ends to agree, a
<C k="anti">cool wire</C> wants them to disagree, and thickness is how much. A
cell wearing the <C k="held">green ring</C> is *held* — pinned by hand, deaf to
persuasion. The thin <C k="meter">violet arc</C> around each cell is a gauge: its
probability of pointing up, given what its neighbors are doing right now.

<DisplayTeach />

Tap cells and watch the arcs. Flipping a cell moves the gauges of exactly the
cells it is wired to and no others — persuasion here is strictly local, along
wires. The held cell's own gauge still moves when you flip its neighbor, but its
state never will. Every figure from here to the end of the lesson speaks this
vocabulary and no other.

## Two coins that gossip

Here are two of those coins side by side, and a two-part target: make them
*agree* nine times out of ten, while each coin *stays fair* — its lean needle
resting near center. You have a bias knob for each. Try to hit both marks:

<PairCoupler mode="biases" />

There is a cheat, and you should take it: crank both knobs to the rail. The
agreement meter sails past the <C k="ghost">gray mark</C> — and both lean
needles slam red. You didn't make two coins agree; you made two constants equal.
The moment you back either knob toward fair, the agreement collapses toward
zero. That trade is exact — under separate knobs, agreement is the *product* of
the two leans — and it is the failure worth feeling: each knob talks to its own
coin only, so the only agreement biases can buy is the kind you get by
destroying the coins. Agreement between coins that still flicker is a fact about
the *pair*, and nothing that addresses coins one at a time can touch it.

What's missing is a wire.

  <PairCoupler mode="wire" />

With the knob toward *agree*, the outer bars — both-up, both-down — swell
together and the meter climbs to the mark, while both lean needles rest exactly
where the biases could never leave them: at fair. The wire buys agreement
without pinning anything — it shapes the pair and leaves each coin alone.
Toward *disagree*, the inner bars take over instead. The <C k="ghost">gray outlines</C>
behind the sampled bars are new: they are the *exact* answer, computed by brute
force, and this is the first time in the lesson the flicker can be checked
against truth. Keep an eye on that gray; it is about to become the whole story.

How does a wire persuade? Watch the second coin's arithmetic:

<PairCoupler mode="wire" fieldGauge />

The right coin no longer consults only its own bias. It consults a running total —
its bias plus the wire's weight times whatever its neighbor shows *right now* —
and feeds that total to the same S-curve as before:

$$P(s_i = +1) = \sigma\!\left(2\Big(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij}\, s_j\Big)\right)$$

The sum is over wired neighbors; $J_{ij}$ is the wire's weight, positive for
<C k="ferro">agree</C>, negative for <C k="anti">disagree</C>. This one line is
the entire update rule of everything in this lesson, the 270-thousand-bit chip
included. Nothing more will be added to it — only more neighbors.

## The mountain range

Four coins now, wired in a ring: three <C k="ferro">agree-wires</C> and one
<C k="anti">disagree-wire</C>. Go around the loop and count — no assignment of
ups and downs can satisfy all four wires at once. The system is *frustrated*, and
frustration keeps it interesting: instead of freezing into one happy state it
must forever compromise.

Sixteen states are possible. Give each one a column, and make the column's height
the state's probability — computed exactly, by adding up all sixteen cases. The
dot is the four-coin sampler itself, hopping columns live:

<Landscape />

The landscape is a law, not a schedule: it says where the sampler *dwells*, not
where it goes next. Drag toward *cold* and the range sharpens — the token gets
trapped in the deep columns and the shallow ones starve. Toward *hot*, the range
flattens toward sixteen equal stubs and the fair coin returns once more. The
heights follow one formula,

$$p(s) \propto e^{-\beta E(s)}, \qquad E(s) = -\sum_{(i,j)} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j - \sum_i h_i\, s_i$$

— the energy $E$ totals every wire's satisfaction, and $\beta$ is the coldness
knob you are holding. This is the Boltzmann distribution, and here is the fact
the whole field rests on: the humble local rule from the last section — each coin
consulting its neighbors and flicking its S-curve — *dwells in exactly this
global law*. One is the rule, the other is the consequence; the machine only ever
runs the rule, and the law is what it earns.

There is a second way to see the same dwelling, and it repays a minute of
staring. Lay out all sixteen states as a map — states that differ by one coin's
flip joined by an edge — and let the walker walk:

<StateGraph />

The <C k="ghost">ring</C> around each node is its exact share; the
<C k="meter">fill</C> is how often the walker has actually been there. Watch the
fills catch up to the rings. That convergence has a name — stationarity — and it
is the entire promise a sampler makes: visit every room of the state space in
proportion to its law.

Promises want auditors. For four coins the truth is sixteen additions, so the
audit can be exact: gray bars for truth, violet bars for the sampler's record,
and their mismatch folded into a single number — the *total variation distance*,
half the sum of the bar-by-bar gaps. Zero means indistinguishable; one means
never right.

<MeterForge />

Watch the number fall as evidence accumulates — and note how it falls: to push it
down twice as far takes four times the samples, the stubborn square-root
arithmetic of all measurement. Now move the temperature knob. The
<C k="ghost">ghost itself reshapes</C> — the target has moved — and the meter
briefly jumps, then re-earns the floor on fresh evidence. Hold on to that
distinction: *the target moving* and *the sampler missing* are different events,
and this pane can tell them apart. Nothing else in this lesson can.

<Waypoint>
  You hold one rule — each coin flicks an S-curve fed by its wired neighbors —
  and one law, the Boltzmann distribution the rule dwells in. And you hold an
  instrument: exact truth in gray, evidence in violet, their distance as one
  number. Everything that follows is an attack on that number's floor.
</Waypoint>

## Everyone talks at once

Sixteen coins were a toy. Here are 256, wired <C k="ferro">agree</C> to their
grid neighbors — and a problem that was invisible until now. *One at a time* is
how we've updated coins so far, each consulting the freshest state of its
neighbors. It is correct, and it is serial: a quarter of a million cells means a
quarter of a million turns per sweep. Every coin's update is just its own little
S-curve — surely we can run them all at once?

The grid below offers both schedules. In its center, four cells are fenced by
eight <C k="held">held</C> neighbors — held in a deliberate split, up on the
west, down on the east, so a domain wall runs straight through the fence and the
patch inside stays honestly undecided. Because the fence never moves, the patch's
exact distribution is computable, and the meter audits it live:

<GridSchedules />

*One at a time* idles at the floor. Now press *all at once*. The lattice starts
to shimmer with a strange crosshatch — and the meter climbs an order of
magnitude and pins there. It does not descend with more evidence. This is not
noise; it is a different, wrong answer, delivered confidently at enormous speed.

The walker's map catches the same crime from a second angle:

<StateGraph schedules />

Under *all at once*, the walker starts jumping along chords that are not edges —
several coins flipping in one move, transitions a one-at-a-time walker can never
make, each flashing red as it happens. The state space is being traversed by a
different process entirely.

Why. When every coin updates simultaneously, each consults its neighbors'
*old* values — two wired coins persuade each other's ghosts. Two coins that
want to agree, both currently down: each sees a down neighbor an instant before
both flip up; next tick each sees up, and both may crash down again. The lattice
crosshatches with exactly this oscillation. The rule ran; what it dwelled in was
no longer the law.

Here is the part that should worry you. The wrongness never announces itself.
The all-at-once lattice still finds low-energy states — happily aligned domains
everywhere you look — and its flicker is as random-looking as the honest one's.
Watch the two numbers under the meter: evidence keeps accumulating by the
thousands, and the distance stops falling anyway. A correct sampler's error
starves with evidence; a broken one's error is a fact about the process, and no
amount of watching dilutes it. Optimization success is not sampling
correctness, and nothing on this screen except the violet number can tell the
difference.

## The two-coloring rescue

The failure was never parallelism. It was *wired neighbors updating together*.
So: color the grid like a checkerboard. Every wire joins a red cell to a black
cell — no red touches red, no black touches black. Freeze black and update all
128 red cells at once: each consults only frozen black neighbors, so nothing it
reads is stale, so the 128 simultaneous updates are exactly 128 legal
one-at-a-time updates that happen to share a clock tick. Then freeze red, flash
black. Two half-sweeps, and every coin has taken a fully honest turn.

  a="near the one-at-a-time floor"
  b="somewhere between the floor and all-at-once"
>
  <GridSchedules chromatic />
</Predict>

The floor — not a compromise between speed and truth but both at once. The only
thing the coloring costs is generality: it works because the grid happens to
admit a two-coloring, which is to say the graph is *bipartite*. That small
observation is worth more than it looks, because it is the entire design secret
of the actual chip.

Extropic's Z1 is 269,568 p-bits, wired in a fixed sparse graph — sixteen
neighbors each — that was *designed bipartite*. The chip's native act is the
figure you are looking at: program the biases and the wire weights, then sweep
red, sweep black, at millions of sweeps per second, on under one watt, with
thermal noise itself playing the dice. The schedule you just rescued is not a
simulation trick; it is the machine. A p-bit chip is a Gibbs machine — that is
the name this one-at-a-time-in-parallel sampling carries — and everything it
will ever compute must arrive dressed as biases and wire weights on that fixed
two-colored graph.
