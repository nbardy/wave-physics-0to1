# A Computer Made of Noise

Every computer you own is at war with noise. Billions of transistors are margined,
clocked, and error-corrected so that not one of them ever flips a bit by accident;
most of the watts your laptop burns are spent winning that war. A chip exists now
that surrenders it instead. Its bits flip at random on purpose, millions of times
a second, driven by the same thermal jitter every other chip suppresses — and the
flipping is not overhead it tolerates. The flipping is the computation.

Randomness is the one output the eye cannot audit. Two streams of flicker look
identical when one of them is right and the other is confidently, permanently
wrong — so alongside the machine, this lesson builds the instrument that can
catch it lying. A fast, plausible-looking sampler is going to produce an
entirely wrong answer in these pages while every energy readout applauds it,
and the instrument will be the only witness.

Here is the surrender, running. Forty small lattices, sixteen cells each, every
cell flipping many times a second — and pictures keep condensing out of the
static: a bar, a box, a cross, dissolving and re-forming, never twice in the
same place, never on a schedule.

<MosaicHero />

Nothing on this wall is drawn. Every cell is a coin toss; the weights loading
the dice were trained, and they ship with this page unexplained — on credit.
The wall returns at the end, dreaming a picture you painted, on weights
trained in front of you.

One amnesty up front: there is no quantum mechanics anywhere in this article.
These are classical coins.

## A bit that leans

A fair coin flipped forever computes nothing — its histogram is flat and stays
flat no matter what you wish it said. The smallest useful move is to give the
coin an opinion. The cell below flickers between its two states, <C k="sUp">up</C>
and <C k="sDn">down</C>, forty times a second. The knob is its *bias*. Drag it and
watch the histogram lean:

<BitFlicker />

The cell still never obeys — at any bias short of the ends it sometimes lands
against its preference. What the knob sets is not the outcome but the *lean*,
and the histogram measures it. This flickering, opinionated cell is a p-bit — a
probabilistic bit — and it is the only component the machine in this lesson is
made of. The coin stays with us as the metaphor; p-bit is the working word from
here on. In silicon a p-bit is a comparator listening to its own thermal noise —
a real circuit, drawn in full when the machine itself arrives.

Now sweep the knob slowly from one end to the other and let it rest a moment at
each stop. Every bias you visit leaves a dot behind — its measured mean — and the
dots trace a curve:

<BitFlicker trace />

That S-shape is yours; the figure only recorded it. Written down, the rule you
just drew is

$$P(s = +1) = \sigma(2h), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}$$

where $s$ is the p-bit's state, $h$ is the bias knob, and $\sigma$ is the S-curve
itself. Check it at the edges: push $h$ hard positive and $\sigma(2h)$ saturates
at 1 — the p-bit pins <C k="sUp">up</C> and the flicker dies. At $h = 0$ it gives
exactly ½, the fair coin we started with — uselessness recovered as a special
case.

## Reading the display

Before anything else happens, the display itself. A cell filled <C k="sUp">amber</C>
points up; filled <C k="sDn">blue</C>, down. A wire between two cells means their
leans are linked: a <C k="ferro">warm wire</C> wants its ends to agree, a
<C k="anti">cool wire</C> wants them to disagree, and thickness is how much. A
cell wearing the <C k="held">green ring</C> is *held* — pinned by hand, deaf to
persuasion. The thin <C k="meter">violet arc</C> around each cell is a gauge: its
probability of pointing up, given what its neighbors are doing right now.

<DisplayTeach />

Tap cells and watch the arcs. Flipping a cell moves the gauges of exactly the
cells it is wired to and no others — persuasion here is strictly local, along
wires. The held cell's own gauge still moves when you flip its neighbor, but its
state never will. Every figure from here to the end of the lesson speaks this
vocabulary and no other.

None of this display is a cartoon of the hardware; it is a diagram of it. The
wire is a programmable conductance on the chip — the neighbor's state feeds a
weighted current in, and that current sum is the p-bit's whole view of the
world. The arc gauge is the transfer curve of a noisy comparator: how often a
threshold crosses when thermal noise rides on its input. The full circuit
arrives with the chip.

One more piece of furniture. The small rail in each figure's corner — TARGET ·
ENERGY · SAMPLER · SUBSTRATE — names a pipeline this lesson refuses to blur:
the distribution you want, the wire-and-bias landscape that encodes it, the
schedule that samples it, and the physical thing that runs the schedule. The
lit slot is where the figure you're looking at lives.

## Two p-bits that gossip

Here are two p-bits side by side, and a two-part target: make them *agree* nine
times out of ten, while each one *stays fair* — its lean needle resting near
center. You have a bias knob for each. Try to hit both marks:

<PairCoupler mode="biases" />

There is a cheat, and you should take it: crank both knobs to the rail. The
agreement gauge sails past the <C k="ghost">gray mark</C> — and both lean
needles slam red, while the second gauge underneath, the *connected* part of
the agreement, never leaves zero. You didn't make two p-bits agree; you made
two constants equal, and the lower gauge knows the difference. The moment you
back either knob toward fair, the agreement collapses toward zero. That trade
is exact — under separate knobs, agreement is the *product* of the two
leans: each knob talks to its own p-bit only, so
the only agreement biases can buy is the kind you get by destroying the coins.
Agreement between p-bits that still flicker is a fact about the *pair*, and
nothing that addresses them one at a time can touch it.

What's missing is a wire.

With the knob toward *agree*, the outer bars — both-up, both-down — swell
together and the agreement gauge climbs to the mark, while both lean needles
rest exactly where the biases could never leave them: at fair. This time the
connected gauge climbs right alongside the agreement — the wire buys the real
thing, shaping the pair while leaving each p-bit alone. Toward *disagree*, the
inner bars take over instead. The <C k="ghost">gray outlines</C> behind the
sampled bars are new: they are the *exact* answer, computed by brute force, and
this is the first time in the lesson the flicker can be checked against truth.
Keep an eye on that gray; it is about to become the whole story.

A wire persuades with arithmetic. Watch the second p-bit's:

<PairCoupler mode="wire" fieldGauge />

The right p-bit no longer consults only its own bias. It consults a running
total — its bias plus the wire's weight times whatever its neighbor shows
*right now* — and feeds that total to the same S-curve as before:

$$P(s_i = +1) = \sigma\!\left(2\Big(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij}\, s_j\Big)\right)$$

The sum is over wired neighbors; $J_{ij}$ is the wire's weight, positive for
<C k="ferro">agree</C>, negative for <C k="anti">disagree</C>. This one line is
the entire update rule of everything in this lesson, the quarter-million-bit
chip included. Nothing more will be added to it — only more neighbors. One number is
quietly set to 1 in that formula: the temperature has been held fixed so far,
and the dial for it arrives in the next section.

The connected gauge you just watched also has one line. Raw agreement is the
average product $\langle s_1 s_2 \rangle$; the connected part subtracts what
the two leans could fake on their own:

$$\mathrm{Cov}(s_1, s_2) = \langle s_1 s_2 \rangle - \langle s_1 \rangle\langle s_2 \rangle$$

Check it against the cheat: for independent p-bits the average of a product is
the product of the averages, so the two terms cancel and $\mathrm{Cov} = 0$ at
*any* pair of biases — which is why the lower gauge sat dead while you
pinned both coins, and why it moves the instant a wire exists. The connected
part is the wire's fingerprint, and no bias can forge it.

## The state atlas

Four p-bits now, wired in a ring: three <C k="ferro">agree-wires</C> and one
<C k="anti">disagree-wire</C>. Go around the loop and count — no assignment of
ups and downs can satisfy all four wires at once. The system is *frustrated*,
and frustration keeps it interesting: instead of freezing into one happy state
it must forever compromise.

Sixteen states are possible, and sixteen is small enough to look at all of
them: one row of sixteen columns, one column per state — the little four-dot glyph under each column is the state itself —
redrawn page after page, each page answering a different question about the
same sixteen. An atlas of the whole state space. The first page asks each state
what it costs. Tap a column and the loop at left totals that state's wires,
term by term:

<StateAtlas mode="energy" />

Every satisfied wire pulls the total down by its weight; every violated wire —
drawn dashed — pushes it up. The total is the state's *energy*, and the tally
you just watched is the whole definition:

$$E(s) = -\sum_{(i,j)} J_{ij}\, s_i s_j \;-\; \sum_i h_i\, s_i$$

Check it on the ladder. All-up satisfies the three agree-wires but violates the
disagree-wire: three terms of $-1$, one of $+1$, energy $-2$. Flip any single
p-bit and you break two unit wires at once — the energy jumps by exactly $4$,
to the upper rung. And the floor of the ladder is $-2$, not the $-4$ a
wire-happy loop would reach: the frustration is visible as a rung that does
not exist. Sixteen states, two shelves of eight.

Energy is a cost, not yet a probability. The bridge between them is one
exponential. The knob below is $\beta$ — *inverse* temperature, so it reads as
coldness: left is hot, right is cold. Drag it both ways:

<StateAtlas mode="weight" />

Each column now carries the *raw weight*

$$w(s) = e^{-\beta E(s)}$$

— cheap states tall, expensive states short, and $\beta$ setting how brutally
the exponential punishes cost. At the hot end the ratio of tallest to shortest
column falls toward 1: every state equally welcome, sixteen fair coins. At the
cold end the ratio explodes and only the cheapest shelf survives: the system
becomes a minimizer. Between the two ends is where sampling lives.

The weights have one defect: they don't add up to anything in particular. The
next page fixes that by dividing every column by their visible sum — the
stacked bar on the right:

<StateAtlas mode="normalize" />

That stacked total is the *partition function*,

$$Z = \sum_{s} e^{-\beta E(s)}, \qquad p(s) = \frac{e^{-\beta E(s)}}{Z}$$

and after the division the sixteen columns sum to exactly 1 — the readout
confirms it at every $\beta$ you choose. This is the Boltzmann distribution:
the law that assigns every state a share of the time. The amber token is a live
four-p-bit sampler running the update rule, hopping columns as it flickers —
watch it dwell in the tall columns and starve the short ones. The law says
where the sampler lives; the rule is how it gets there. That the two agree is
the central fact of this field — for now, an observation.

A distribution is also a scale for weighing questions. Take the one the two
gossiping p-bits cared about — the correlation of the first pair. Each column votes with
its height — up-columns where they match, down-columns where they differ — and
the answer is the balance of the two sides:

<StateAtlas mode="observable" />

$$\langle A \rangle = \sum_s p(s)\, A(s)$$

for any per-state quantity $A$; here $A = s_1 s_2$. The <C k="ghost">gray</C>
bars are the exact balance and the <C k="meter">violet</C> fill is the live
sampler's running vote, converging on it. Drag toward hot and both estimates
die toward zero — with every state equally likely, matches and mismatches
cancel and the wires' influence washes out.

The last page of the atlas is the one the rest of the lesson stands on. Tap the
loop's cells to pin them — each tap cycles free, <C k="held">held</C> up, held
down. Every pin erases the columns that contradict it, and the survivors
re-normalize: they inherit the erased columns' share and sum to 1 again.

<StateAtlas mode="conditional" />

Pin three of the four and only two columns survive — the free p-bit's up and
down. The figure prints their renormalized split next to something it computes
a completely different way: the update rule, $\sigma(2\beta \cdot
\text{field})$. The two numbers agree to the last digit, at every pin pattern,
and the reason takes two lines. Divide the surviving columns' shares — every
factor of $Z$ cancels, and every term of $E$ not touching the free p-bit
cancels too:

$$\frac{p(s_i{=}{+1} \mid \text{rest})}{p(s_i{=}{-1} \mid \text{rest})} = \frac{e^{-\beta E_+}}{e^{-\beta E_-}} = e^{\,2\beta\left(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij} s_j\right)}$$

Solve for the up-share and the odds fold back into an S-curve:

$$p(s_i{=}{+1} \mid \text{rest}) = \sigma\!\left(2\beta\Big(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij}\, s_j\Big)\right)$$

Check it in the figure: pin the free p-bit's neighbors both up across unit
agree-wires and the field is 2, so at $\beta = 1$ both readouts print
$\sigma(4) = 0.982$. The local update rule and the global Boltzmann law are
not a rule and a consequence — they are one fact read at two scales. A p-bit
consulting its neighbors *is* drawing from this conditional slice of the law;
run that everywhere, forever, and the dwelling times are the law itself. This
is the object every Gibbs update in the rest of the lesson draws from.

The atlas can afford all this honesty because sixteen states fit on a page.
Promises this checkable deserve a permanent instrument. Gray bars for truth,
violet bars for the sampler's record, and their mismatch folded into a single
number — the *total variation distance*, half the sum of the bar-by-bar gaps.
Zero means indistinguishable; one means never right.

<MeterForge />

Watch the number fall as evidence accumulates — and note the gray band hugging
the top of each truth bar. That band is $\pm\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$: the *typical*
wobble of an honest $N$-sample measurement, not a promise about any single
run. The live bars spend most of their time inside it, wander out, wander back
— and the band itself tightens as $\sqrt{N}$, which is the stubborn arithmetic
of all measurement: halving the typical error costs four times the evidence.
Now move the coldness knob. The <C k="ghost">ghost itself reshapes</C> — the
target has moved — and the meter briefly jumps, then re-earns the floor on
fresh evidence. Hold on to that distinction: *the target moving* and *the
sampler missing* are different events, and this pane can tell them apart.
Nothing else in this lesson can.

<Waypoint>
  You hold one rule and one law, proven on the atlas to be the same fact — the
  rule is the Boltzmann law sliced one p-bit at a time. And you hold an
  instrument: exact truth in gray, evidence in violet, their distance as one
  number with an honest error band. Everything that follows is an attack on
  that number's floor.
</Waypoint>

## Everyone talks at once

Four p-bits were a toy. Here are 256, wired <C k="ferro">agree</C> to their
grid neighbors — and a problem that was invisible until now. *One at a time* is
how every update so far has run, each p-bit consulting the freshest state of
its neighbors. It is correct, and it is serial: a quarter of a million cells
means a quarter of a million turns per sweep. Every update is just one little
S-curve, and nothing about the arithmetic forbids running them all at once.

The grid below offers both schedules. In its center, four cells are fenced by
eight <C k="held">held</C> neighbors — held in a deliberate split, up on the
west, down on the east, so a domain wall runs straight through the fence and the
patch inside stays honestly undecided. Because the fence never moves, the patch's
exact distribution is computable, and the meter audits it live:

<GridSchedules />

*One at a time* idles at the floor. Now press *all at once*. The lattice starts
to shimmer with a strange crosshatch — and the meter climbs an order of
magnitude and pins there. It does not descend with more evidence. This is not
noise; it is a different, wrong answer, delivered confidently at enormous speed.

A second display shows how different. Shrink back to the frustrated four-loop
from the atlas, whose sixteen states fit on one map: states one flip apart are
joined by an edge, each node's <C k="ghost">ring</C> is sized by its exact
share, and the sampler is a dot walking the map, its <C k="meter">fill</C>
recording where it has been. Give the honest schedule a moment first — the
fills swell to match the rings, which is the whole promise a sampler makes,
kept in miniature. Then press *all at once*:

<StateGraph schedules />

Under *all at once*, the walker jumps along chords that are not edges — several
coins changing in a single move, where the one-at-a-time walker only ever slides
along one edge at a time. But look carefully at what the chords do and do not
prove. They say this is a *different process*. They do not say it is an unlawful
one — moving many p-bits in one tick is something a perfectly legal schedule can
also do, and one is about to rescue this grid. The crime is somewhere else.

Here is where. When every coin updates simultaneously, each consults its
neighbors' *old* values — two wired p-bits persuade each other's ghosts. Two
p-bits that want to agree, both currently down: each sees a down neighbor an
instant before both flip up; next tick each sees up, and both may crash down
again. The lattice crosshatches with exactly this oscillation. The crime needs
two things at once: a wire, and both of its ends being *written* in the same
instant. Call that a write conflict. The panel below freezes a bare copy of the
grid — no fence, all 256 cells free — at the moment one dispatch goes out. Every
cell being written this instant wears a ring, and every wire joining two ringed
cells glows red:

<WriteConflict />

*One at a time* rings a single cell, and a wire cannot join a cell to itself, so
the count reads zero by construction. *All at once* rings the whole board, and
all 480 wires turn red at once — every wire is a pair of p-bits deciding on each
other's stale ghosts. That number, not the multi-coin moves, is what separates
the two schedules — and the rescue is about to drive it to zero without
giving up the rings.

And notice what the grid itself never told you. The all-at-once lattice still
finds low-energy states — happily aligned domains everywhere you look — and its
flicker is as random-looking as the honest one's. Watch the two numbers under
the meter: evidence keeps accumulating by the thousands, and the distance stops
falling anyway. A correct sampler's error starves with evidence; a broken one's
error is a fact about the process, and no amount of watching dilutes it.

## The two-coloring rescue

The failure was never parallelism. It was *wired neighbors updating together*.
So: color the grid like a checkerboard. Every wire joins a red cell to a black
cell — no red touches red, no black touches black. Freeze black and update all
128 red cells at once: each consults only frozen black neighbors, so nothing it
reads is stale. Then freeze red, flash black. Two half-sweeps, and every coin
has taken a fully honest turn.

The floor — not a compromise between speed and truth but both at once. Run the
freeze-frame again with the half-sweep on its menu and watch the discriminator
call it:

<WriteConflict chromatic />

128 rings — half the board written in one simultaneous stroke, exactly the kind
of multi-coin move the walker's chords could never tell apart from the crime —
and zero red wires. That zero has an equation. With the black cells frozen, no
wire runs between any two red cells, so the red half's joint update splits into
independent factors:

$$p(s_R \mid s_B) \;=\; \prod_{i \in R} p(s_i \mid s_B)$$

— each factor the same S-curve that cell was already running on its own turn.
One 128-cell write *is* 128 legal one-at-a-time updates that happen to share a
clock tick. And the equation shows its own boundary: write the same product over
all 256 cells at once and it is false, because wires run inside that set — the
480 red wires of the all-at-once frame are precisely the places the
factorization breaks.

One defense of *all at once* is still standing, and it is the one you will
actually meet: if the job is optimization — find the deepest valley, never mind
the law — does the distance even matter? Stage the fight fairly. Below, one
frustrated problem: a 4×4 patch with its 24 wires assigned at random, nine
<C k="ferro">agree</C> and fifteen <C k="anti">disagree</C>, so that three of
its nine little squares can never satisfy all four of their wires — real
frustration, a real search problem. Sixteen spins means the oracle still holds
everything: the true minimum energy is *certified* by enumeration, and so is the
exact Boltzmann weight of every energy rung. Both panes race in hardware ticks —
*all at once* writes the whole lattice every tick, *red/black* needs two ticks
per sweep:

<Race />

Both bold traces fall to the certified floor. The broken sampler finds the true
minimum — not approximately, the enumerator-certified ground state — about as
fast as the honest one, even while the states it passes through run hot (the
faint ribbon riding high is its instantaneous energy, the oscillation you
already know). Now read the meters: a distance north of a half against a
few hundredths — half of
all probability in the wrong place, forever. As an optimizer, *all at once* is
defensible. As a sampler, it is wrong, and nothing on either pane except the
violet number can tell you which machine you are holding. Optimization success
is not sampling correctness.

That verdict forces a bookkeeping question the field keeps getting wrong: what
is the honest speed of a sampler? The board below scores all three schedules on
the same fenced grid, three columns each. *Writes per tick* is raw parallel
width — the hardware brag. *Samples per thousand ticks* discounts it by measured
dependence: consecutive sweeps are correlated, so they are not each a fresh draw,
and the column divides the sweep rate by an autocorrelation time read from the
audited patch (a lag-one reading — crude, and enough to rank schedules).
*Distance from exact* is the meter you already trust:

<Dashboard />

Parallelism honestly buys evidence: *red/black* posts a couple hundred
independent samples per thousand ticks against *one at a time*'s two — a
hundredfold, real, and yours to keep. And *all at once* posts the best
evidence-rate on the whole board, which is exactly why its row is struck
through. Throughput and evidence-rate are properties of whatever sampler you
have; if it samples the wrong law, they measure speed toward the wrong answer.
The first two columns mean nothing until the third one passes — a gate, not a
trade-off.

The only thing the coloring costs is generality: it works because the grid
happens to admit a two-coloring, which is to say the graph is *bipartite*. That
small observation is worth more than it looks, because it is the entire design
secret of the actual chip.

Extropic's Z1 is roughly a quarter-million p-bits, wired in a fixed sparse
graph — sixteen neighbors each — that was *designed bipartite*. The chip's
native act is the figure you just rescued: program the biases and the wire
weights, then sweep red, sweep black, millions of Gibbs iterations a second,
each one estimated to cost some three hundred *picojoules* — thermal noise
itself playing the dice, so the randomness costs nothing extra. The schedule
is not a simulation trick; it is the machine. A p-bit chip is a Gibbs
machine — that is the name this one-at-a-time-in-parallel sampling carries —
and everything it will ever compute must arrive dressed as biases and wire
weights on that fixed two-colored graph. A patch of such a fabric, running
its two-phase clock:

<ChipFabric />

One confession about the drawing: the wiring here is a stand-in built from the
chip's published numbers — degree sixteen, sparse, two-colorable — not the die's
actual routing, which is not public at the wire level. What the patch shows is
faithful anyway, because it is the same three facts the whole section turned on:
sixteen wires into the marked cell, count them; every ring red or black with no
wire joining like to like; and the clock writing one whole color class per
tick — 72 simultaneous cells, zero write conflicts, the same zero the
freeze-frame printed.

<Waypoint>
  The discriminator is now yours: parallel updates are legal exactly when no
  wire joins two spins written in the same instant — zero write conflicts is
  what makes a half-sweep exact, and the factorization over a frozen half is
  its proof. The harder verdict came with it: a sampler can reach a certified
  minimum and still be wrong by a third of all probability, with the meter as
  the only witness. What remains is the machine itself — what one p-bit is in
  silicon, and what it costs to program a landscape onto sixteen wires.
</Waypoint>

## What the chip is and is not

In the first section a single sentence claimed that a p-bit is, in silicon, a
noisy comparator. Time to open one. Below is one cell of the fabric with its
lid off. Two neighbors feed currents in through programmable conductances; a
bias DAC adds a current of its own; everything sums at a node. Then comes the
ingredient every other chip spends its power suppressing: thermal noise, riding
on the summed current. A comparator asks one question — is current plus noise
above zero? — and its answer is the cell's state. Drag the DAC and watch the
right pane: every summed current you visit gets its flip rate *measured*, one
dot per current, each dot a tally of real threshold events.

<Microscope />

The dots land on the <C k="ghost">gray curve</C>, and the gray curve is
$\sigma(2I)$ — the same S-shape your hand traced in the first figure of this
lesson. No lookup table produces it. The noise has a spread; the farther the
summed current sits from zero, the rarer the noise spike big enough to carry
the comparator across; the flip rate falls off in exactly the sigmoid's shape.
A noisy threshold does not *approximate* $\sigma$ — the sigmoid is the
description of what a noisy threshold is. So the update rule this whole lesson
runs on, $P(s_i{=}{+}1) = \sigma(2\beta(h_i + \sum_j J_{ij}s_j))$, is not
firmware loaded into the chip. It is what this circuit does by physics, with
$\beta$ set by how loud the noise runs. Push the DAC to either rail and the
current dwarfs the noise: the flicker dies, just as it did when you pinned the
first coin.

dictionary is short enough to print whole:

The local field is literally an analog current sum. That is the whole

thermally-noisy threshold does; which transistors implement it is the part
this lesson happily leaves to the datasheets.

A machine built from these cells has a small vocabulary of things you can
physically do to it: program a bias, program a conductance on a wire that
exists, hold a cell, sweep red/black. The patch below offers exactly those
four verbs. It also, quietly, offers nothing else — try to couple two cells
that share no wire.

The drag snaps back. That refusal is the machine's true shape: the wires were

"couple two arbitrary cells" any more than there is one for "branch" or

do not upload an algorithm. You set numbers on a fixed graph and sweep, and
whatever you wanted computed must be smuggled into those numbers.

Which raises the first casualty: problems whose wiring diagram is not the
chip's. The fabric was designed two-colorable; your problem was not consulted.
Suppose three constraints close into a ring of three — the smallest graph that
breaks the checkerboard trick. Try to paint it below.

<Triangle />

Every complete painting leaves at least one same-color wire, so a triangle can
never run red/black as it stands. The repair is the *chain spin*: cut the odd
loop, route the third wire through an auxiliary cell whose chain coupling
holds it equal to its partner. The square that results is two-colorable, and
the counter states the price plainly: four physical spins to host three
logical ones. On real problems this overhead is the dominant cost of using the
chip at all.

The chain strength is a genuine dial, and both of its ends are bad. Drag it
weak and the aux spin forgets its partner: the embedded machine samples
beautifully from the *wrong* law, and the meter pins well off the floor no
matter how long you watch. Drag it strong and the copy is enforced so
fiercely that the chained pair can barely flip at all: the law is now right,
but the sampler stops mixing, and at any honest evidence budget the meter
starves high. Somewhere between sits a band where the marginal is faithful
and the mixing survives — finding it is a real engineering search, run per
problem, and it is the first tax the triangle pays for not being born
bipartite.

One more honest subtraction before the chip earns its compiler. Every figure
so far has assumed all cells share one transfer curve. Fabricated silicon does
not: each comparator comes out of the furnace with its own gain. The knob
below scales a fixed gain-scatter pattern across the audited four-loop while
the <C k="ghost">ghost</C> stays the ideal law — so the violet number is the
exact price of the imperfection.

At ±60% scatter the distribution sits about a tenth from ideal — real error,
delivered with a perfectly healthy-looking flicker, in the same silence that
hid the synchronous failure. Offsets and timing skew cost the same way and are priced the same way;
real designs calibrate them down and budget what remains. This lesson's chip
stays ideal from here on, and you have seen what that idealization is worth.

Four verbs, then, on a fixed graph, at a known price — and none of the verbs
mentions the thing we actually want, which is an instruction. Turning wishes
into biases and wires is a compiler's job.

## Compiling an instruction

A deterministic instruction maps an input to an output. This machine's native
product is distributions, so its native instruction maps an input to a
*distribution over outputs*: a table $K(y\,|\,x)$ with one row per input value,
each row a probability distribution. Here is the simplest interesting one —
copy the input, lie ten percent of the time. The entries are yours to edit;
each row renormalizes as you drag, because a row that does not sum to one is
not a conditional distribution.

<KernelTable />

Any such table is a legal stochastic instruction, and tables wire into
circuits — one kernel's output feeding another's input — exactly as gates
wire into logic. Extropic's name for that language is **Torx**: a Torx
program is a wired composition of kernels like this one, drawn from a small
standard vocabulary of named gates — PNOT flips a bit with a chosen
probability, PSWAP trades two states with another. The ten-percent liar you
are editing is a PNOT that fires one time in ten.

Now compile the copy table by hand, using nothing but the machine's own
verbs. One cell holds $x$; one wire of weight $J$ runs to a free cell $y$;
$\beta$ is locked at 1 on the figure. Drag $J$ until the flip-rate meter
reads the table's lie: 10%.

<CompileCopy />

The knob settles just above 1.1, and the compiled table converges onto the
target with the error pane fading toward white. The number your hand found
has a closed form. The free cell sees field $Jx$, so it disagrees with $x$ at
rate $\sigma(-2\beta J)$; set that to one tenth and solve:

$$\sigma(-2\beta J) = \tfrac{1}{10} \;\Rightarrow\; e^{2\beta J} = 9 \;\Rightarrow\; J = \tfrac{1}{2\beta}\ln 9 \approx 1.099 \;\;(\beta = 1)$$

Check it at the ends: $J = 0$ gives a flip rate of one half — the wire is
silent and $y$ is a fair coin again — and $J \to \infty$ gives a perfect,
noiseless copy, the table frozen to the identity. One coupling, one closed
form, one instruction compiled.

The general recipe has to allow more cells than the two you can see. The
triangle already showed the pattern: its aux spin was physically present and
absent from the answer. A compiled circuit may use any number of such working
spins $w$ — clamp the input, let everything free thermalize, read the output,
ignore the help. The kernel the hardware then implements is

$$\tilde K(y\,|\,x) \;=\; \frac{\sum_{w} e^{-E(x,w,y)}}{\sum_{y',\,w} e^{-E(x,w,y')}}$$

and it is the State Atlas's conditional slice, reused: clamping $x$ throws
away every column of the atlas that disagrees with it and renormalizes the
rest, and marginalizing the working spins is nothing but summing the
surviving columns that differ only in $w$. With no
working spins the sum has one term and the formula collapses back to the
$\sigma$ rule — the boundary case you just compiled. And when the outputs
are few and the working spins don't touch each other, both sums can be done
outright on paper, no sampling anywhere — which is how every fit on
this page has been computed, and why its numbers carry no error bars.

Whether a target table is *reachable* is now a real question with real
stakes, and here is the smallest table that bites. Noisy-XOR: $y$ should
report whether the two inputs differ, and be wrong 10% of the time. Wire $y$
to both inputs, give it a bias, and let gradient descent shape $(h, J)$
directly — with at most two free spins there are only four states per input,
so the machine below computes its kernels and gradients *exactly*, by
enumeration; nothing in this fit is sampled.

The visible-only descent hits a wall at mean error 0.400 — the coin — and no
amount of patience moves it, because its conditional is a monotone function
of a weighted vote, and "do my inputs differ" is not a vote: raising both
inputs together must push a vote one way, while XOR needs it pushed back.
No $(h, J)$ on the visible wires exists, full stop. Switch on the hidden
p-bit and the same plain descent collapses the floor to zero. With $w$ free,
the conditional on $y$ is a *mixture* of two S-curves, one per state of $w$,
and a mixture can rise in the middle where any single S-curve must keep
going one way. One spin you will never read is the difference between impossible and
exact.

That trained object — an energy landscape shaped until its clamped
conditional matches a target table — is called a **thermodynamic kernel**,
and shaping it is precisely the job of **Thermalizers**, the compiler in
Extropic's stack. What Thermalizers emits is a description in **THRML**
terms — biases, couplings, blocks, clamps, the Gibbs machinery to sweep
them — and THRML is what a substrate finally executes, whether that
substrate is a JAX simulation, the sampler running behind every figure on
this page, or Z1 itself, whose silicon runs chromatic Gibbs natively.

Tap the layers: each one lives on a slot of the rail this lesson has worn

from TARGET to ENERGY — from the distribution you want to the landscape that
encodes it. One vocabulary note, for honesty about what this lesson used:
Torx's wires come in three types — the two-state p-bit, the many-state
p-dit, and the continuous p-mode — and everything on this page spoke only
the first. Composing kernels into longer programs, matching the contexts
between stages, and placing-and-routing the result onto a quarter-million
physical sites is a story for a sequel, not this lesson.

## Two measurements and a subtraction

Compiling one instruction meant one hand on one knob until a meter read ten
percent. The machine ahead of us needs hundreds of weights at once, and nobody's
hand is that patient. The weights will have to come from examples.

The examples are six little pictures — a bar, a cross, a box, an X, a checker,
a corner — each drawn on a 4×4 grid of p-bits, <C k="sUp">ink up</C>,
<C k="sDn">blank down</C>. Sixteen p-bits per image, on purpose: small enough
that exact enumeration — the meter's gray half — still audits everything that
happens here.

The first move is destructive. Take a glyph and run it through a short loop:
at step $t$, every pixel flips with probability $p_t$ — eight percent, then
twenty, then fifty. This loop deserves an honest label: it is three lines of
ordinary code, a biased coin per pixel, and nothing about it is thermodynamic.
The machine's native act is still red/black Gibbs on a programmed landscape —
the schedule that turned out to be the chip — and its role here is the
opposite one: it will learn to run this cheap wrecking loop *backwards*.

<Filmstrip />

Watch a few beats. The strip redraws with a fresh wreck each time — corruption
is a distribution, so one ruined frame proves nothing. Each pixel's
keep-probability is the same S-curve as ever, $\sigma(2\beta_t)$, which makes
the last step $\beta_3 = 0$: at fifty percent the pixel forgets which way it
pointed, and $x_3$ is sixteen fair coins. Drag the noise up and the glyph dies
earlier in the strip; the last frame cannot get any deader. The useless fair coin this article
opened with turns out to be manufacturable on demand — and that is the trick's
hinge, because a picture erased down to fair coins can be *re-grown* from fair
coins, one gentle step at a time, if something knows each step's undo.

That something is one small conditional machine per noise level. Its cells come
in three populations. The noisy input $x_t$: sixteen p-bits, always
<C k="held">held</C> — the machine consults the wreck, it never edits it. The
output $y$: sixteen free p-bits that must settle into a plausible
one-step-cleaner image. And four hidden p-bits $w$ with no pixel meaning at
all, free in every phase — room for the machine to represent *which glyph this
smells like*. Wires run from input to output and from hidden to output, none
within a layer — so the free cells form two banks with no internal edges, and
the two-coloring rescue applies to them by construction: flash all of
$y$, flash all of $w$, exactly Gibbs.

Training this machine takes two measurements. In the **data phase**, clamp the
input *and* the answer — $x_t$ and the true cleaner frame $y$ both wear halos —
and let only the hidden four flicker. In the **dream phase**, release the
answer: clamp $x_t$ alone and let $y$ and $w$ run free under red/black sweeps —
the machine hallucinating what it currently believes cleaner frames look like.
The data phase sits on a <C k="held">green</C> wash — the clamped phase, in the
clamp's own color — and the dream phase on a <C k="meter">violet</C> wash, the
sampler running loose. Below the phases, two strips accumulate the same
statistic from each: how strongly input pixels agree with output pixels. Their
difference is the third row.

<PhaseTrainer />

For the first few seconds nothing trains, and the two strips disagree
loudly — the dreams are static, the data is not. Then the trainer starts, and
you can watch the disagreement curve fall as the dream strip creeps toward the
data strip. That is the entire learning rule:

$$\Delta U_{ij} \;\propto\; \langle x_i\, y_j\rangle^{+} \;-\; \langle x_i\, y_j\rangle^{-}$$

— nudge each wire by the gap between what it measured with the answer held
$(+)$ and what it dreamed without it $(-)$, and the same subtraction for every
bias and every hidden wire. A wire that dreams too little agreement gets
warmed; one that dreams too much gets cooled; a wire whose two phases agree is
finished. When every strip matches, the machine's dreams are statistically
indistinguishable from the data, which is the only sense of "learned" on offer
here.

The landscape being nudged is worth writing down. Call the input→output wires
$U$, the hidden→output wires $W$, the output and hidden biases $b$ and $c$:

$$E_\theta(x_t, w, y) \;=\; -\,b\cdot y \;-\; c\cdot w \;-\; x_t^{\mathsf T} U\, y \;-\; w^{\mathsf T} W\, y$$

Check its corners. Kill $W$ and the hidden four disconnect: each output pixel
votes alone, fed only by input pixels — hold that corner, it comes back as a
machine of its own. Grow one diagonal $U_{ii}$ wire large and that pixel
becomes the noisy-copy gate you compiled by hand, one section ago; this energy
is sixteen of those plus company. The dream phase here runs ten sweeps from
the data rather than to full stationarity — the standard shortcut, called
contrastive divergence — so the gradient is slightly approximate, and the
warranty comes from the meter instead: every few seconds the pane on the right
compares the learned kernel against the *exact* reverse conditional, computed
by summing all 65,536 output states, six-glyph mixture arithmetic the
enumerator can still do at this size. The pane shows the auditable corner —
the fenced-patch move again, a 2×2 marginal — while the full 65,536-column
comparison runs offstage in this article's check harness. This is the oracle's
last stand: one lattice size larger and no such number could be printed at
all.

## The wall of dreams

Generation is the training diagram run as a chain. Start from sixteen fair
coins — the fully erased frame the corruption ends at. Clamp them as $x_3$,
let level 3's outputs settle through a few red/black sweeps, and the sample
becomes $x_2$. Clamp, settle, step down. At $x_0$ a glyph has condensed out of
static in three moves.

<DreamChain />

The finished dreams collect along the bottom, and they are the readout: bars,
checkers, crosses — family members, never on a schedule, never in a fixed
order. Starve the slider to one sweep per level and the resemblance slips —
more strays, more smudges — because each sample gets clamped as the next
level's evidence before it has settled. The dreams' coherence is bought with
Gibbs time.

all at once, either. It degrades into a hierarchy of witnesses, each with a
smaller jurisdiction: conditionals that stay exact inside a fenced patch;
moments that theory pins; autocorrelation and effective sample counts; small
embedded problems whose answers are known in advance — every one of them an
instrument this article built at toy scale, still on duty at full scale. A
stochastic computer is trusted the way this page's little walls came to be:
not because its output looks random — everything's output looks random — but
because every witness that can still speak agrees.

## Further Reading

Extropic's Torx paper (arXiv:2608.01612) is the source of the instruction
language taught here: stochastic gates on typed wires — the two-state p-bit
this lesson spoke throughout, plus the p-dit and p-mode it left alone. Its
second half moves the dice into matter: gates whose random draws come from
physical noise on real test silicon rather than a pseudorandom generator,
scored the way this article scored everything — by distance from exact
truth, including the paper's own version of the
broken-versus-colored-schedule comparison staged above.

The Thermalizers paper (arXiv:2608.01615) is the compiler. It is where the
thermodynamic kernel — the clamp, marginalize, normalize object from the
kernel-table page — is developed into a full pipeline: fitting kernels,
matching the contexts between chained stages, and bounding how one stage's
error propagates down a chain. It also describes Z1 itself, the
quarter-million-p-bit two-colorable fabric this lesson's rescue turned out
to be a drawing of. Of everything in it, the habit worth imitating is the
relentless one: compiled programs are judged against exact small-system
truth wherever truth can still be computed.

Both halves of that stack are public code: THRML, Extropic's JAX library
for block Gibbs sampling on sparse heterogeneous graphs — the red/black
sweeps of the big lattice at industrial strength — and the Torx toolkit for
composing kernel programs. If this page left you wanting to run the real
schedule at sizes the browser cannot audit, that is the door.

Stuart and Donald Geman's 1984 paper, *Stochastic Relaxation, Gibbs
Distributions, and the Bayesian Restoration of Images*, is where the Gibbs
sampler got its name — and, forty years early, most of this article's
furniture: a lattice of pixels, each updated from its neighbors'
conditional, images condensing out of noise. Read it after this page and the
sampler running every figure here is recognizably theirs — waiting four
decades for silicon willing to run it natively.

with, and the thing every other chip in the room spends most of its power
shouting down. Nothing is listening to it. For one afternoon, on this page,
some of it had a job.
