# Is Light a Wave or a Particle?

> *Is light made of particles, or of waves?*
> — Quantum mechanics says: both.
> *How can it be both?*
> — It can't. But it is.

That last line is a report, not a shrug, and it comes from a bench any
undergraduate lab can build.

Below: a lamp, a card with two narrow slits cut in it, a screen two metres
behind. The loupe is there because the card's workmanship is invisible at arm's
length — each slit is a twenty-fifth of a millimetre across, the pair a fifth of
a millimetre apart. Run the lamp bright and the screen wears the striped
<C k="pdf">pattern</C> drawn at the right; that pattern is the whole subject of
this article.

<BenchSetup />

The lamp is turned down until photons leave it separately — the slider
raises the rate from a trickle to a beam. The upper card has both slits open. The
lower card is the same bench with the right slit covered over. Nothing else about
the two differs.

<PhotonRain />

Each <C k="hit">dot</C> is one photon arriving. The
<C k="pdf">profile under each screen</C> is a tally of the dots above it — bins
counted from the arrivals, not a curve drawn over them. That colour scheme holds
for the whole article: amber is always a single arrival, violet is always a
measured count, and <C k="amp">blue</C> is always the wave-like quantity that
does the deciding.

Turn the rate to the bottom and watch. The arrivals are separate events. Each one
is the whole of a photon landing at one place and nowhere else; nothing lands
half here and half there, and no dot is fainter than any other dot. Detectors do
not report fractions of a photon, ever, at any brightness. That is as
particle-like as behaviour gets.

Now let it run. The dots do not spread evenly. They pile into bars about six
millimetres apart, with stripes between the bars where almost nothing ever lands.
A single photon has no way to know where the bars are — it is one event, and the
bars are a property of the collection — yet every photon avoids the stripes
anyway. That is as wave-like as behaviour gets.

Neither of those observations is the problem. The problem is the red column.

It sits 3.2 mm off centre, and it is drawn at the same place on both screens. On
the lower screen, with one slit open, it is one of the brightest places on the
card — 97% of peak. On the upper screen, with two slits open, it is one of the
darkest. Cutting a second hole in the card made that spot dimmer. Not
proportionally dimmer, not dimmer as a share of a larger total: **fewer photons
per second land there with two openings than with one.**

Nothing that travels as a lump can do that. A lump that goes through the left
slit and a lump that goes through the right slit are two disjoint groups of
events, and the rate at the red column is the sum of the two group rates. Sums of
non-negative things do not go down when you add another one. You cannot subtract
by opening a door.

There is a second number in the same figure, and it is the same effect with its
sign reversed. The bright bars stand four times the height of the one-slit
profile, while the total light through the card only doubled. The bars borrowed
their excess from the stripes; integrate the two profiles across the whole screen
and the ratio is exactly 2, which is the honest accounting of a card with twice
as many holes. Interference does not create light. It relocates it, and the
relocation is what no collection of independent lumps can perform.

## Light Where No Ray Could Go

For two centuries "light is a wave" was not a philosophical position but a
working prediction. Here is the prediction.

One slit now, and one knob: how wide it is. The <C k="amp">filled curve</C> is
what arrives on the screen. The dashed top hat is what ray optics predicts —
light exactly where the slit is, dark everywhere else, edges sharp. The vertical
scale is the pattern's own brightest point rather than the unobstructed level,
because a narrow slit passes so little light that on an absolute scale its
pattern would be a flat line along the floor; what it gave up is the third meter,
and it is worth watching too.

<SlitSpread />

Wide open, the two agree over the middle, and disagree at the edges in a specific
way: the actual intensity rings, overshooting about 40% above the unobstructed
level in the first fringe inside each edge before settling. Squeeze the slit and
the agreement dies altogether. Below about a millimetre the light stops
respecting the geometry that produced it — the lit patch stops shrinking with the
slit and starts *growing* — and at 0.05 mm a patch 22 mm wide comes out of an
opening a twentieth of a millimetre across, with measurable light everywhere
else on the card as well.

Straight lines cannot do this. Every photon that got through passed within
0.025 mm of the axis, and they land as far as 30 mm away — more than a thousand
times the width of the hole they came through, with nothing in the apparatus to
deflect them.

The number that governs the crossover is printed in the figure: $a^2/\lambda L$,
the slit width squared over the wavelength times the distance. It compares the
aperture to the size of the region a wave can reach out of it, and it is the only
thing that matters — not the slit's absolute size, not the brightness, not the
detector. Above about 1 the ray picture is accurate; below about 1 it is not
approximately right, it is qualitatively wrong. The single curve in the figure is
the exact Fresnel diffraction integral and holds across the whole slider; ray
optics and the far-field $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ pattern are its two limits, not two
rival theories.

## Tip to Tail

So light has a wavelength, and things with wavelengths interfere. Here is the
mechanism, exactly.

Give each route to a point on the screen a two-dimensional arrow — an
<C k="amp">amplitude</C>. Its length is set by how much light that route
delivers. Its direction is set by how long the route is, turning through a full
circle for every wavelength travelled. Then: **lay the arrows tip to tail, and
the arrival rate is the square of the length of the closing arrow.**

<PhasorSum />

The <C k="slitA">first arrow</C> is the left slit's contribution and the
<C k="slitB">second</C> is the right slit's; the angle between them is the phase
the extra path costs. Only that angle is physical — turning both arrows together
changes nothing anyone can measure — so I have split the turn evenly between
them, which keeps the <C k="pdf">closing arrow</C> lying flat and makes its
length the only thing moving.

Drag along the screen. At the centre both routes are the same length, the arrows
line up, and the closing arrow is twice either one — so the arrival rate is
*four* times what one slit delivers. That is the factor of four from the hero
figure, arriving here as the square of a doubled length.

Keep dragging. The path difference climbs, the arrows swing apart, and half a
wavelength out they are opposed: the chain walks out and straight back to where
it started, and the closing arrow has no length at all. Zero arrivals. Both
routes are open, both are delivering light, and nothing arrives. The dashed line
is what the rate would be if the two contributions were combined as amounts
rather than as arrows — flat, featureless, and never zero. It is what the hero's
lower screen measures, doubled.

Written out, with $I_A$ and $I_B$ the rates each slit delivers alone and $\delta$
the phase the extra path costs:

$$
I \;=\; |\psi_A + \psi_B|^2 \;=\; I_A + I_B + 2\sqrt{I_A I_B}\,\cos\delta .
$$

The first two terms are the arithmetic a particle picture allows. The third is
the entire disagreement, it has no sign, and its average over a screen is zero —
which is why interference never adds or destroys light, only moves it.

<Waypoint>
  Two facts are now on the table and they do not fit together. Light arrives in
  indivisible units at single points; and the rate at which those units arrive is
  the square of a sum of arrows, which can cancel. Nothing yet forces the second
  fact to be about the individual unit rather than about crowds of them, and
  until something does, the contradiction is escapable.
</Waypoint>

The loophole is real and worth naming, because it is the answer everyone reaches
for first: perhaps the arrows describe a *beam*, some collective property of many
photons jostling, and a lone photon knows nothing of them. The hero figure has
already answered it. Turn the rate to the bottom and there is one photon in the
apparatus at a time — the next leaves the lamp long after the previous one has
landed, so there is no crowd to be a property of. The stripes still come. Whatever
carries the phase, it accompanies each photon separately.

## The Fact That Broke the Wave

The experiment that ends the wave account has no slits in it at all.

Shine light on a metal and electrons come off. The classical account is
unambiguous: light is an oscillating electric field, the field pushes on
electrons, a stronger field pushes harder, and an electron that is pushed harder
leaves faster. Brightness should therefore control the energy of the ejected
electrons. And a dim light should still work, just slowly — the electron sits in
the field absorbing until it has enough to escape.

  <Photoelectric />
</Predict>

The <C k="pdf">left bar</C> counts electrons per second; the
<C k="ejecta">right bar</C> is the energy of the fastest one. The dashed outline
drawn over that bar is the classical prediction — energy per electron rising with
the field strength. Crank brightness. The outline climbs to the top of the frame,
the count bar climbs with it, and the energy bar stays exactly where it was
through a twentyfold change in how hard the field is shaking.

Now move the colour instead. The energy bar climbs, and it climbs as a straight
line in *frequency* — the left panel plots it. Follow the line down to the left
and it meets zero at a definite frequency, and below that frequency nothing
happens at all. Switch to platinum, whose electrons are held more tightly, and the
whole line slides right without changing slope. At any brightness, at any
duration, red light frees nothing from platinum. There is no dim-but-patient
regime; the delay never arrives.

Einstein's 1905 reading of this is one line:

$$
K_{\max} = h\nu - \varphi .
$$

Light of frequency $\nu$ delivers its energy in indivisible packets of size
$h\nu$. One packet goes to one electron. The electron spends $\varphi$ — the
work function, a property of the metal — buying its way out of the surface, and
keeps the rest. Brightness sets how many packets arrive per second and has no
say in what any one of them contains. A packet too small to pay $\varphi$ frees
nothing, and a billion of them per second still free nothing, because they cannot
pool.

The slope of that line is $h$, the same $h$ that sets the size of the packet. It
is a number about light that has nothing to do with the metal, and it does not
appear anywhere in the wave account. Millikan spent the better part of a decade
trying to break the equation, and in 1916 published a measurement of $h$ from its
slope, agreeing with the modern value to about one percent.

<Waypoint>
  Both halves are now nailed down by measurement. The arrows are real: interference
  survives one photon at a time, so each photon carries the phase. The packets are
  real: energy arrives in lumps of $h\nu$ that cannot be subdivided or accumulated.
  The contradiction is no longer avoidable by picking the better of two pictures,
  because each picture is now the unique explanation of an experiment the other
  cannot touch.
</Waypoint>

## The Exchange Rate

Put a detector at the slits. Each photon is indivisible, so it went through one
or the other; find out which, and keep watching the stripes. That would put the
contradiction on the table in a single run — a definite path *and* an
interference pattern — and it is the obvious thing to try.

The measurement has been done, in many forms. It never yields both. What it
yields is a rate of exchange.

<WhichPath />

The slider is how much the apparatus knows — <C k="cutoff">$D$</C>, path
distinguishability, from nothing to certainty. The screen answers immediately.
Learn a little and the stripes shallow out; learn everything and they are gone,
leaving the flat doubled profile of two independent slits. The marker on the left
is the pair the relation below forces; the <C k="pdf">contrast meter</C> under
the screen is measured off the plotted pattern instead, so the two can be read
against each other. The marker traces a quarter circle and cannot leave it:

$$
V^2 + D^2 \le 1 ,
$$

with equality whenever nothing else has disturbed the light. Perfect stripes cost
total ignorance of the path. Certainty about the path costs every stripe. And
crucially, the intermediate settings are not a mixture of two runs — they are one
pattern with partial contrast, and every point on that quarter circle is
realisable.

The demand that light be a wave *or* a particle is a demand that it fit
one of two categories abstracted from things at our own scale: swell on water,
stones in flight. Nothing obliges it to fit either. What it does is definite and
has been stated twice already — an amplitude that adds like an arrow, and a
squared length that counts arrivals. "Wave" is the correct name for the adding.
"Particle" is the correct name for the counting. Each is right about its half and
wrong about the whole.

And the reason the two halves never collide is arithmetic, not diplomacy. To
catch a contradiction you would need sharp stripes and a known path in the same
run. $V^2 + D^2 \le 1$ says the apparatus that sharpens one blurs the other, by
exactly enough. It is paradoxical in the strict sense — it violates our
expectation — and it has never once produced an inconsistent measurement, because
the inconsistent measurement is the one the relation forbids.

## Not a Fact About Light

None of this was about light.

De Broglie's 1924 proposal was that the relation between wavelength and momentum
runs both ways. Light was known to carry momentum $p = h/\lambda$; he read it
backwards. Anything with momentum has a wavelength $\lambda = h/p$. Davisson and
Germer put electrons through a nickel crystal in 1927 and found the diffraction
pattern the formula demands.

The ruler below is metres, logarithmic, twenty-nine decades wide. Each specimen
sits at its own $\lambda$, computed from its own momentum. The readout says what
fringe spacing that wavelength would produce on the bench this whole article has
been using — the same $\lambda L / d$, the same two slits 0.20 mm apart, the same
two metres.

<DeBroglieRuler />

The red photon lands at 633 nm and reproduces the 6.33 mm fringes from the hero
figure, which is the arithmetic closing on itself. The 100-volt electron lands at
123 picometres — four thousand times shorter than visible light, which is why
electron microscopes resolve what optical ones cannot. The C₆₀ molecule, sixty
carbon atoms in a ball, lands at 2.8 picometres; Arndt and colleagues sent those
through a grating in 1999 and got fringes.

Then select the baseball. Its wavelength is $1.1 \times 10^{-34}$ m, and its
fringes on this bench would stand $1.1 \times 10^{-30}$ m apart — less than
$10^{-15}$ of the width of a proton. The baseball is not exempt from the arrows.
Its arrows turn so fast that no aperture anyone could build would spread them far
enough apart to notice, and every question about it that can actually be posed
has the same answer under both accounts. Classical mechanics is what this
arithmetic looks like from the far left of that ruler.

## And Yet It Works

The rule assembled here — attach an arrow to every route, lay them tip to tail,
square the closing length — is not a heuristic for slits. Extended to fields, it
is quantum electrodynamics, and the arithmetic is the same arithmetic. Its
sharpest test is the magnetic moment of the electron, measured to thirteen
digits. Compute it from these amplitudes and it agrees — to about a part in ten
billion, which is not where the theory runs out but where the independently
measured fine-structure constant the calculation needs does.

What was refuted along the way is an assumption, not an experiment: that a thing
must be one of the two kinds of thing we already had names for. That assumption
was never physics. It was inherited vocabulary, and the bench declined to honour
it. What survived is exact — amplitudes add, arrivals are counted by the square,
and the two descriptions we find intuitive are the two ends of a relation that
will not let both be sharp at once.

It cannot be both, in the only sense of "both" our language offered. It is both,
in the sense the arithmetic requires. And that arithmetic has so far survived
being checked to one part in ten billion.
