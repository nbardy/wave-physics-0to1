# The History of Navier–Stokes

The name reads like a partnership. It wasn't one. Claude-Louis Navier and George
Gabriel Stokes never met and never corresponded; they worked twenty-three years and
one Channel apart, and Navier had been dead for nine years when Stokes published.
The hyphen between them was installed by posterity — and it skips over three other
men who derived the same equation in between, each convinced the others had done it
wrong.

Its terms took shape through a hundred and sixty years of argument. Below, the
wing and the incoming stream stay the same while the model of the fluid changes.

## Still Water

For its first two thousand years, the mathematics of water only worked when the
water didn't move. Archimedes settled floating around 250 BC: a body immersed in
fluid is pushed up by the weight of the fluid it displaces. That single sentence
still runs shipyards. In the tank below, the slider sets the block's density
relative to the water's:

<BuoyancyCrown />

The block sinks exactly deep enough for the displaced water to pay for its weight —
at half the water's density it rides half-submerged, and past the water's density no
depth is deep enough, so it goes down. Statics gives crisp, exact answers like this,
and it gives them about *rest* only.

Pressure, too, was born in still fluid. In 1643 Evangelista Torricelli stood a
closed tube of mercury upside down in a dish and watched the column fall to a
fixed height, leaving a vacuum above it. A tilted, wider, or longer tube changes
nothing: at sea level the mercury still stands 760 mm above the dish. What the air supports is a
vertical head, and the
[balance](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/14-2-measuring-pressure)
is $p_{\rm air}=\rho g h$. The height is a reading of the air's weight.
Torricelli pictured an ocean of it with us submerged at the bottom. Pascal took
that picture seriously: climb a mountain and there is less air above you. If
its weight supports the mercury, the column should fall.

But a falling column alone would not settle it. The weather might have changed
while you were climbing. On 19 September 1648, Pascal's brother-in-law Florin
Périer compared two tubes in Clermont, found their readings equal, and left one
with Father Chastin to watch throughout the day. He carried the other up the
Puy-de-Dôme. The tube that stayed behind was as important as the one that climbed.

Follow the three stops below. Each instrument is Torricelli's tube, the dark
column mercury, and both are drawn on a common baseline so you can compare
their heights even when they are in different places. These are the observations
Périer reported, with the old French units converted approximately to
millimeters.

<PascalMountain />

[Périer's account](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Œuvres_de_Blaise_Pascal/Lettre_de_Florin_Perier_à_Blaise_Pascal)
gives the summit drop as three French inches and one and a half lines—about
8.5 centimeters. He repeated the summit measurement in exposed and sheltered
places, and reported the same height. The result survived both a change of
surroundings and the journey back.

Less air overhead, less pressure, a shorter mercury column: the instrument gave
Torricelli's ocean a measurable depth. We are still weighing fluids at rest.
Pressure inside moving water, redirecting it from one place to another, is a
century away.

Moving water posed a harder demand. Leonardo's [studies of flowing water,
around 1510–13](https://www.rct.uk/collection/912663/recto-studies-of-flowing-water-with-notes-verso-the-head-of-a-woman)
trace curling waves through cascades. A drawing can record where water went.
But push the water somewhere else, and what happens next?

In the upper view, each place keeps its starting velocity forever. In the lower
view, a modern fluid equation updates those velocities as the water moves.
First let them run. Then **drag through either view to stir the lower fluid**;
the same fresh dye appears in both. Try **Add dye only** as well: should adding a

supply the missing rule for how velocity changes.

Knowing the motion now and calculating the motion next: that was the missing
step. Newton made it the central problem of mechanics.

## Newton's Guess

The *Principia* of 1687 comes in three books, and the second — the one about motion
through resisting fluids — is the least read and the most wrong. Buried in it is
a one-sentence guess that turned out to be exactly right. Newton proposed that the resistance arising from the *want
of lubricity* between parts of a fluid is proportional to the velocity with which
those parts are separated from one another. Give two equal masses of water
opposite velocities. If they exchange momentum, which one should slow down?

Both views below start with the upper layers moving right and the lower layers
moving left. The upper view keeps them uncoupled. In the lower one, viscosity
lets adjacent layers pull on each other. Move the coupling slider, then watch
near the seam; changing it starts both views together. **Kick layers apart**
repeats the experiment at the current setting:

<MomentumExchange />

Both sides slow near the seam, and the change spreads outward. The colored
markers show displacement; the arrows show signed velocity. No wall is driving
these layers, and no momentum leaves at the outer edges. Their total momentum
stays zero even as their relative motion fades. With no coupling, the two views
agree. Strong coupling makes their disagreement visible within a few seconds.

Stress proportional to the rate of sliding remains the defining assumption for a
Newtonian fluid, and it is the physical content of the equation's green viscous
term. Newton stated it in passing, as a hypothesis, and
made no attempt to put it inside an equation of motion. It waited a hundred and
thirty-five years for one.

His actual theory of fluid resistance went differently, and it is worth watching
fail. Newton computed drag by treating a fluid as a hail of independent particles
striking the front of the body:

<CorpuscleHail />

The averaged reading settles on a perfectly definite drag — Newton could calculate
with this model, which is why it survived — but look at what the "fluid" is doing.
The faint gray lines show where a continuous fluid would go; the hail does nothing
of the kind. Nothing goes *around* the disc. There is no wake, just an empty
shadow; the particles never feel each other at all. Water is not like this: every
parcel leans on its neighbors, and pushing one somewhere means the whole
neighborhood rearranges. (The corpuscle model did eventually come back to life —
two and a half centuries later, for spacecraft skimming the thin upper atmosphere,
where the molecules really don't feel each other.)

The same book also computed the speed of sound, and came out about fifteen
percent slow; Laplace supplied the correction in 1816.

A quarrel that began with the *Principia* shaped the next century: the calculus
wars. When the priority fight came to trial at the Royal Society, the committee's
report was drafted, anonymously, by its president — Newton, ruling on his own
case. Britain sided with Newton's notation out of loyalty and clung to it for a
hundred years, while the Continent ran ahead with Leibniz's. The next hundred
years of fluid mechanics are therefore Swiss and French; Britain rejoins in 1845.

## The Family Business

The first book with the field's name on it was *Hydrodynamica*, published in 1738
by Daniel Bernoulli — he coined the word. Its method was bookkeeping: track the
energy of water flowing through pipes and orifices, and trade speed against
pressure along the way. Narrow the pipe below and watch the gauges:

<BernoulliPipe />

Where the pipe narrows, the markers speed up and the pressure falls. Where it
widens, they slow down and the pressure rises. Bernoulli's energy balance relates
these changes, but it does not determine the full velocity field.

What the book could not do is as important as what it could. An energy ledger along
a pipe says nothing about water in the open — it cannot tell you what the stream
around our gray disc *does*, point by point — and it carries no friction at all.
It also nearly didn't survive its own family. Daniel's father Johann, one of the
great mathematicians of Europe, published his own treatise, *Hydraulica*, dated it
1732 — six years before it was actually written — and claimed the subject as his.
"I am robbed of my entire Hydrodynamica," Daniel wrote to a friend in Berlin,
"of which I truly did not owe one iota to my father." The friend was
Leonhard Euler, who admired them both, and who was about to make the quarrel
obsolete.

## The Prize Question

Before anyone could write the law of the open stream, someone had to invent the
kind of mathematics a law like that would be written in. In 1747 Jean le Rond
d'Alembert, studying a plucked string, produced the first partial differential
equation in physics — an equation relating how a shape changes in time to how it
curves in space:

<StringSection stage="string" />

The instrument was built *for a wave*: the string got its equation a decade before
fluids got theirs.

It also started a thirty-year fight, and the fight was over what a *solution* even
is. D'Alembert held that the string's starting shape had to be expressible as a
single formula — pluck it into a corner, two straight segments meeting at a point,
and his mathematics refused the shape. Euler thought that absurd: a real string can
be pulled into any shape a hand can draw, so the mathematics must accept any curve
you can draw. Daniel Bernoulli said both were making it hard — every motion a
string can perform is a sum of its pure modes, the fundamental plus its harmonics,
and nothing else. Each of the three was partly right, none could finish the
argument, and it outlived all of them; Fourier settled it two generations later by
showing that Bernoulli's sums of modes can build Euler's hand-drawn curves.

Then the Berlin Academy asked a question that looked much easier. Its 1749 prize
called for the resistance of a body moving through a fluid — the practical problem
of the age, the number that decides hull shapes and cannon ranges — worked out for
the simplest fluid imaginable: no friction, no stickiness, nothing but inertia and
pressure. That fluid is the one d'Alembert's instrument could actually handle, so
he took the question and computed. A theory of moving water ought to recover
something as ordinary as the resistance you feel pulling an oar.

Hold a long cylinder across a stream and look at it end-on: it appears as the
circle below. The gray dots follow the water. The colored arrows belong to the
*cylinder*: they show the horizontal part of the pressure force at each patch of
its surface. Before adding those forces, commit to a guess.

The water exerts forces, but **their sum is zero**. The brown arrows contribute
downstream; the indigo arrows contribute upstream. Reflect any patch across the
vertical dashed line and you find a partner with the same pressure and the
opposite horizontal force. The two large arrows collect those contributions.

Try doubling **Stream speed** from 1× to 2×. Each total grows from 1 to 4; the
faint gray arrows keep the original size in view. The pressure changes grow as
speed squared, so the water acts harder on both sides. It still produces no net
drag. Slowing the stream to 0.5× shrinks both totals to a quarter. You cannot get
resistance out of this flow by turning it up.

These forces measure the pressure change from the undisturbed stream. Uniform
surrounding pressure already cancels around a closed body, so subtracting it
doesn't change the drag. A low-pressure patch can therefore contribute an outward
*pull* in this accounting. Color tells you the direction of the horizontal force;
it does not divide the cylinder into a front half and a back half.

Why do the pressures match? Follow the paths around the cylinder. Water slows
near the nose, speeds up around the shoulders, then slows again toward the rear.
By Bernoulli's law, pressure falls as speed rises and recovers as speed falls.
**In this solution the flow stays attached all the way around.** Nothing takes a
cut from the mechanical energy: the water climbs the back pressure hill with
what it gained coming down the front one. The rear pressure recovers completely,
and the forces cancel. This is the [exact potential-flow solution](https://www-mdp.eng.cam.ac.uk/web/library/enginfo/aerothermal_dvd_only/aero/fprops/poten/node37.html);
real water can leave a separated wake behind a cylinder, and the rear pressure
then fails to recover.

D'Alembert knew the verdict was absurd — the prize question was precisely the
resistance that every sailor and swimmer feels. Sixteen years on he was still
turning it over: the theory, developed in all possible rigor, gives in several
cases a strictly vanishing resistance — "a singular paradox," he wrote, "which I
leave to future Geometers to elucidate." The Academy never awarded the 1749 prize
to anyone, on the grounds that no entrant had backed the theory with experiment;
Euler sat among the judges.

Keep your eye on that back hill.

## Euler's Field

Euler's answer came in a burst of memoirs around 1757, and its deepest move is one
your hands already know.
Stop following individual bits of water. Stand still, and ask instead: at *this*
point, at *this* instant, how fast is the water moving?

<FlowVis mode="arrows" />

This picture — a velocity attached to every point of space, $\mathbf{u}(x, y, t)$ —
is Euler's invention. So is the second field standing behind it: he took pressure,
Torricelli's static weight, and set it free to vary from point to point *inside*
moving water, pushing every parcel from its high side toward its low side. Then he
wrote Newton's law, force equals mass times acceleration, for an infinitesimal
parcel carried by the flow — and the pieces snapped together for the first time
in history:

Euler also understood the difficulty of solving his equations. If we cannot fully grasp the
motion of fluids, he wrote, it is not mechanics we should blame, nor any hidden
principle of nature still missing — it is analysis itself that fails us. He was
right that writing the equations would not be enough to solve them.

But run your eye back over that term stack. The green slot is empty, and the drag
meter from the last section still reads zero — Euler's equations inherit
d'Alembert's paradox whole. Worse, in this world honey and water obey the *same*
equation. The equations still have no way to account for viscosity.

## The Missing Term

The man who found it was not a professor of mathematics. Claude-Louis Navier built
bridges — star engineer of the École des Ponts et Chaussées, editor of the standard
construction manuals of France — and his career carried an engineer's scars. His
showcase project, a radical suspension bridge over the Seine at the Invalides,
cracked at an anchorage when a water main burst in 1826, and was dismantled to
public mockery — the papers called him "that eminent man of science whose
calculations fail in Paris" — and a review committee reproached him for having
relied *too much on mathematics*. His closest teacher and lifelong friend
was Fourier, the man who wrote the mathematics of heat.

On the 18th of March, 1822, Navier read a memoir to the Académie des Sciences in
which he did what Euler could not. His route was strange: he imagined water as a
lattice of molecules pulling on their neighbors, with a pull that depends on how
fast they slide past one another. A moving layer could drag the next one along.
That is the missing mechanism: **motion can spread sideways, across the flow.**

Below, identical plates move back and forth above two layers of fluid. The
<C k="dye">amber dots</C> mark fluid parcels; the <C k="vel">blue arrows</C> show
which way they move and how fast. With no coupling, the plate slides over still
fluid. With coupling, it carries nearby fluid along. Turn the slider from weak
to strong and watch how far down that motion reaches. The shaded middle row is
our comparison point; its readout measures its travel as a percentage of the
plate's travel over a full cycle.

<MolecularSprings />

The lower layers move less and turn around later. Stronger coupling passes more
of the plate's motion down to them. The gray tracks mark each parcel's full
excursion, so you can still compare the layers when a dot stops to reverse. The
fluid here is a continuum, not Navier's lattice of molecules.

What matters in his argument is the local balance. A layer moving faster than
its neighbors slows down; a slower one speeds up. The change depends on its
neighbors' average velocity minus its own. In the continuum limit, that
difference becomes the Laplacian of velocity, multiplied by a constant. It is
the heat operator, learned from Fourier and written into water. The stack is complete:

**The molecules were fiction, and the term was right.** Navier's intermolecular
model was wrong in its details, and his constant — he called it $\varepsilon$ —
was a molecular quantity he could not connect to a measured viscosity. The
correct equation did not settle the dispute over its foundations. Four more
derivations followed, each starting from different assumptions about the fluid.

(One more of Navier's headaches: at the wall, he assumed the fluid *slides*. The
1822 and 1845 stops of the first figure differ in exactly this wall condition.
The century's pipe experiments would say otherwise — but keep a little sympathy
for the slip condition; it has a modern afterlife.)

## Five Times

What followed was not five isolated flashes of genius. It was one long, in-person
Paris argument about what a fluid actually *is* — molecules or continuum — carried
out in memoirs, referee reports, and open polemic, with the same equation falling
out the end every time.

Cauchy entered the argument in 1823 while refereeing an earlier memoir of
Navier's, on elastic solids. He asked a
more general question: how do we describe the internal forces at a point in any
material? His answer was the stress tensor. Drag a face of the fluid element below:

<StressCube />

Drag one face and all four answer. The sideways drag you apply to the top must be
matched by sideways drags on the *vertical* faces — the matrix stays symmetric —
and the two ghosts beside it show what the symmetry is protecting you from. Their
$\sigma_{yx}$ is pinned at zero, so the couple on them is unbalanced, and they
spin — the smaller one faster, because torque shrinks with a parcel's volume while
its resistance to turning shrinks faster still. Follow that to an infinitesimal
parcel and the spin rate diverges. That object — nine numbers at every point, describing every possible
internal push — is the container into which you can drop *any* material's law:
steel, honey, water, air. Cauchy built the container; the fluid law is one thing
you can put in it.

Poisson re-derived the equation in 1829 by his own molecular route, compressibility
included, satisfied with nobody else's foundations — and said so in print. In the
polemic that ran through the Annales de Chimie, his sharpest shot went at the
foundations: Navier's own assumptions, taken seriously, give a solid with zero
elasticity — a material that could not hold a shape. Navier answered that
it was he who had "conceived the idea of a new question," and that all this new
talk of rigor was a way of belittling the man who asked it.

Saint-Venant derived it in 1837 by making internal stress proportional to the
local rate of shearing strain, the approach used today. He published in 1843,
two years ahead of Stokes, and carefully credited the earlier contributions.
He was also the one who defended Navier's bridge in print — the failure, he wrote,
was one misjudged force on one stone, the kind of oversight fixed on site, and the
dismantling was the work of a "spirit of denigration... of science, disparaged
under the name of theory opposed to practice." His contribution is absent from
the equation's name, which credits the first and last of its five discoverers.

George Gabriel Stokes published the last of these derivations in 1845. He belonged
to the first generation of Cambridge mathematicians trained in the Continental
calculus. Working from the French literature's
conclusions but distrusting every molecular story in it, he re-derived the equation
from continuum assumptions alone: no molecules, just smoothness, symmetry, and the
requirement that internal friction depend linearly on the rate of deformation.
That derivation became the standard textbook approach. Stokes also tested the
equation against measurements. His 1851 analysis of
a sphere sinking through viscous fluid gave a clean, checkable prediction —
$F = 6\pi\mu R U$ — and the pendulum data of the age matched it. Two steel balls,
each in its own column of honey, are released together below; the slider shrinks
the second one:

<FallingSphere />

Halve the radius and the small ball is a quarter of the way down when the big one
lands — terminal speed goes as $R^2$, and the pair of bars over each column says
why. Weight scales with volume, as $R^3$, while the factor
$6\pi\mu R$ multiplying speed in the drag law scales only as $R$. The smaller ball therefore reaches a lower
terminal speed. Conversely, timing a ball whose size and weight you know lets
you determine $\mu$. Navier's $\varepsilon$, which had connected to nothing anyone could
measure, became a number you could look up for honey, water, and air. The same analysis settled a decades-old argument about the wall:
the fluid *sticks* — no slip. (Navier's slip survives in one place: channels a
few nanometres wide, where nanofluidics has quietly rehabilitated him.)

The equation's first exact laboratory triumph had, fittingly, nothing to do with
any of the five. Jean Poiseuille was a physician measuring blood flow through fine
glass capillaries in the 1840s; Gotthilf Hagen, a German engineer, found the same
law in brass pipes independently. Both pipes below have the same length, fluid, and
pressure drop. The upper pipe stays at radius 1; the slider changes the lower one.
At radius 1 they must collect equal volumes. Narrow the lower pipe and watch how
quickly that equality disappears:

<PoiseuillePipe />

The columns collect over the same eight-second interval and empty together.
Changing the radius starts a fresh comparison. Their heights measure fluid volume

Halve the radius and the flow falls to a sixteenth. Flux scales as the *fourth
power* of the radius. The equation reproduced the dependence measured in the
capillaries, giving the viscous term a direct experimental test.

<Waypoint>
The equation predicts how fast a small sphere falls and how much water passes
through a narrow pipe. Viscosity is now something we can measure and put into the
calculation. But these successes concern steady, orderly flows. Send water faster
through the same pipe and it can start to swirl and mix. The equation still
applies; finding the motion from it becomes much harder.
</Waypoint>

## Direct and Sinuous

That change was made visible in 1883, in Manchester, by Osborne Reynolds and a
piece of glassware: a long transparent tube drawing water from a tank, with a
thread of dye released on its axis. At low speed the thread runs the length of the
tube like a stretched wire. The dye is a marker carried by the water: a straight
thread tells us that water released at the injector stays near the axis.
If that water moves sideways as well, the thread changes shape.

while every marked parcel keeps moving downstream. New dye keeps arriving
behind it, so the thread contains different parcels as time passes. The slider
sets an imposed sideways motion in a prescribed velocity field, while the mean
volume flow through each channel stays the same; it does not change the
viscosity, and the figure does not calculate how the disturbance arises.

In Reynolds's experiment, increasing the flow could break the narrow thread
into what he, by the light of an electric spark, resolved into "a mass of more
or less distinct curls." He named the two regimes *direct* and *sinuous*; we
say laminar and turbulent. The relevant comparison combines speed, size, and
viscosity into one dimensionless number:

$$Re = \frac{UL}{\nu}$$

Here $U$ is mean speed, $L$ is pipe diameter, and $\nu$ is kinematic viscosity.
At fixed diameter and viscosity, increasing speed raises $Re$. Pipe transition
also depends on the disturbances admitted at the inlet; a single critical
number cannot describe every preparation of the experiment. The christening came later,
when Sommerfeld called it the *Reynolds number* in a 1908 lecture, and the name
stuck. One of the two referees who handled Reynolds's paper at the Royal Society
was Stokes — sixty-three years old, the man whose own pendulum memoir had carried
the number in embryo three decades earlier.

Reynolds also saw what the eruption meant for the mathematics, and in 1895 he made
the move the field still leans on: split the velocity into a mean and a
fluctuation, $\mathbf{u} = \bar{\mathbf{u}} + \mathbf{u}'$, and average the
equation. Averaging describes the flow's *statistics*, introducing correlations
between the fluctuations that also need to be determined. Turbulent motion
still obeys the fluid equation; averaging changes what we choose to calculate.

By 1900 the equation had experimental support, and Reynolds had identified a
measure of when viscosity matters. Yet d'Alembert's zero drag remained unexplained.
At high $Re$, viscous forces are small through much of the flow. Why, then, does
neglecting them give such a poor prediction of drag?

## Ten Minutes in Heidelberg

On the 12th of August, 1904, at the Third International Congress of Mathematicians
in Heidelberg, a twenty-nine-year-old engineering professor named Ludwig Prandtl
was allotted ten minutes. The paper he summarized ran about eight pages.

After separation, the rear pressure no longer recovers to the ideal-flow value.
The front and back pressure forces fail to cancel. The meter integrates that
imbalance from the simulated pressure field. It shows the dimensionless
pressure-drag coefficient, excluding the additional force from viscous shear
at the wall.

This is what the eight pages contained. At high Reynolds number, viscous effects
[remain strong in a thin layer](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/boundary-layer/)
next to the surface. Prandtl added no new force term. He separated the near-wall
region, where velocity changes rapidly with distance, from the outer flow, where
viscosity can often be neglected.

The resolution is stranger than "d'Alembert was wrong."
The frictionless calculation is internally consistent. What fails is applying
its attached, symmetric flow to a body with a separated wake. A thin boundary
layer can change an extended region behind the body and alter the pressure
forces around it. Prandtl located a small region whose consequences are large;
he did not confine every discrepancy to that region.

In the Heidelberg audience sat Felix Klein, who had already secured Prandtl for
Göttingen that summer. Out of Göttingen came Blasius, then a doctoral student
named Theodore von Kármán, whose 1911 analysis of the staggered vortex wake gave
the Kármán vortex street its name, and much of the century's aerodynamics.

## The Open Question

Lewis Fry Richardson spent the First World War as an ambulance driver, and between
runs to the front he computed, by hand, the world's first numerical weather
forecast. It took him six weeks and it was catastrophically wrong — a predicted
pressure rise about a hundred times too large, wrecked by unbalanced starting data.
Undeterred, he published the method in 1922, along with a fantasy he costed out
seriously: sixty-four thousand human computers in a great hall, conducted like an
orchestra, calculating the weather faster than the weather. His cells were some
two hundred kilometres across, and inside one cell the wind carried whirls of
every smaller size, none of which his arithmetic could see. For what those unseen
whirls do with the motion, the book has four lines of verse: *big whirls have
little whirls that feed on their velocity, and little whirls have lesser whirls,
and so on to viscosity.*

The rhyme is a claim about where the energy of motion goes: a push at a large
size passes to smaller whirls, and smaller again, until the whirls are small
enough for viscosity to turn their motion into heat. The two budgets below set
that claim beside the alternative. Start with the same push at a large scale in
both. Above, energy stays at that scale until viscosity removes it. Below, it can
pass to successively smaller scales. Amber

The cascade picture became quantitative on the eve of the war. Kolmogorov's three short papers reached
the Doklady between the 28th of December, 1940 and the last day of April, 1941;
eight weeks after the third, the Wehrmacht crossed the border and the Academy was
evacuated east. The claim inside them: deep in the cascade, the statistics forget
how the stirring started. Coffee spoon or storm front, by the middle generations
the energy is shared out among eddy sizes by one rule — the −5/3 power law,
written against the eddies' wavenumber (the spectrum form cast that same year, in
the same Moscow school, by his student Obukhov).

For twenty years that exponent lived on paper. Then a Canadian team towed a
hot-film probe through Discovery Passage, a tidal channel in British Columbia
where the sea runs at up to fifteen knots and the Reynolds number reaches
280,000,000 — three orders of magnitude past any laboratory.
The eddies were larger than the 217-foot ship, which spent the runs being carried
about by the thing it was measuring. The 1962 spectra track −5/3 across decades of
wavenumber, supporting a prediction derived from dimensional reasoning.

While the physicists climbed into the cascade, a French mathematician asked the
question underneath all of it. The equation predicts and laboratories confirm,
but does a smooth flow, evolved by Navier–Stokes, stay smooth? In 1934 Jean
Leray proved the guarantee that could be proven: solutions exist forever if you
let them be rough. He named his objects *turbulent solutions*, and he built the
weak derivatives they needed years before the function spaces that now hold them
existed.

Olga Ladyzhenskaya — taught mathematics by her father, a schoolteacher the NKVD
shot in 1937, and barred for his sake from Leningrad University — later fenced off
the flat world: in two dimensions, the setting of our planar flow solvers,
smoothness holds, uniqueness holds, the theory is clean. Every difficulty lives in
the third dimension, where vortex lines can stretch. Whether three-dimensional
solutions always stay smooth is, as of this sentence, unknown. In May 2000 the
Clay Mathematics Institute named it one of seven Millennium Prize Problems, with
a bounty and a problem statement six pages long. The strongest recent evidence cuts the wrong
way for optimists: in 2016 Terence Tao showed that a slightly averaged cousin of
the equation *does* blow up in finite time. Whatever protects the real equation —
if anything does — is something subtler than anything yet written down.

## The Machine Age

In 1950, a team around Jule Charney and John von Neumann ran the first computer
weather forecast on ENIAC, and Richardson's plan became practical. The solvers
behind the figures above, built step by step in the
<Link to="../navier-stokes" relative="path">previous lesson</Link>, use numerical
methods developed over the following decades. After adding any outside pushes,
a solver has three jobs: carry motion with the flow, exchange momentum between
neighboring layers, and find the pressure that keeps volume balanced.

The staggered-grid method came from Francis Harlow's group at Los
Alamos in 1965. The projection step — advance the velocity freely, then solve for
the one pressure field that restores incompressibility — is Alexandre Chorin's,
1968. Jos Stam's 1999 [*Stable Fluids*](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/stam/reality/Research/pdf/ns.pdf) put semi-Lagrangian advection and pressure
projection into a practical graphics solver, published at a computer-graphics
conference. Backtracing and interpolation keep that advection step bounded even
at large time steps; they do not guarantee that a coarse calculation is accurate.
Even the compact notation came later. All five discoverers wrote three separate
scalar equations. The vector form shown here uses Gibbs–Heaviside notation,
developed about sixty years after the equation.

## Final Words

The name Navier–Stokes leaves out much of the work that made the equation useful:
Cauchy's stress tensor, Saint-Venant's derivation, the measurements in capillaries
and pipes, and Prandtl's account of the boundary layer. Their contributions changed
what could be calculated and what could be checked against real water.

We can now compute flows that none of them could have followed by hand. The
question of whether smooth three-dimensional solutions always remain smooth
is still open.
