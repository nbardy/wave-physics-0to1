# Building a Fluid Simulation

Below, a current carries two patches of dye along a set of paths, then sends every
point back along the same paths in reverse. With no diffusion along the way, the
dye returns to its original shape, however thin the strips it was stretched into
on the way out.

Two calculations make that trip. Both use the same prescribed current, six
shears that slide neighbouring rows and columns by different amounts, then the
same six undone in reverse order. Opposite edges of each square join, so dye leaving
one side enters the other. One calculation can evaluate the paths exactly. The
other stores dye on a grid. Even with no molecular
diffusion in either calculation, they disagree about what comes back. A fluid
simulation needs rules for moving numbers between grid points, and those rules
can change the flow we think we are seeing.

Choose **Start**, then **Stretched**, then **Returned**. The <C k="dye">amber</C> and
<C k="dye2">rose</C> patches recover their shapes on the left. On the right, much of
their colour remains spread around the square. The overlap bars count dye that
coincides with its starting pattern; 100% means a complete return. The grid
returns only about 16% overlap.

There is no hidden fading effect. The dye total under each square stays at 100%:
this grid update preserves the amount of dye to roundoff. It loses *where the dye
was*, while keeping how much there was.

The slider visits saved moments. The second half of the trip, from three to six
seconds, actually reverses the motion. Neither pane calculates what forces
produce the current. The velocity is given in both; only the dye is computed.

## What the Computer Keeps

At each location, the dye has a concentration $q$: zero for clear fluid, one for
the strongest initial colour. Its velocity has two components,
$\mathbf{u}=(u,v)$, telling us how fast fluid moves horizontally and vertically.
Both quantities vary over space and time. The grid replaces those continuous
fields with finite lists of numbers.

Here is a coarse view of the initial dye. Select a cell to read its amber and
rose concentrations. The dot at its centre marks where we sample the dye. The blue dots
on its edges show where we will store the velocity components that carry fluid
across those edges.

<CellStorage />

The cell boundary stays put when fluid moves through it. At the next update, a
different parcel may occupy the centre, so we need a new concentration there.
We store values at fixed places and never follow an individual parcel.

The arrangement of centre values and face velocities is called a *staggered grid*.
For the moment, take the velocity as given, as it was in the return experiment,
and find the concentration that arrives at a grid point during one timestep.

## Follow the Arrival Backward

Suppose the current carries dye to the right. The dye arriving at a grid point
came from somewhere to its left. Follow that motion backward by one timestep,
look up the old concentration there, and use it as the new value.

For a constant horizontal speed $u$, timestep $\Delta t$, and target position
$x_i$, this gives

$$q_i^{n+1}=q^n(x_i-u\Delta t).$$

The superscripts count updates, not powers. The departure point
$x_i-u\Delta t$ usually falls between stored samples. In the next figure, the
blue arrow runs from the selected arrival cell back to that point. Change the
travel distance and watch which old values contribute.

<BacktraceLab />

When the departure lies a fraction $a$ of the way from sample $j$ to sample
$j+1$, linear interpolation supplies the missing value:

$$q_i^{n+1}=(1-a)q_j^n+a q_{j+1}^n,\qquad 0\leq a\leq1.$$

A departure halfway between concentrations zero and one receives one half. At an
integer-cell displacement, no blending is needed: we can copy a stored value
directly. Between those positions, every new sample averages neighbouring values.

This procedure is *semi-Lagrangian advection*: follow the arriving parcel for one
step, then store its value back on the fixed grid. In two dimensions, a departure
point can lie between four samples. Bilinear interpolation weights those four
values by its position within their square.

The averaging is useful. Provided the old concentrations lie between zero and
one, positive weights that sum to one cannot produce a negative concentration or
one greater than the old maximum. The update remains bounded even if the parcel
crosses several cells in a step. That does not mean a large step follows a
complicated current accurately.

## An Update That Goes Wrong

We can also derive an update by looking at how concentration changes at a fixed
point. With constant speed and no diffusion, a profile simply translates:
$q(x,t)=q(x-ut,0)$. Differentiating gives the advection equation,

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial q}{\partial x}=0.$$

Replace the time derivative with a forward difference and estimate the spatial
slope from the samples on either side. With grid spacing $h$, the resulting rule
is

$$q_i^{n+1}=q_i^n-\frac{u\Delta t}{2h}\left(q_{i+1}^n-q_{i-1}^n\right).$$

This is a natural approximation: subtract the amount implied by the local slope.
It is also unstable. Compare it with backtracing below. Both start with the same
block of dye and move it 0.65 cells per update. The dashed curve marks the exact
translated block, sampled as cell averages so a partially occupied edge cell has
an intermediate value.

<TransportErrors />

After only a few updates, the local slope method produces negative concentrations
beside the block and concentrations above one. These are errors in the values,
not ripples in real dye. Continue the run and they grow; the printed range keeps
reporting their size after the curve leaves the plotting window.

The problem persists at smaller timesteps. Applied to a sinusoidal error, this
combination of forward time and centred space differences multiplies
its amplitude by a factor whose magnitude is
$\sqrt{1+(u\Delta t/h)^2\sin^2(kh)}$. For modes with a nonzero sine, that exceeds
one. Here $k$ is the error's spatial wavenumber. Reducing the timestep slows the
growth but does not make this scheme stable.

Backtracing keeps the concentration in range. Its edges gradually soften, however,
and its peak falls as the block spreads. We have exchanged an unstable update for
one whose error is quieter and much easier to mistake for fluid behaviour.

## Where the Pattern Went

Interpolation explains the failed return. Once a thin strip lies between grid
points, its samples no longer describe its full shape. Averaging spreads some of
its colour to its neighbours. Reversing the velocity makes another sequence of
averages; it does not reconstruct the earlier values from those averages.

Repeat the same trip with more samples. The controls below change the *simulation
grid*, while the displayed square, the prescribed current, the elapsed time, and
the number of updates stay fixed.

Before selecting $96\times96$, make an estimate: will four times as many samples
as $48\times48$ recover most of the original pattern?

<ReturnExperiment refine />

At **Returned**, overlap rises from about 9% on a $24\times24$ grid to 16% on
$48\times48$ and 38% on $96\times96$. At **Stretched**, the exact calculation
shows why recovery is difficult: some strips are already much thinner than a
coarse cell. Even the finest offered grid loses detail. Interpolating from more
than two neighbours, or measuring this backward error and correcting for it,
recovers more of the pattern; both refine the same update.

To measure overlap, we take the lesser of the starting and current concentrations
at each point, sum that shared dye for both colours, and divide by their starting
total. Dye in the wrong place cannot raise the score merely by becoming brighter.

Three checks now give different answers. The concentrations remain bounded, so
this update passes the stability check. The dye total stays at 100%, so this
test passes a conservation check. Yet the returned shape is wrong.
Neither of the first two checks establishes accuracy.

The conservation result has a specific reason here: each shear shifts every
sample in a row or column by the same amount, with opposite edges joined. The
interpolation weights redistribute its sum exactly. General semi-Lagrangian
transport in a varying velocity field does not have that guarantee.

Real dye also spreads through molecular diffusion. We left it out to isolate
the numerical error. Adding it would require another term in the dye equation;
blur from interpolation is already present before we do that.

## Where Velocity Comes From

So far we have supplied the motion. For a fluid simulation, we must calculate how
that motion changes. A parcel of fluid accelerates under forces, just as any
other piece of matter does. The complication is that our grid records velocity at
fixed places while the parcel moves between them.

Consider a steady current through a narrowing channel. Every blue arrow below
stays fixed in time. The amber parcel moves from a slower part of the current to
a faster part. A gray probe remains at one location. Scrub time to compare what
they measure.

<ParcelAcceleration />

The probe stays at 2.20 metres per second. The parcel's horizontal speed grows
from about 1.05 to 3.49 metres per second over the displayed trip. A steady field
can therefore contain accelerating fluid. This figure prescribes a convenient
incompressible narrowing flow; it does not solve the forces inside a real nozzle.

Following a parcel changes both its position and the time at which we evaluate
the field. The chain rule combines those changes:

$$\frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial y}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}.$$

The first term is the change at a fixed probe. The remaining terms account for
the parcel travelling into places with different velocities. Their compact name
is *advection of velocity*. The same backtracing operation that carried dye can
carry a velocity component; we now update the field that will determine the next
departure points.

That accounts for motion through a varying field. To determine the parcel's
acceleration, we still need its forces.

## Exchanging Momentum

Adjacent layers of a viscous fluid exert tangential forces on each other. A fast
layer pulls a slower neighbour along, while the slower layer holds the fast one
back. For a Newtonian fluid, the shear stress is proportional to the velocity
gradient between them.

For equally spaced horizontal layers, the net effect on one layer depends on how
its speed compares with the layers above and below:

$$\frac{du_j}{dt}=\nu\frac{u_{j-1}-2u_j+u_{j+1}}{h^2}.$$

Here $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity, with units of length squared per time. The
numerator is zero along a linear velocity profile: the pulls from the two sides
balance. A narrow peak has a negative value there and loses speed while its slower
neighbours gain it.

Increase viscosity, then switch between an isolated jet, layers, and stripes.
The gray outline preserves the starting profile.

<DiffusionLab readout="differences" />

The jet spreads momentum into neighbouring layers. The alternating stripes lose
their sharp velocity differences quickly. This experiment changes velocity,
shown by blue arrows; it does not blur a dye image. Its stationary outer walls can
also absorb momentum, so the fluid's total momentum need not remain constant.

In two dimensions, the corresponding second differences in both directions
approximate the Laplacian $\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$. Viscosity contributes an
acceleration $\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$. The explicit update above has its own
timestep restriction: in one dimension, $\nu\Delta t/h^2$ must be at most
$1/2$. The figure uses smaller steps within that limit. Bounded advection does not
remove a restriction imposed by a different operation.

## The Water Has to Fit

Our cells are already full of water. Under the incompressible approximation,
their fluid density remains constant. If more volume leaves a fixed cell than
enters it, the proposed velocities cannot describe that fluid.

Adjust the right-face outflow below until the cell balances. The other three
flows stay fixed. All numbers are volume per second; the cell has a volume of one
cubic metre.

<CellFlux pressureMark={false} />

The top and bottom flows cancel. The right face must therefore carry out the
same one cubic metre per second that enters from the left. Changing the dye
concentration inside the cell would not solve an imbalance in water volume.

For a square grid of spacing $h$, write the normal velocity components on the
right, left, top, and bottom faces as $u_R,u_L,v_T,v_B$. Dividing net outward
volume flow by cell volume gives the discrete divergence,

$$D\mathbf{u}=\frac{u_R-u_L}{h}+\frac{v_T-v_B}{h}.$$

Positive divergence means more volume would leave than enter; negative divergence
means the reverse. Incompressibility requires $D\mathbf{u}=0$ in every cell.
This is why storing normal velocities on faces is useful: a shared face supplies
one cell's outflow and its neighbour's inflow with the same number.

Balancing one cell by hand was easy. On a grid, changing its right-face velocity
changes the neighbouring cell's balance too. We need a correction that satisfies
all of those constraints together.

## Pressure as a Grid-Wide Correction

A pressure difference accelerates fluid from higher pressure toward lower
pressure. For constant density $\rho$, that acceleration is
$-\nabla p/\rho$. Suppose advection, viscosity, and an external push have already
produced proposed velocities $\mathbf{u}^{*}$. A pressure update over one
timestep would give

$$\mathbf{u}^{n+1}=\mathbf{u}^{*}-\frac{\Delta t}{\rho}G p.$$

$G$ is our discrete gradient: the pressure difference between neighbouring cell
centres divided by their spacing. Apply the divergence operator to this equation
and require the corrected velocities to balance:

$$D G p=\frac{\rho}{\Delta t}D\mathbf{u}^{*}.$$

The product $DG$ is a discrete Laplacian. This is the pressure Poisson equation.
The known right-hand side is the imbalance in the proposed flow; the unknowns are
pressures at all the cell centres. We solve for those pressures, then use their
differences to correct the shared face velocities.

The next figure supplies a deliberately unbalanced proposal. Blue arrows show
velocity and violet marks the size of the local volume imbalance. On the right,
cyan shows the low-pressure region needed to draw the outward-moving fluid back.
The slider scales the computed correction. Before trying 150%, predict whether it
will leave less imbalance than 100%.

<ProjectionLab />

At 100%, the meter reads 0.00%: the measured imbalance is below one billionth of
its starting value. At 150%, the same pressure pattern overcorrects and creates
imbalance again. The meter reports the
root-mean-square divergence across the cells relative to the original proposal;
it is not a measure of how closely the entire simulation matches real water.

These pressure experiments join opposite edges of the domain. Fluid leaving the
right re-enters on the left, and the top joins the bottom. There are no solid
walls to handle here. Walls would add boundary conditions to both the velocities
and the pressure solve.

We also have to decide how accurately to solve that coupled equation. One simple
method repeatedly updates each pressure from its neighbours and the local
divergence. A sweep visits all the cells. The next comparison shows the velocity
correction available after different numbers of Gauss–Seidel sweeps, always
starting with the same proposal and zero pressure guess.

<ProjectionLab iterations />

Early sweeps reduce the strong local imbalance; a weaker, broader error remains.
On this small grid, forty sweeps leave about 4% of the original root-mean-square
divergence. At 160 sweeps it is about 0.07%. There is no universal number of sweeps
that makes pressure correct: the required work depends on the grid and the input.

The final live solver below uses conjugate gradients instead, stopping when the
pressure residual is small relative to its starting value, with an iteration
limit. Its gradient, divergence, and pressure operator use the same discrete
differences. Solving one Laplacian accurately would not help if we corrected the
velocities with an incompatible gradient.

In the code, the stored pressure variable includes the factor $\Delta t/\rho$.
Its differences are therefore velocity corrections directly. The colours above
show relative pressure; they are not a scale in pascals.

## One Timestep

We can now assemble the equation for a two-dimensional, constant-density,
incompressible Newtonian fluid:

$$\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}=-\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{f},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0.$$

$\mathbf{f}$ denotes an external force per unit mass, such as a push. Dye obeys
its own transport equation,

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)q=0,$$

because we are treating it as a passive marker without molecular diffusion. It
does not change the density, exert a force, or determine the pressure.

The computer approximates one timestep by applying the operations separately.
Below, each button exposes an intermediate state from the same update. The input
on the left becomes the result on the right. Step through from the stored state
to carrying the dye; enlarge the velocity change when an individual operation
is too small to see clearly.

<SolverCycle />

The push changes velocity first. Backtracing then carries velocity, viscosity
smooths its spatial differences, and pressure corrects the resulting volume
imbalance. Finally the corrected velocity carries both dyes. A single timestep
is only $1/40$ of a second, so an accurate-looking step need not contain a dramatic
change. The optional magnification shows the *difference* in velocity, multiplied
by ten, rather than a faster version of the whole flow.

This separation of operations is called *operator splitting*. It approximates
effects that act together in the continuous equation. The order and timestep
therefore contribute error, alongside the grid spacing, interpolation, and
incomplete pressure solve.

Now leave one operation out. Both runs below
begin with the same state and push, and advance for the same amount of time.
The left includes all three velocity operations. On the right, select one to
omit. Dye transport remains enabled in every case.

<TermExperiment />

Without pressure correction, the arrows allow water to accumulate or disappear
locally; the violet view exposes that violation. Without viscosity, sharp
velocity differences persist longer. Without velocity advection, the velocity
field no longer carries its own components through space, although it still
transports dye. The moving colour alone can hide that last omission; the arrows
and the left pane show it.

Omitting the pressure solve does not make a compressible-flow solver: nothing
here evolves density or supplies an equation of state in place of the missing
constraint.

## Let the Grid Evolve

The final grid now computes its own velocity. Drag across it to push the fluid,
or use **Push upward** for a repeatable impulse. **Add dye** marks the current
without pushing it. Pause to inspect the arrows, then resume and follow the
colour they carry.

<LiveSolverLab />

Increase viscosity and small velocity differences smooth more quickly. The
control is measured in cell widths squared per second, with this grid fixed at
$48\times32$ cells. At zero viscosity, the interpolation error still softens
structures: zero physical viscosity does not give us an exact inviscid solver.
Opposite edges join here too, so this is a periodic patch of fluid, not water
inside a tank.

The simulation advances with a fixed physical timestep independent of the
screen's refresh rate. Each step backtraces velocity, diffuses it with stable
substeps, solves pressure, and transports dye. Pointer pushes receive a pressure
correction immediately, including while paused, so a new impulse respects the
volume constraint before the next step.

The imbalance readout in the corner can be tiny while the dye looks increasingly
washed out. That readout checks whether the face flows balance. The return
experiment checks how much spatial detail survives a known trip.

For a flow whose answer we do not know, repeating the calculation with smaller
cells and timesteps is part of finding out whether a feature belongs to the
fluid or to the method, and the comparison has to be made on the quantity in
question: a transported shape, a force on a surface, a mixing rate. Here that
quantity was two patches of dye. The averaging that washes out the dye in this
grid is the averaging that returned 16% of them at the top of the page.

## Further Reading

[Jos Stam's *Stable Fluids* (1999)](https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs468-05-fall/Papers/p121-stam.pdf)
develops the semi-Lagrangian approach that made stable interactive fluid animation
practical. The solver here shares that idea but uses its own staggered grid,
diffusion update, and pressure solve.

[Robert Bridson's fluid simulation course](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/fluidsimulation/)
provides notes on advection, pressure projection, boundary conditions, and the
numerical errors that remain after a simulation becomes stable.
