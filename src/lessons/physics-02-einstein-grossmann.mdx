# The Draft That Had to Fail

Everything you have ever dropped went straight down. A stone out of your hand
falls about five metres per second faster with every second —
$g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, the first number physics ever taught you, and the
most obedient one you own.

In August 1912 Albert Einstein walked across Zurich with the replacement for
that sentence finished in his head, and no mathematics to write it in. His old
classmate Marcel Grossmann, professor of mathematics, answered with sixty
years of geometry Einstein had never read.

Falling is going straight through curved geometry.

The figure below is yours to bend. Two pairs of lines, each launched
parallel. The upper pair holds its separation all the way across. The lower
pair does not. The slider bends the lower sheet and nothing else.

<StraightLines />

The upper pair is the geometry you were given in school. The lower pair is
the geometry you live in — drawn in the same ink, by the same hand, and just
as straight by its own rulers' account.

Two confessions before anything moves. First, the curved sheet stands in for
something one dimension richer than itself: the geometry of gravity is
spacetime curvature, and I am showing you spatial curvature, which can be
drawn. Time joins honestly at Mercury, where the numbers need it; until then
every claim here is about the shape of space. Second, the dramatis personae:
Grossmann is not the assistant in this story. He is the co-author — the 1913
paper signs Einstein for the physical part and Grossmann for the mathematical
part, and both signatures mean it.

## What "Straight" Means

A ruler is a contract. Laid on the flat sheet, every copy of it agrees with
every other: end to end they tile, parallels stay parallel, and a triangle's
three angles add to exactly 180°. Nothing about that is negotiable, and
nothing about it is interesting — until you lay the same rulers on the lower
sheet.

There the copies stop agreeing. Carried across the bend, a ruler comes back
stretched or turned against the one it started beside. Two lines drawn
"straight" — each short segment laid by the local ruler's own account — drift
toward each other and meet. A triangle drawn the same way overshoots 180°.
Drag the hero's slider back to flat and all of it unwinds: 180°, parallels,
agreement. The bend is the whole difference, and the slider is the whole
argument.

The field of rulers has a name, and the name is a compression: $g$, the
metric — "the ruler at every point, and how each one compares with its
neighbours." On the flat sheet $g$ is the same everywhere, which is why
school geometry works. On the lower sheet $g$ varies from place to place,
which is why the lines meet. Every formula this article earns will be a
sentence about $g$ first and a symbol second.

The path itself has a name too: a geodesic — going straight by the local
rulers' account, with no other meaning of "straight" admitted. On the flat
sheet a geodesic is what you always called a straight line. On the curved
sheet it is the amber curve that meets its neighbour. That is the only
definition in force from here on. Which sheet is the world — that is the
question the rest of the article spends.

## The Derivative Dies

Carry an arrow around a small loop on the flat sheet, keeping it parallel to
itself at every step, and it returns pointing where it started. Every child
with a compass walking a field knows this. Now carry it around a loop on the
protagonist.

<LoopMeter />

It comes back rotated. The loop encloses area, the patch has curvature, and
the arrow keeps the receipt: the rotation equals curvature times area. Make
the loop small and the rotation shrinks with it; shrink it to nothing and the
arrow returns itself — the flat verdict recovered as a boundary check, which
is how you know the curved behaviour is honest rather than drawn that way.

The right pane is the same fact wearing different clothes: a triangle whose
angles sum past 180° by exactly the rotation on the left. Two witnesses, one
quantity, and they agree at every setting of the knob because they are two
readings of the same geometry, not two assertions about it.

The verdict, stated flat: the ordinary derivative of a direction
is a fact about flat habits, not about the surface. "Keep pointing the same
way" has no meaning until someone says which rulers decide what "the same
way" is at the next point. On the flat sheet the answer was free — every
ruler agreed, so nobody had to choose. On the curved sheet the choice is the
whole subject.

In symbols, the ruler-field is $g$ — the compression from the previous
section, now load-bearing. A derivative that asks $g$ before comparing
neighbouring rulers is the covariant derivative; one that does not is the
unrepaired kind from the hero figure, reporting the bend in our habits as
if it were physics. Unfortunately, every law of motion ever written is
built from derivatives.

## The Repair

Give each point the rule its own rulers imply: to move a direction from here
to next door, consult $g$, correct for how the rulers change between the two
doors, and only then compare. That rule is the connection — built from the
rulers, not added beside them — and a path that never turns by its own
rulers' account is a geodesic. The amber curves from the hero were geodesics
all along; now they have their paperwork.

The law of free fall, words first: a body with nothing pushing it follows a
geodesic of $g$. The symbol $g$ already carries it, colour-bound — where $g$
is flat the geodesics are straight lines and the stone obeys
$g = 9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, school physics recovered rather than replaced.
Where $g$ curves, "straight" and "down" stop being two different ideas.

Rulers at every point, straightness by the rulers' account, a meter that

## The Draft

Two laws were written on this same geometry. Both drop the apple onto
Newton's track. The sky disagrees with one of them — with a number.

The human part takes three dated sentences. In August 1912 Einstein brings
Grossmann the physics — gravity is spacetime geometry, free bodies ride its
geodesics, Newton must fall out — and Grossmann answers with the mathematics:
Riemann's geometry and the absolute differential calculus of Ricci and
Levi-Civita, the machinery for writing laws about $g$. In 1913 they publish
the Outline together, the
Entwurf: Einstein responsible for the physical part, Grossmann for the
mathematical part, presented jointly on 9 September 1913. In June 1913
Einstein sits down with Michele Besso and runs the draft against the sky.

The draft's geometry is right. Its field law — the equation that says which
ruler-field the world's matter produces — is not. The law they chose works
only in a restricted family of coordinate systems: rotate the grid and the
draft's answer shifts, where a physical answer should sit still. The figure
is a toy with the same symmetry property, not the equations themselves:
the <C k="gr1915">green bar</C> is a rotation-proof operator and holds its
value at every grid angle, while the
<C k="entwurf">red bar</C> is a grid-aligned operator that answers fully on
its home grid and dies to nothing at ninety degrees. Both laws agree where
the draft was built to agree. Everywhere else the draft reports the grid.

<CovarianceMachine />

"Works in every coordinate system" sounds like bookkeeping until a law fails
it in public. Covariance is not notation. It is the demand that the answer
belong to the world rather than to the surveyor — and the draft visibly
answers the surveyor.

What the draft did honour was the fall. Newton, the draft, and the 1915 law
all drop the same balls down the same shaft and land them together, at every
height the knob offers. Watch all three land. The simultaneity closes an
escape route: had the draft merely flunked falling, its story would be a
footnote about a wrong guess. It passed every test anyone could run on Earth.
The next test is Mercury's orbit.

## Mercury Grades Them

Mercury's orbit does not close. Each perihelion — each closest approach to
the Sun — arrives a little ahead of where Newton puts it, and the arrears
accumulate: 43 seconds of arc per century, measured first by Le Verrier in
1859, a remainder Newton never paid. The draft and the final law disagree
about the size of that remainder, and the disagreement is quantitative —
down to the second of arc.

Three lanes, one integrator, one difference: the gray lane is Newton, and it
closes — its fan never opens, which is the integrator proving its own
honesty. The <C k="gr1915">green lane</C> carries the November 1915
correction and opens about a degree per orbit in this rescaled strength. The
<C k="entwurf">red lane</C> carries the draft's correction and opens a
smaller fan at every eccentricity the knob offers. The measured ratio sits
in the meter: 0.42, against history's 18/43 = 0.42.

That is the verdict, and it was already on paper in June 1913. Einstein and
Besso solved the draft's equations for the Sun's field and computed
Mercury's advance: about 18 seconds of arc per century against the observed
43. He kept the result quiet and kept working on the draft for two more
years. The wrong theory graded itself, quantitatively, before the right one
existed.

Strengths and time here are rescaled so the fan opens visibly — the true
correction would need some thirty thousand silent orbits to pry the lanes
this far apart, and no reader should watch that. What the rescaling preserves
is the ratio, and the ratio is the claim: the draft earns less than half the
observed advance at every eccentricity, and no choice of orbit repairs it.
The shape was right. The law was wrong. Mercury knew.

## November

Four weeks, four communiques to the Prussian Academy: the 4th, the 11th, the
18th, and the 25th of November 1915. On the 18th Einstein shows the new field
equations give 43 seconds of arc per century — the full remainder, with the
techniques built beside Besso in 1913 doing the work. The tools of the draft
closed the case against it.

The hero figure, revisited: the same two pairs of lines. The upper pair is
still the geometry you were given. The lower pair reads now — those meeting
lines are geodesics of $g$, and a falling stone is one of them, going
straight by the only rulers the world offers.

<StraightLines />

No law in this article was chosen for its elegance. The draft was kept for
two years because it seemed to protect energy and the Newtonian limit, and
it was dropped when it failed the sky. That is the whole method, and it is
why the friend belongs in the title: the physics saw the shape, the
mathematics built the object, the planet graded the law, and none of the
three could have done the others' job.

A stone leaves your hand. Nothing pulls it and nothing pushes it — it
follows the straightest path the rulers allow, through a world curved to
meet it.

## Further Reading

Michel Janssen and Jürgen Renn, [*Einstein and the Perihelion Motion of
Mercury*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.11238v1.pdf) — the manuscript trail
page by page: the 1913 Einstein–Besso calculations that gave 18 seconds,
the November 1915 papers that gave 43. The place to watch a wrong theory
convict itself in primary sources.

The Zurich Notebook itself, summer 1912 to spring 1913, in facsimile at the
Einstein Archives Online (search "Zurich Notebook"): the pages where
equations near the final ones were considered and set aside, in Einstein's
own hand with Grossmann's mathematics arriving mid-stream. The lone-genius
telling goes to die here.

Galina Weinstein, [*Einstein, Schwarzschild, the Perihelion Motion of
Mercury and the Rotating Disk
Story*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.7370) — the November weeks in their
original order, the four communiques and the rotating-disk reasoning that
ran alongside them.

Einstein and Grossmann, *Entwurf einer verallgemeinerten
Relativitätstheorie und einer Theorie der Gravitation*, 1913 — the Outline,
physical part and mathematical part, worth meeting in translation at least
once, if only to see the signatures side by side.
