# Compiling Into Heat

The smallest stochastic program worth compiling is a walk. Five nodes in a
ring, a token hopping between them once per step: from four of the nodes it
drifts clockwise more often than not, and at the fifth it sticks, staying put
seven times out of ten. Three probabilities per node and nothing else — a
program you could run with dice. The compiler paper's first demonstration is
the same kind of object — a biased walk on a 5×5 grid, compiled gate by
gate; this ring is that demonstration shrunk until Part 1's oracle — exact
enumeration, gray ghosts, numbers with no error bars — holds every quantity
in sight.

Below, the walk twice. On the left it runs *as written*: the token obeys its
three probabilities directly, and the histogram under it counts where the
token has been. On the right, the same walk after the treatment Part 1 ended
with — each step re-expressed as a thermodynamic kernel, clamped, sampled,
read out — running on a patch wired with the actual chip's connection rules.
The <C k="ghost">gray outlines</C> are the exact one-step law; both sets of
<C k="meter">violet</C> bars settle toward them.

<WalkHero />

By the histograms, the compilation worked. The quiet strip along the bottom
disagrees: there the same kernel is chained thirty steps deep, each output
fed back as the next input — which is all that running the program means —
and it prints two numbers the histograms cannot see: a distance from the
walk's law that exceeds any single step's error, and nearly a fifth of all
occupancy pooled in a <C k="ferro">red</C> bucket, states that mean no node
at all. One-step agreement is the cheapest promise a compiler makes, and
the matching histograms check no other. The lie has a name the papers
coined for it, an instrument that catches it, a repair whose price got
measured, and a floor below which no repair reaches — and this walk is
small enough to keep every one of those claims in exact numbers.

## Four moves and a checkerboard

Part 1 owed one debt to honesty that this article can now pay. Its fabric
drawing was a confessed stand-in — degree sixteen, sparse, two-colorable,
wiring invented. The real wiring is public, and it is stranger than the
stand-in: every cell on the Z1 die follows the same four connection rules,

$$(a,b)\in\{(1,0),\;(2,1),\;(2,3),\;(4,1)\}$$

each rule contributing its offset and that offset's three 90° rotations —
four wires per rule, sixteen per cell. Pick cells below and watch the same
sixteen moves redraw around each one:

<Neighborhood />

The shape never varies: one rook-step, then three progressively stranger
knight-moves, the longest reaching $\sqrt{17} \approx 4.1$ cells away. This
is not a grid with trimmings — most of a cell's sixteen neighbors are not
adjacent to it, and two cells sitting side by side on the die often share no
wire at all. The edges are where drawings of this fabric have to pick a
lie. The figure above picks the die's own: its border cells simply run
short — count the corner's wires and eleven fall off the edge — which is
what "mostly regular," the papers' own phrase, means in practice. The
larger patches later in this article pick the other lie and wrap,
torus-style, so that every cell keeps its full sixteen; wherever a wrapped
patch appears, that idealization rides with it.

Four strange moves, chosen from the infinity of possible offset sets. The
choice hides a fact you can test. Add each rule's coordinates: $1{+}0$,
$2{+}1$, $2{+}3$, $4{+}1$ — odd, every one, and a 90° rotation only swaps
and negates coordinates, so all sixteen moves are odd. An odd move on a
checkerboard always lands on the other color. Every wire in the fabric joins
<C k="sUp">amber</C> to <C k="sDn">blue</C> — the graph is bipartite by
construction, and Part 1's red/black rescue runs on it natively. The figure
below lets you break this on purpose: swap one rule for an even move and
watch what the checkerboard loses.

<ParityToggle />

With any even rule in the set, wires appear joining like color to like — and
every such wire is a write conflict waiting for the sweep, the exact crime
the freeze-frame counted in Part 1. The four published moves are not four
random eccentricities. Odd parity *is* the design: reach scattered widely
across the plane, purchased without surrendering the one property that makes
a massively parallel sweep exact.

Bipartite is a stronger word than it looks, and the next figure shows what
it buys. Choose a few cells of the fabric to be the ones you actually read —
three, say — and re-hang the whole graph from them: every cell placed in a
column by its wiring distance to the nearest readable cell.

<Z1Layers />

Every wire in the re-hung graph spans two adjacent columns. None stays
inside a column — a same-column wire would close an odd loop, and the
parity fact forbids odd loops outright. What hangs there is a *layered*
machine: readable cells, then a bank one wire away, then a bank two wires
away, each bank speaking only to its neighbors — the deep, hidden-layered
Boltzmann machines of Part 1's training pages, emerging from a flat die the
moment you decide which cells count as visible. Re-roll the choice and the
layers reshuffle; the graph never changes. The figure draws its readable
cells from a single checkerboard color, and that choice is what guarantees
no wire hides inside a column — mix the colors and two visible neighbors
would share column zero, wire and all.

One last fact about the wires, and it is the strangest on the die. Each edge
carries *two* programmable couplings, one per direction: what cell $i$
whispers to cell $j$ and what $j$ whispers back are separate numbers.
Everything Part 1 proved — the energy ladder, the Boltzmann law, the
gray ghost itself — quietly assumed they agree. Below, a small loop of
cells with a knob that splits the two directions apart, and a meter reading
the net probability current circulating around the loop:

<AsymmetricLoop />

Symmetric, the flux meter idles at zero: the machine detailed-balances, and
every trip around the loop is paid back in full. Split the couplings and the
meter wakes — probability *circulates*, steadily, forever, which no
equilibrium allows. There is no landscape under this machine anymore: no
scalar energy exists whose conditional slices generate an asymmetric sweep,
so the entire apparatus of Part 1 — cost, weight, $Z$, the ghost — has
nothing to grip. A stationary law still exists; it just has no closed form,
and the papers' own verdict on learning in this regime is blunt: it appears
intractable, and their document — like this one, from here on — restricts
itself to the symmetric case. The second coupling stays on the die, a door
the theory cannot yet walk through.

## Clamp, marginalize, normalize

Part 1 built the thermodynamic kernel with hands: clamp the input, let the
free spins thermalize, read the output, ignore the help. The atlas said why
that works — clamping erases the columns that disagree and renormalizes the
rest; marginalizing sums the survivors that differ only in spins you don't
read. What the sequel needs is the same object with its roles made
permanent. Every spin in a compiled kernel is conscripted into exactly one
of three populations: inputs $V_{\text{in}}$, <C k="held">held</C> for the
whole transaction; hidden spins $V_{\text{hid}}$, free and never read;
outputs $V_{\text{out}}$, free and read. One energy, three jobs:

<PartitionFigure />

Written down, the kernel the hardware serves is

$$P^F(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z^F(x)} \sum_{w} e^{-E(x,w,y)}, \qquad Z^F(x) \;=\; \sum_{y',\,w} e^{-E(x,w,y')}$$

— the same formula the copy-gate page collapsed to a $\sigma$ when the
hidden sum had one term, now carrying its full anatomy. Check it at the
boundary as always: strip $V_{\text{hid}}$ empty and the sum over $w$
vanishes, leaving the clamped softmax Part 1 fit by hand. The detail worth
holding is the argument of $Z$. This is not the one partition function of
the atlas; it is a *family* of them, one per input — clamp a different $x$
and the whole normalization is re-run in a different room. A per-input
normalizer will matter twice — later as the loophole a compiler never
fixes, and first, right now, as the thing that decides which inputs a fit
even cares about.

## The context tax

Compile one step of the walk. The token's position becomes five p-bits,
one-hot: the occupied node's spin <C k="sUp">up</C>, the other four
<C k="sDn">down</C>. Five inputs, five outputs, three hidden spins between
them, and the fit runs exactly — every kernel and every gradient on this
page enumerated, nothing sampled, Part 1's luxury still paying out.

The first trouble is visible before any training starts. The energy model
normalizes over all $2^5 = 32$ output configurations — but only five of
them *mean* anything. Twenty-seven configurations mark zero tokens or two;
the program cannot say them; the kernel can. With no hidden spins the
output bits are conditionally independent and mass *must* smear onto the
invalid states — Part 1's factorized failure, recurring one level up the
stack. Hidden spins buy concentration back, never all of it. The papers
have a name for what leaks out: **conservation leakage** — the compiled
gates assign small nonzero probability to states outside the one-token
world, and over many chained steps the trajectory accumulates real
occupancy there.

Small per-step, real at depth: catching that combination is a two-pane job,
and the instrument for it sits on every compiled figure from here to the
end of the series. The **split-meter**. Left pane: per-step KL, one bar per
input context — how wrong each row of the compiled table is. Right pane:
trajectory occupancy at depth $T$ — where the chained kernel actually
lives, <C k="ghost">exact law outlined</C>, compiled chain in
<C k="meter">violet</C>, and the leaked mass collected in its own
<C k="ferro">red</C> bucket at the edge. Two panes because the two numbers can disagree — a
kernel can hold every row of its table close while its chain drifts, and
the reverse is coming — and neither pane alone is the truth.

Here is the instrument by itself, on a throwaway kernel, with one knob:
the depth the right pane is asked about.

<SplitMeterTeach />

Drag the knob end to end. The left pane never moves — per-step error does
not mention depth anywhere in its definition — while the right pane climbs
with every step the kernel is chained, the <C k="ferro">red</C> bucket
filling as leaked mass compounds. The two panes ask different questions,
and only one of them can hear the word "deep."

One pane wears a color of its own. The warm
wash behind the per-step bars is the *visitation glow*: how often the
upstream program actually shows the kernel each input. The walk's glow is
lopsided — the sticky node hoards attention — and the fit below starts by
ignoring that entirely, training all five contexts with equal weight. The
loss being descended is a weighted sum of the per-context errors,

$$\mathcal{L} \;=\; \sum_{x} q(x)\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(K(\cdot \mid x)\,\big\|\,\tilde K_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\right)$$

and the whole question of this section is what to put for $q$. Before the
figure, a handicap to declare: this kernel's input wires are deliberately
poor — tied around the ring, five shared numbers instead of twenty-five
private ones. With a private wire for every context, each row of the table
fits alone, and weighting has nothing to reallocate; the tie is a stand-in
for the real scarcity, a placed kernel owning only the wires the fabric
routes. Every number that follows belongs to this tied kernel.

Under uniform weighting the fit converges and the two panes tell different
stories: the per-step bars settle at modest heights, while at $T = 30$ the red
bucket holds roughly two-thirds of all occupancy — the chain has mostly
leaked off the graph, and trajectory TV sits near 0.66. Toggle to the
walk's own visitation and the same capacity refits from scratch: the leaked
bucket falls to about 0.19, trajectory TV to about 0.49 — and the
cold contexts' bars did not rise. No price appeared. That is a measured
fact about this kernel, not a law: weighting helps where it counts, and
here it cost the cold contexts nothing, because the tied wires spill
whatever the hot contexts learn. Make capacity scarcer and a genuine trade
emerges; this model is not yet poor enough to show one.

The papers carry the weighting question as one inequality, and its scariest
inhabitant is worth meeting in the open. Train the kernel under some
distribution $\mu$, deploy it under the program's actual $q$, and the
deployed error inherits an inflation factor

$$c \;=\; \max_x \frac{q(x)}{\mu(x)}$$

— which is exactly as frightening as a worst-case ratio and no more: it is
the price of the single input your training weighted least, relative to how
often the program sends it. Train uniform over $n$ contexts and $c$ is just
$n$ times the hottest context's share. Match $\mu$ to $q$ and $c = 1$: the
inflation is gone by definition, which is the entire argument for
context-matched training, made in one line. The bound also confesses its
own pessimism — it charges every step the worst context's rate, so it grows
linearly in program depth even when the truth, as the next figure shows,
does not.

The full repair is a ladder, kindred to the papers' own pipeline and run at
our scale — their middle rung weights by the visitation the *model* itself
generates, where ours uses the target walk's: fit under uniform weighting,
refit under the program's visitation,
then a third stage that abandons per-step fitting altogether and trains on
the trajectory itself — REINFORCE, nudging parameters by reward times a
centered score. The third stage is operationally *cheaper* than the
training Part 1 did: contrastive divergence needed two clamping patterns,
data phase and dream phase; REINFORCE needs only the dream — inputs held,
everything else free. What it costs instead is variance: a score-function
gradient only discriminates once the model's samples already reach the
region the reward cares about, which is why it rides last, handed a good
model by the two stages before it.

<WalkLadder />

The stairs land at 0.662, 0.491, 0.144 — trajectory TV at thirty steps,
cut to under a quarter of where uniform fitting left it. The papers' grid
walk steps 5.64 to 0.30 to 0.08 through its own three stages; the shape is
the reproduction, the numbers are ours. Now read the split-meter's left
pane across stage three, because it is moving the wrong way: per-step KL
*rises* from 0.414 to 1.917 while the trajectory error collapses. That is
not a glitch in the figure; it is the deal. REINFORCE does not improve the
kernel's table — it warps the table, deliberately, so that each step's bias
cancels the accumulated drift of the steps before it. A one-pane instrument would call
stage three a regression or a triumph depending on which pane it kept;
keeping both is why the split-meter exists.

## The floor

The hero's strip put a number on error at depth; the ladder priced its
repair; one more fact makes the picture honest, and it is good news with a
boundary. Chained error does not accumulate forever. Each step of the
compiled chain *contracts*: feed it two different distributions and it
returns two less-different ones, closer by a factor $\rho < 1$. Old errors
fade while fresh ones arrive, and the contest settles at a depth-independent
ceiling:

$$\tilde\delta \;\le\; \frac{\bar\varepsilon}{1-\rho}$$

with $\bar\varepsilon$ the worst single-step error and $\rho$ the chain's
contraction rate — both measurable from the compiled chain itself, no
oracle required. Two honesty notes about $\rho$ before the figure prints
one. The papers state this ceiling against the *ideal* chain's contraction
$\rho_0$; the figure below measures the compiled chain's own. And what it
measures is the asymptotic rate — the factor the chain's slowest surviving
mode shrinks by per step — not a guarantee on any single step, so the
ceiling is motivated arithmetic, not a theorem. Geometric decay against
steady injection; the ceiling is the geometric series, summed.

<WalkFloor />

With two hidden spins the arithmetic behaves like a promise: measured
$\bar\varepsilon = 0.177$, measured $\rho = 0.753$, ceiling 0.718 drawn
as the line the curve is not expected to cross — and the
curve saturates far under it, flattening at 0.305, a little over
four-tenths of the bound, and flat it stays: over the last twenty steps of
depth it moves by parts in a hundred thousand. Their large construction
rides its bound the same way, at about six-tenths. Now take
the hidden spins away. Per-step error swells until
$\bar\varepsilon/(1-\rho)$ passes 1 — a bound on a probability distance
that exceeds its maximum bounds nothing — and the figure says so in place
of drawing the line. The curve still saturates, near 0.77, because
saturation belongs to the chain; the *guarantee* belonged to the capacity,
and it starved with the hidden spins. So the accumulation story ends in
full: trajectory error grows, then stops growing, and where it stops is
computable from two numbers — provided the kernel was good enough for the
arithmetic to say anything at all. A shadow rides along, though: train a
kernel longer and sharper and its measured $\rho$ creeps upward —
contraction is mixing wearing other clothes, and sharpness taxes it. That
account gets opened properly when the couplings themselves come under the
meter.

## What the forward law cannot see

Everything so far audited how well the compiled forward law was fit. This
section is about what the forward law does not even witness. Take a
compiled kernel with energy $E_A$ and build a second one by adding a
per-input offset: $E_B(x,w,y) = E_A(x,w,y) + \lambda(x)$, any function of
the input at all. Given a clamped $x$, $e^{-\lambda(x)}$ is one constant
shared by every $(w,y)$ — and §3's private normalizer collects it:

$$\tilde K_B(y \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\sum_w e^{-E_A(x,w,y)-\lambda(x)}}{\sum_{y',w} e^{-E_A(x,w,y')-\lambda(x)}} \;=\; \tilde K_A(y \mid x)$$

The constant rides both the sum and its normalizer and divides itself out —
exactly, at any $\lambda$, for any energy. The figure computes it rather
than assuming it: $\lambda$ enters every energy evaluation below, and the
forward panes refuse to move anyway.

<LambdaShift />

Drag $\lambda$ to its limit. The forward row holds model B's outline
flush against model A's fill, sixteen bars, and prints its verdict to six
digits: max TV 0.000000, at every setting of the slider. The backward row
runs the same two models the other direction — inferring, for each output,
the input that produced it — and there the difference pane burns brighter the
harder you drag, because the *joint* law never forgot: $\lambda$ reweights
how much of the model's mass sits at each input, which is invisible when
$x$ is clamped and decisive when $x$ is inferred. Boundary check both
claims at the slider's other end: at $\lambda = 0$ the models are
identical and the backward difference dies too; the forward number never
had anything to lose.

The consequence is a fact about compilation itself, stated flatly: fitting
a forward kernel does not determine an energy. It determines an
*equivalence class* of energies — one member per choice of $\lambda$ — and
every training run on this page landed on some member without being
asked. For a single kernel run forward, the choice is pure convention. The
moment compiled kernels are tiled together, or anything runs in reverse —
inference, backward passes, the joint sampled whole — the convention is
load-bearing, and a compiler that never pinned it down has left a free
parameter in the machine.

## The mixing tax

Everything the compiler has bought so far, it bought with couplings. Hidden
spins wired strongly into the output to do their work; the ladder's last
rung shaped the landscape harder still; and back at the floor figure a number
moved in a direction worth remembering — training the kernels sharper raised
the chain's contraction. Strong couplings are how an energy model says
anything definite. They are also, it turns out, how a sampler stops moving.

The whole mechanism fits in two spins. Wire them to agree with strength $J$
and the two states they love — both up, both down — sit in valleys separated
by a barrier of height $2J$. To cross, the thermal flicker must land a move
against a field of that size:

$$E(s_1, s_2) = -J\, s_1 s_2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P_{\text{cross}} \propto e^{-2J}, \qquad \tau \sim e^{\,2J}$$

At $J = 0$ the coins are free and every sweep is a fresh draw. Every unit of
coupling multiplies the waiting time by $e^2$. The definiteness and the
waiting are the same wires — a model cannot be given strong opinions without
also being given long silences between changes of mind.

The figure below stages the trade as one knob. A four-spin model is fit to a
fixed, strongly-coupled target — the fit is real gradient descent, and after
every step the couplings are clipped to a cap $J_{\max}$, which is the knob.
Three readouts, all measured, none sketched: <C k="meter">KL to the
target</C>, exact by enumeration on both sides; the <C k="ferro">
autocorrelation time τ</C>, read from an actual forty-thousand-sweep Gibbs run
on the fitted model; and <C k="anti">effective samples per sweep</C>, which is
just $1/\tau$ — what one sweep of work now buys in evidence.

The fit pane earns its perfection: KL falls from 1.119 at the tight end to

τ climbs from 1.7 sweeps to 194, and the effective yield collapses from 0.589

it hands over one independent draw per two hundred sweeps. Part 1 ended its
schedule fight with a gate: throughput means nothing until the distance
passes. Here is the gate's other jaw. This sampler passes the distance and
fails the throughput: correct, and nearly motionless.

The trade bites twice in a compiled kernel's life: it biases the truncated
dream phases during fitting, and it inflates the sweeps each sample costs
at deployment.

But one class of spin is exempt, and it is the class the compiler leans on
hardest. A clamped spin never flips, so no barrier of its making ever has to be *crossed* — it shifts
its neighbors' fields and creates no valley among them. Clamped spins do not
enter this tradeoff at all: a kernel's inputs may be wired to the free spins
as strongly as the hardware allows, at no cost in mixing time. Read the
compiled walk kernels again with that exemption in mind — their
most violent couplings hang off the clamped inputs, where violence is free,
and the tax falls only on the hidden and output spins that actually flicker.
The partition into roles was never bookkeeping. It is where the mixing tax
gets assessed.

## The bill

Two taxes now carry measured numbers — what honest context weighting costs,
and what stiff couplings cost in sweeps. The chip itself charges in a
third currency, and it is the natural one: Gibbs iterations, the thing the
fabric does natively, millions of times a second. Everything else the machine
can do is billed as a multiple of them.

The rates come from the papers, and they are lopsided. Reading the state out
costs about as much *energy* as $10^2$–$10^3$ iterations — a ratio of
energies, not a waiting time. Flashing new couplings onto the fabric costs
about 91 times a readout, node for node. And holding a cell — the clamp every
kernel is built on — is priced like flashing one. The strip below runs a live
patch of the real fabric and bills your choices at those rates: sweeps accrue
at one iteration each, the buttons charge readout at 300 and reflash at
27,300, and a rate line watches their one stated operating limit — the chip's
speed and energy advantage holds only while re-flashing stays below about
once per second — turning <C k="ferro">red</C> when you cross it.

<CostStrip />

The left pane is the walk kernel from the ladder, placed. Five
<C k="held">input</C> cells, three <C k="hid">hidden</C>, five
<C k="meter">output</C> — thirteen logical spins — and every logical coupling
routed through the fabric as a shortest path, the interior cells of each route
shaded <C k="ferro">warm</C>. Those are chain spins, Part 1's triangle repair
recurring at kernel scale, and the counter totals them: 13 logical spins cost
51 physical cells, 3.92 per logical. That number is a floor — overlapping
routes were counted once, and a placement that must keep routes apart pays
more. The patch of fabric a compiled kernel occupies this way has a name in
the papers — a Thermodynamic Sampling Unit — and the name draws the honest
boundary: the unit of hardware is not a p-bit but a placed, routed, clamped
region.

Run the strip for a while before touching anything. The bill climbs by one
per sweep — and the right-hand column reminds you that nothing has been
learned yet, because the state runs unobserved. Sampling is the cheap part;
*knowing what was sampled* is what costs. Press read-out and five spins
arrive, billed at three hundred sweeps' worth of energy. Press reflash and the
bill jumps by 27,300 — swapping which kernel lives on the patch
costs as much energy as an entire long sampling run. The strip converts the
total to joules in its last line at the papers' own estimate of roughly
$3\times10^{-10}$ joules per iteration, and the word *estimate* is
load-bearing: it is a projection, not a measurement, and the strip charges in
iterations first for exactly that reason.

The shape of the bill is the design constraint the rest of the stack lives
under. A program that holds one landscape and sweeps it for thousands of
iterations runs at the advertised advantage. A program that wants a fresh
kernel every few milliseconds — reflash, clamp, read, repeat — pays flash
prices at flash frequencies, and the advantage is gone.

<Waypoint>

  fabric: an embedding tax of 3.92 physical spins per logical one — a floor,

  on purpose — on a model the chip cannot even write down.

## Sampling the impossible

Here is the target: twelve spins, a field on each, eighteen pairwise couplings
drawn from the 66 possible pairs, and twenty *three-body* terms — triples
whose energy contribution is a product $x_n x_m x_{m'}$ — with all magnitudes
drawn from $\mathcal N(0, 0.6^2)$. Twelve spins keep the oracle alive: 4,096
states, every figure in this act audited against the exact law. Lay the
target's wiring over the die:

<MetaImpossible />

Re-roll the placement a few times and watch what changes and what cannot. The
pairwise wires are a lottery — a few land <C k="held">green</C> on native
fabric edges, the rest hang <C k="ferro">red</C> with no direct wire at any
placement, waiting for routed chains. That is the familiar impossibility, the
embedding tax again. The three-body row is the new one: zero of twenty, at
every roll, and re-rolling does not budge it, because it is not a placement
problem. A pairwise Ising energy has no product-of-three term at *any*
distance — and this fabric is bipartite by construction, so it does not even
contain a triangle to hang one on. The first impossibility costs spins. The
second is categorical: the fabric cannot say $x_n x_m x_{m'}$ at all.

The papers' answer is a change of object. Do not compile the model — compile
the *sampler*. A Gibbs sweep of the target is twelve single-spin updates, and
each update is something this article already knows intimately: a conditional
kernel, one row per context — the same species of table the walk's steps
were compiled into. Site $n$'s update draws from an S-curve on the logit

$$\theta_n(x) = W_n + \tfrac{1}{2}\textstyle\sum W_{mn}\, x_m + \tfrac{1}{3!}\textstyle\sum W^{(3)}_{nmm'}\, x_m x_{m'}$$

and there is the obstruction in its purest form: a *product* $x_m x_{m'}$
sitting inside a logit that the substrate must realize with pairwise wires,
which natively supply only the constant and the linear terms. What buys the
product is the machine's oldest trick, pushed one step further. In Part 1, one
hidden spin bought XOR — a function no weighted vote could express. Here, one
hidden spin buys one product term, and this time the mechanism has a closed
form. Give a hidden spin coupling $\alpha_a$ from the inputs and $\beta_a$ to
the output, and marginalizing it adds to the logit

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\,\mathrm{sp}\!\left(-2(\alpha_a^{\mathsf T}x - \beta_a)\right) - \mathrm{sp}\!\left(-2(\alpha_a^{\mathsf T}x + \beta_a)\right)\right], \qquad \mathrm{sp}(z) = \ln(1 + e^z)$$

— the difference of two softplus ramps. Kill $\beta_a$ and the two ramps
coincide and the contribution vanishes identically: a hidden spin that does
not talk to the output does nothing, as it should. One subtlety the formula's
notation hides, and our own derivation had to find the hard way: the
difference of ramps is a constant plus an *odd* function of its input field,
and an odd function of a purely linear projection contains no even term — its
$x_m x_{m'}$ content is identically zero for every choice of $\alpha$. The
hidden spin must carry a bias of its own into that field to break the
symmetry. With the bias in place and the projection aligned to the pair, the
gate's value on the four input corners decomposes exactly — not in a limit,
as an identity — into a constant, a linear part, and a product:

$$\text{gate} = A + B\,(x_m + x_{m'}) + C\, x_m x_{m'}$$

The constant and the linear part are native Ising vocabulary — the compiler
cancels them with programmed field and coupling offsets, furniture the fabric
already owns — and what remains is $C\, x_m x_{m'}$: one hidden spin *is* a
product term. The figure below builds the gate for this model's largest
three-body coefficient. The knob is the coupling magnitude.

Now compile all twelve updates. Each site's kernel gets one soft-product spin
per three-body term touching it — sixty hidden spins across the twelve
kernels, up to eight in one kernel — and the compilation, like the papers'
own experiment, is over a fully connected spin set with the coupling cap
$J_{\max}$ as the single knob. Before the chain runs, the claim's boundary:
the papers state that the connectivity residual — the extra cost of forcing
this onto Z1's actual graph — is not simulated in that experiment, so no
number below prices it either. What is measured is the cap, alone.

percent. Compilation error
does not compound with depth. Each sweep's small infidelity is partly erased
by the contraction of the next, and the papers bound the balance point in one
line:

$$\tilde\delta_t \;\le\; \frac{\bar\varepsilon}{1 - \rho_0}$$

worst per-step error over one minus the ideal chain's contraction — a

target's own couplings make its Gibbs dynamics slow. The knob owns only the
violet curve. Tighten the cap and the floor climbs — not because the chain
mixes worse, since the contraction belongs to the target, but because the cap
clips ever sharper logits and the per-step residual $\bar\varepsilon$ rises.
Loosen it from 2.5 to 4.5 and the floor collapses from 0.0385 to about
$1.4\times10^{-5}$ — nearly three orders of magnitude. The cap
buys fidelity; the target's own physics sets the pace; and the compiled
sampler is now doing something worth saying plainly: sampling, faithfully,
from a twenty-term three-body law that pairwise couplings cannot express —
because what was compiled was never the model. It was the moves.

## Further Reading

The Thermalizers paper (arXiv:2608.01615) is this article at full strength:
the three-role kernel, the inflation factor, REINFORCE as the last rung,
the depth-independent floor, and the meta-EBM are all its §III–§IV, and its
habit of riding a measured number next to every bound is the habit these
pages borrowed. Its demonstrations are chosen so that honesty degrades
gracefully — an enumerable walk, then an ecosystem, then a market no oracle
can audit — and the walk's ladder is reported with the same candor about
what each rung costs.

The Torx paper (arXiv:2608.01612) owns the other half of the thesis: what a
stochastic program *is* before anything compiles it. It carries the gate
language Part 1 taught further than either lesson needed — three distinct
ways of differentiating through a stochastic circuit, and a walk of its own,
built from the physics side: a continuous-time chain Trotterized into
gates, the graph's edge coloring deciding which gates may share a layer.
The biased, sticky variant this article ran is the compiler paper's demo,
compiled from a gate in the torx library. Read the estimator section beside
the ladder's third rung; the centered score REINFORCE rides here is the
first of its three.

Both halves run in public code: THRML, Extropic's JAX library for block
Gibbs sampling on sparse heterogeneous graphs, and the Torx toolkit for
composing kernel programs. The caps, schedules, and placements this article
priced are all things you can set with your own hands there, at sizes no
in-page oracle could check.

David Levin and Yuval Peres's *Markov Chains and Mixing Times* is the
classical account of the two quantities this article leaned on hardest: the
contraction coefficient under the floor and the mixing time under the tax.
It works from concrete chains — shuffled cards, glued graphs, Glauber
dynamics on the Ising model itself — and its bottleneck arguments are what
make $e^{2J}$ waiting times a theorem rather than an anecdote. It is the
honest next step for a reader who wants the mixing tax as mathematics
instead of a meter.

## Final Words

The chip itself runs one motion on one fixed graph. Everything this
article did was arrange for that motion to be some other machine's: a walk it
has no token for, a conditional it has no table for, a three-body law it has
no wire for. The arranging was never free, and the price list is now
explicit — spins for the graph you wanted and weren't given, honesty about
which inputs your kernels will actually face, and sweeps surrendered for
every coupling stiff enough to mean something. But notice what the price list
is *for*. Nothing on it says impossible. Every fixed machine is a universal
sampler one compiler away, at a price you can now compute.

That sentence is the two papers this article was compiled from, read as one.
Torx says what a stochastic program is; Thermalizers says what it costs to
make heat run one. You now own the instruments they assume — the
split-meter, the ghost, the taxes — and with those in hand their equations
arrive the way the best ones always do: as the formalization of something
you have already paid for.
