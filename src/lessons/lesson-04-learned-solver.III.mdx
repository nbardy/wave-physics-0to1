# Where the Simulation Is Wrong, and What a Network May Touch

The fluid solver in lesson 01 stops its pressure solve after forty sweeps with 28% of
the divergence still in the field, and its advection step loses between a third and a
half of a dye's mass over the length of one figure. Both errors have meters that run
inside the loop: the residual of the linear system, and the integral of the dye. The
error larger than either has no meter. In every cell of every turbulent simulation
run this year, the stress from motions smaller than the grid is filled in by a formula
whose constant was fit in 1963, and nothing in the run reports when the formula is
wrong. Networks are now trained to replace that formula, the solver's arithmetic, the
grid's smearing, the forecast's inputs, and, at the two largest weather centres since
2025, the whole simulation at once. Below, the smallest of those replacements that
this site can run honestly: a coarse advection step, the same step with a 954-weight
correction riding it, and the fine run both are trying to be.

<FluxRollout height={330} />

Three lanes from one seed. The left lane is the semi-Lagrangian scheme of lesson 01
on a 24 × 16 grid. The middle lane is the same scheme with a trained correction
applied every step. The right lane is the reference, run at 96 × 64 and averaged down
to 24 × 16, so the two coarse lanes are scored against a target on their own grid. The
plot under them is the error of each coarse lane against that target, per step, and
the <C k="dye">corrected curve</C> runs below the plain one for the first two to three
hundred steps and then crosses above it. Under the panes, two mass meters: the plain
lane and the reference both lose dye, because the scheme was never conservative, while
the corrected lane's own ledger reads zero on every step, including the steps where it
is the wrong lane. A learned component made this simulation more right for two hundred
steps, then less right, and the only meter that runs inside the loop could not tell the
two periods apart. The rest of this article is the map of where such components can
go, ordered by what can still check them.

## Five things a model is

A running fluid or wave simulation is five separable objects, and error enters at each
one. The equations: incompressible Navier–Stokes for one fluid, two-phase Navier–Stokes
with surface tension for a breaking wave, the wave-action balance for a spectral wave
model. Each is exact for what it describes and silent about what it omits. The
representation: a grid, a mesh, a set of particles, or a set of spectral bins, at a
spacing Δx and a step Δt, below which nothing exists in the run. The closure: the rule
for what the unrepresented scales do to the represented ones, which in every
operational model is a formula with tuned constants. The inputs: the initial state,
the boundaries, the forcing, the geometry, measured incompletely or not at all. The
solver: the arithmetic that advances the representation one step, each piece with its
own numerical error and its own stopping rule.

"Deep learning makes the model better" is five claims with five different tests. The
test that decides whether a learned part is trustworthy is what can check it while it
runs, and the checks form a ladder.

| what checks it | the verdict it can give | which learned parts have it |
|---|---|---|
| the residual of a linear system | acquits: the answer is right to tolerance, whatever produced it | warm starts, preconditioners, learned search directions |
| conservation of mass, momentum, energy, action | convicts only: a violation proves wrong; obedience proves nothing | flux-form corrections, antisymmetric exchange terms |
| statistics against a finer run | offline, hours per verdict | closures, discretization corrections |
| observations | offline, sparse, and uncertain themselves | assimilation, whole-model surrogates |
| nothing | the checker was replaced along with the physics | whole-model surrogates |

The lanes at the top sit on the second rung. The sections below climb down the
ladder from the top, and at each rung the learned part buys more and is checked less.

## The solver stops early

The pressure projection is a linear system, $A\,p = b$, with $A$ the five-point
stencil and $b$ the divergence the rest of the timestep left behind. Lesson 01 solves
it by sweeping, each cell set to the mean of its neighbours minus a quarter of the
local defect, and stops after forty sweeps without measuring anything. The figure
below runs that solver on lesson 01's own cylinder, with one meter added: after each
projection, one pass over the grid reads the divergence left uncancelled as a fraction
of the divergence the projection was handed, on a log axis, with a gate of one part in
a thousand drawn as a line.

<SolveDebt height={250} />

At forty sweeps the trace sits at 0.28, and at four sweeps three quarters of the defect
survives and the wake changes shape. At 240 sweeps, the right end of the slider, the
trace reads 0.087, still 87 times the gate, because the sweeps remove the rough part of
the error in a dozen passes and the smooth part in thousands. That meter, the residual
$\|b - A p\| / \|b\|$, is the one check in this article that can acquit. Any field that
brings it under the gate is the answer to that tolerance, and it does not matter what
produced the field.

A network of 809 weights, trained on this solver's own divergence fields, proposes a
pressure field before the first sweep. On the field below it is about nine tenths of
the converged answer, and the residual meter scores it 2.42 where an empty grid scores
1.00.

Both panes sweep to the gate. The pane that opened at 2.42 crosses it in 792 sweeps;
the pane that opened at 1.00 crosses in 2,679; the two accepted fields differ by 0.24%
of peak pressure. The meter reads the error through the operator $A$, which scales a
pattern by roughly the square of its wavenumber, so it hears a seam between cells about
two thousand times louder than a channel-wide bulge. The network supplies the bulge,
which costs the sweeps a thousand passes, and invents seams, which cost a dozen, and
the meter reports the seams. The figure below separates the two on a system whose
exact answer is zero, so that everything in the field is error.

At the default roughness, nine tenths of the ripple is gone in twelve sweeps and nine
tenths of the bulge in 1,287, and the <C k="div">violet trace</C>, the meter, follows
the ripple. After thirty sweeps it reads under one percent of its start with two thirds
of the error still in the field.

The obvious economy is to skip the meter and take the network's field as the pressure.
That is the arrangement of Tompson, Schlachter, Sprechmann, and Perlin's 2017 paper,
with a far larger network. Below, two channels warmed up for 120 timesteps; on the

With the gate kept, the same weights can be destroyed and the answer does not move.

In passes over the grid: sweeps from zero 2,679, sweeps from the network 792, conjugate
gradients from zero 420, conjugate gradients from the network 378. Hestenes and
Stiefel published conjugate gradients in 1952; its cost is set by the square root of

preconditioner or a learned search direction under a residual, the constructions of
Li and colleagues and Kaneda and colleagues in 2023, cannot corrupt the state, and
cannot buy anything but time.

## The grid smears

One rung down, the representation. The figure below runs the advection step of lesson
01, trace each cell backward and interpolate, in the same swirl at two resolutions,
seeded with the same dye. The left pane spends 6,144 cells. The right pane spends what
the buttons allow, and its meter reads the distance from the fine run averaged onto its
own grid, so blockiness alone scores nothing.

<SmearRace height={260} />

Every backtrace lands between cells, every landing blends four neighbours, and every
blend is a small blur. On the fine grid the blur is a fraction of a cell. At 24 × 16
each blur is four times wider, and a few hundred steps dissolve the disc into fog with
nothing unstable anywhere. The classical exit is to pay: sixteen times the cells,
sixteen times the arithmetic per step, and on most schemes a finer timestep on top.

The learned exit keeps the coarse grid and learns what the averaging steals, and this
rung has a clean teacher: the target is the gap between the coarse and fine runs of the
same equations, which is the one place in this article where the target is known to be
the truth. Kochkov and colleagues (PNAS 2021) put a network inside the convective flux
of a finite-volume solver, kept the pressure projection and the conservative form, and
trained it through 32 unrolled steps of the solver on 2D turbulence at Re = 1000 with a
2048² reference. The result runs on a 64 × 64 grid as accurately as the same solver at
eight to ten times finer resolution in each direction, which is 40 to 80 times less
compute, generalized to a doubled domain and to Re = 4000, and stayed stable over tens
of thousands of steps. All of it is two-dimensional, periodic, one forcing family, one
solver. Um, Brand, Fei, Holl, and Thuerey (NeurIPS 2020) measured where the gain comes
from on a cylinder wake: the uncorrected coarse solver has mean error 0.146 against a
fine reference, a correction trained on precomputed pairs brings it to 0.049, and the
same network trained with the solver in the loop for 32 steps brings it to 0.013. On a
randomly forced Burgers equation the in-the-loop training gains nothing, and the
authors say why: a correction can only anticipate what the state determines.

## A correction that cannot cheat

The network for this site's version of that seam has 954 weights, and its architecture
is mostly a list of things it is forbidden to do. It does not output dye. It outputs
fluxes, one number per face between neighbouring cells, and the correction is what the
fluxes add up to: whatever leaves a cell lands in its neighbour, the wall faces are
pinned at zero, and the total is untouched by any setting of any weight. Each flux is
capped at a fraction of the local dye scale, so one step can move at most a sliver of a
cell's contents, and the input is normalized by the dye's own magnitude, so conservation
holds in the plumbing before any training data is seen.

The data is six swirls from the fine solver, 240 steps each, every state averaged down
to 24 × 16, and the loss asks the corrected coarse step to land on the next true state.
The training loss settles near $10^{-6}$; the corrected step matches the true next
state almost to the pixel.

<OneStepDrift mode="failure" height={300} />

Run free, that model wrecks the flow inside two hundred steps. Every pair in its
training set began from truth, so the model has never seen its own work; run free, its
input is its previous output carrying its previous error, a distribution no training
pair drew from, and its response to that error is, on average, a slightly larger
error. The mass ledger under the pane reads zero on every step of the detonation,
because the flux form cannot do otherwise. Conservation is the check this rung has,
and it convicts only: it can catch a scheme inventing mass and it has nothing to say
about a scheme inventing motion.

## The solver in the loss

The repair is to make training look like deployment. The model takes sixteen
consecutive steps riding its own outputs, every step is scored against truth, and the
gradient is carried backward through the network sixteen times and through the solver
sixteen times; with the swirl frozen, a semi-Lagrangian step is a fixed table of
gathers, and a fixed gather has an exact adjoint. Each training window starts from a
slightly perturbed state, so the model earns credit only by pulling errors in.

<OneStepDrift mode="both" height={330} />

Same weights, same data, same optimizer, and the only change is what the loss could
see. The one-step lane's error ends the window in multiples of the entire field; the
in-the-loop lane stays in the plain solver's neighbourhood the whole way. Rescored on
the one-step exam the first model was trained to ace, the two models come out 1.4×
apart; on the rollout they are 117× apart. The property that separates wreckage from
an instrument is invisible at one step.

That is the model in the opening figure, and the opening figure carries its limits.
Across held-out swirls the corrected lane leads the plain one for two to three hundred
steps and then trades places with it, case by case. On the swirl half again faster
than anything in training, the lead holds through the whole window, because a faster
flow smears more per step and gives a smear-repairer more to repair: 0.42 against the
plain lane's 0.56 at the end. A plain run at 48 × 32 beats every corrected 24 × 16 lane
on this page, so 954 weights buy back roughly a third of the distance to a grid twice
as fine, early, and less of it late. Past 100% on the strength slider the correction
overshoots, flecks of impossible <C k="div">violet</C> ink appear, and the error curve
climbs over the lane with no correction at all, while the mass ledger holds at zero
through every step of the wrong fluid.

Every rollout in the literature is a recurrence trained one step at a time and tested
on a distribution it never saw, its own errors, and the three fixes that recur are all
ways of showing the model its mistakes: noise added to the training inputs
(Sanchez-Gonzalez and colleagues, 2020, at a standard deviation of $3 \times 10^{-4}$),
training on the model's own prediction with the gradient stopped through the earlier
step (Brandstetter, Worrall, and Welling, 2022), and diffusion-style refinement passes
that restore the high-frequency content a mean-squared loss neglects (Lippe and
colleagues, 2023, at about four times the cost per step). Those buy tens of percent in
how long the model stays correlated with the truth, not factors.

One test this article did not run separates a discretization correction from a
surrogate wearing one's clothes: hold the weights fixed and refine the grid. A
correction for discretization error must shrink as the grid does, at the scheme's own
order; a correction that does not shrink has learned something else, and on the
swirls above the something else is rollout stabilization at the training resolution.
The manifest already reports which lane wins at step 300, and the sweep would report
why.

## The closure has no meter

The rung below is the one this site cannot show, and the reason is worth one sentence.
The wake behind the cylinder at Reynolds numbers below 400 is laminar and periodic,
the fine run resolves it, and the whole gap between the coarse and fine lanes above is
numerical diffusion. Nothing in that channel is physically sub-grid, so a network
trained on the gap and called a closure would be a discretization correction in
costume. The closure bin needs a flow with a cascade, and the smallest honest testbed
is a periodic box with forcing, run against a spectral reference, which is not on this
site.

The bin is the largest. Resolving turbulence directly needs a number of grid points
that grows as roughly Re to the 9/4, so every engineering and geophysical flow is
under-resolved by orders of magnitude and stays that way as computers grow. The missing
scales drain energy from the resolved ones and, intermittently, hand it back. The
algebraic closures that stand in for them, Smagorinsky's eddy viscosity in large-eddy
simulation and the k–ε and SST models in Reynolds-averaged simulation, fail in
separation, adverse pressure gradients, rotation, and transition, which are the regimes
the simulations are run to predict.

The form of a closure is constrained by a theorem. The Mori–Zwanzig projection writes
the exact effect of the unresolved scales on the resolved ones as a term in the current
resolved state, plus a memory integral over the resolved history, plus a noise term
orthogonal to the resolved state. A closure that is a deterministic function of the
current, local, resolved state, which is every classical closure and most learned
ones, has dropped two of the three terms, and parameters added to a wrong form move
the fit rather than the ceiling. The three questions to ask of any learned closure are
what history it sees, what neighbourhood it sees, and whether it is stochastic.

The record of learned closures is a record of the gap between fitting the term offline
and surviving inside the solver. Beck, Flad, and Munz (2019) trained networks on
filtered direct simulations to reproduce the exact closure terms, reached a
cross-correlation of 47%, and found the models "lack long-term stability as high
frequency errors accumulate" when used directly; the stable model they ship uses the
network only to set an eddy viscosity. Frezat and colleagues (2022) report the same on
quasi-geostrophic turbulence: closures trained on the offline target drift or blow up
online, and closures trained end to end through a differentiable solver are stable and
match the reference spectra. Ross, Li, Perezhogin, Fernandez-Granda, and Zanna (2023)
benchmarked 148 closures with 19 online metrics in a two-layer ocean model and found
that every physics-constrained network is stable in the regime it was trained in, that
every one generalizes poorly to a new regime, and that a symbolic closure found by
regression and genetic programming performs on par in-domain and better out of it.

Two learned closures have run inside production models. Guillaumin and Zanna's network
predicts both the mean and the standard deviation of the sub-grid momentum forcing,
which makes it stochastic; placed in the MOM6 ocean model it ran stably, injected
energy as backscatter, and weakened as resolution improved, which is what a closure
should do, and it needed depth-dependent tuning and did worse near boundaries.
NeuralGCM (Kochkov and colleagues, 2024) keeps a classical, differentiable atmospheric
core and learns only the column physics, trained end to end through the core on
reanalysis; it ties GraphCast at one to three days, tracks global temperature for
decades with prescribed sea surface, and 15 of its 37 forty-year runs crashed, with
drift at four degrees of warming that its authors attribute to processes too subtle at
weather timescales for the training signal to see.

What the record says a closure must be forbidden to do, by construction rather than by
penalty: create energy (every term written as an exchange between the resolved field
and an account of sub-grid energy carried with the flow, so that the resolved energy
plus the account changes only by forcing and molecular loss); return energy from an
empty account; depend on the frame (inputs are velocity differences and gradient
invariants, never velocities); forget (a memory carried with the fluid rather than
fixed to a cell); and be deterministic where the exact term is not (a noise term whose
amplitude is paid from the account and whose correlation time is learned). The
energy account exists hand-built, in Jansen and Held's 2014 backscatter scheme, and
the learned stochastic closure exists unbounded, in Perezhogin and colleagues' 2023
model, whose runaway eddies its authors attribute to exactly the missing bound. The
two have not been joined, and the check that a joined closure would carry is an
identity: the two-account budget, every step, which convicts a violation and acquits
nothing.

## The inputs were never right

For real forecasting the largest error is not in the equations, the grid, or the
closure. In weather the initial-condition error dominates, and chaos amplifies it to
the predictability limit of about two weeks. In wave forecasting the wind is the
dominant error, and a wave model given the wrong wind cannot be corrected by anything
downstream of the wind. Most of the skill gained in forty years of operational
forecasting came from assimilation, the blending of model and observation.

A network can be trained on observations with no physics model as teacher, and in 2025
two were. Aardvark Weather (Allen and colleagues, Nature) ingests raw satellite
radiances, scatterometer backscatter, and station, ship, and radiosonde reports, about
8% of what an operational centre assimilates, and produces a global forecast in about
one second on four GPUs against roughly a thousand node-hours for the operational
system; it beats the American global model on surface temperature at all leads and
trails it on upper-air height through day eight, and its encoder is pretrained with a
physics-based reanalysis as the target, so the physics model remains the teacher even
though none runs at inference. GraphDOP (ECMWF, 2024) removes that too and trains
only on observations, with reported skill to five days. The check at every stage is
observations, which are sparse and uncertain themselves.

The rule for what a learned part may supply at this rung is observability. An
observation operator sees some directions of the state and not others: a dye image
fixes the velocity across its contours and says nothing about the velocity along
them; the sea surface fixes the shallow bottom and not the deep one. A network
confined to the directions the data cannot see can never contradict an observation,
and a network allowed into the directions the data can see is overriding data with a
prior.

## The whole machine replaced

At the bottom rung the network replaces the solver, the grid, the closure, and the
checker together, and since 2023 this rung has been measured as carefully as any.

| model | trained on | headline, with its condition | where it loses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pangu-Weather (Bi et al., Nature 2023) | 43 years of ERA5 reanalysis | lower error than the operational European model on every reported variable to seven days | effective resolution 500–700 km; no butterfly effect |
| GraphCast (Lam et al., Science 2023) | ERA5 1979–2017 | beats the European model on 90% of 1,380 targets; a ten-day forecast in under a minute | loses short-wavelength power; under-forecasts peak storm winds |
| GenCast (Price et al., Nature 2024) | ERA5 1979–2018 | a 50-member diffusion ensemble beats the European ensemble on 97% of targets and keeps the spectrum | initial conditions still from physics-based assimilation |
| NeuralGCM (Kochkov et al., Nature 2024) | classical core, learned physics, ERA5 rollouts | competitive with the European ensemble to fifteen days; runs for decades | 15 of 37 forty-year runs crashed; drift at +4 K |
| AIFS (ECMWF, operational February 2025) | ERA5, then operational analyses | up to 20% better on measures including cyclone tracks at a thousandth of the energy | initialized from the physics model's assimilation; the physics model kept |
| AIGFS (NOAA, operational December 2025) | GraphCast fine-tuned on NOAA analyses | about 10% lower height error than the American model at day ten | larger error than the physics model inside two days |

Three independent tests measured what these models contain. Bonavita (2024) computed
Pangu-Weather's spectra and found its wind and mass fields "increasingly dynamically
inconsistent with one another" as the forecast runs. Selz and Craig (2023) shrank the
initial-condition perturbations to a thousandth of their operational size: the physics
model's error grew fast at small scales and saturated, which is the butterfly effect,
and the learned model's error barely changed, which would indicate an atmosphere with
unlimited predictability. Charlton-Perez and colleagues (2024) ran four learned models
on Storm Ciarán, and all four placed the pressure minimum within a few hectopascals of
the analysis and forecast peak winds of 25 to 26 metres per second against 34 in the
analysis, missing the intensification after hour thirty; forecasts from the reanalysis
system itself do not have that bias, so the training data are not the cause. The
models encode the balanced dynamics of their training distribution and lack the
small-scale, unbalanced, fast physics, which is where a mean-squared loss puts no
weight.

Every model in the table is trained on the output of a physics model, and every one is
initialized by a physics model's assimilation. The physics model was not replaced. Its
job moved: it is the teacher and the source of the initial state, and the learned model
is the student that is cheaper to run inside the distribution it was taught.

## Structure instead of parameters

Everything in this section removes a degree of freedom from the network, and each
removal is a check that runs for free. Ling, Kurzawski, and Templeton (2016) write a
turbulent stress as a combination of the ten basis tensors of the resolved gradient,
so Galilean invariance holds by construction. Greydanus and colleagues (2019) learn a
scalar energy and take its symplectic gradient, so energy cannot drift. Mohan and
colleagues (2020) predict a vector potential and take its curl, so the velocity is
divergence-free to machine precision. Beucler and colleagues (2021) build conservation
layers that hold column energy and mass exactly, and report that a soft penalty on the
same quantities does not. The control group is the soft constraint: physics-informed
networks put the equation's residual in the loss, and Chuang and Barba (2022) ran one
on the cylinder at Re = 200, where it converged to a steady flow with no vortex
shedding, and on a Taylor–Green vortex, where it took about 32 hours of training to
match a 16 × 16 finite-difference run that took under twenty seconds.

The most generalizable learned closure in the record is one a person can read. Zanna
and Bolton's eddy closure was found by sparse regression with conservation in its form;
Cranmer and colleagues fit symbolic expressions to a graph network's messages and found
the expressions generalized further than the network; Ross and colleagues' symbolic
closure transferred between ocean regimes where the networks did not. When a closure
can be written down, the written form generalizes further than the network that found
it.

## Waves

Every operational spectral wave model computes the four-wave nonlinear transfer, the
term that shapes the whole spectrum, with the discrete interaction approximation of
Hasselmann and colleagues (1985), because the exact integral costs 100 to 140 times
more. The first learned emulator of the exact term, Tolman, Krasnopolsky, and Chalikov
(2005), reached 4.7 times the approximation's accuracy on spectra of the kind it was
trained on and then, inside the model, could not reproduce consistent wave growth and
lost directional symmetry within three hours, because "modeled wave spectra can be
expected to include features not found in the training data set." Two emulators
reported stable integration in 2026. NLML (Ikuyajolu, Van Roekel, Brus, and Thomas)
is trained on 3.2 million spectra from twin global runs, one with the approximation and
one with the exact term, ran a year of 2020 from a cold start with no fallback, cut the
global significant-wave-height error from 0.17 m to 0.07 m, and costs 1.04 times the
approximation on a GPU; its authors report low offline skill in low-wind regions and
instability unless the spectrum is converted to frequency space before the network
sees it. Both 2026 emulators are locked to their spectral grid, and neither has been
checked against a buoy. No learned dissipation, wind-input, or bottom-friction term
runs inside any spectral model.

The one learned component in operational wave forecasting is a whole-model surrogate.
ECMWF's AIFS has carried waves since May 2026, trained on a hindcast of its own physics
wave model from 1979 to 2025; it holds ten fields per point in place of the more than
1,200 spectral components the physics model carries, gains about 10% in medium-range
wave-height error, and loses skill at the sea-ice edge and on the strongest winds, and
the physics model it was trained on still runs beside it.

In phase-resolving breaking the bottleneck is the teacher. Mostert, Popinet, and Deike
(2022) ran two-phase Navier–Stokes with surface tension at adaptive resolution
equivalent to 2048³, 181 million cells and about half a million CPU-hours for one run,
matched the laboratory's bubble size distribution and the 70% of wave energy a
plunging breaker dissipates, and did not converge the droplets: many have radii of
about the smallest cell, the low-Bond-number case produces fewer than a hundred of
them, and the next refinement level "is not feasible to run currently." Against that
reference the learned parts are three, and none ships in a released solver. Eeltink and
colleagues (2022) attached a recurrent network to a nonlinear Schrödinger envelope
equation, trained on 438 wave-tank trajectories, and on held-out breaking cases cut the
error against the tank by about an order of magnitude, where the uncorrected equation
predicts no loss and the tank loses about 30% of a focused group's energy; by its
authors' account it cannot extrapolate from one wave type to another and has no
stability guarantee. The learned interface operators inside two-phase solvers, trained
on synthetic circles, "outperform conventional methods on coarser grids but diverge
with grid refinement" (Önder and Liu, 2023), and the one that recovers convergence does
so by gating: a classifier hands coarse interfaces to the network and fine ones to the
conventional reconstruction. The learned particle solvers have the best-measured
free-surface failure: on a 2D dam break the twenty-step error is $3 \times 10^{-5}$ and
the full-trajectory error is 0.19, four orders of magnitude of growth, as the fluid
compresses to 1.4 times its reference density and particles detach from the surface.
No learned particle solver has been run on a breaker, and there is no published neural
surrogate of a breaking-wave simulation.

## What more parameters buy

More parameters help only where there is signal to fit them to and where the model's
form can express the answer. In the closure bin there is a true target, so capacity
helps up to the data, and a stateless local net has the wrong form, so its ceiling is
set by form; history, neighbourhood, and a stochastic term move the ceiling and
parameters do not. In the discretization bin the target is small and known, capacity is
rarely the limit, and every stability fix in the record is a change to the training
procedure. In the whole-operator bin error falls as a power law in size and data
in-distribution, Aurora at 1.3 billion parameters reports no ceiling reached, and out of
distribution the benefit decays with distance from the training range, which is
NeuralGCM's drift at climate scale in another form. In the input bin the limit is
observations. In the solver bin the tolerance is fixed by the residual and parameters
change the speed of acquittal, not the verdict. So "with more parameters" is answered
yes in one bin, the closure, and only after the closure's inputs are right.

## The ladder, read on the first figure

The opening figure is the ladder in one frame. The corrected lane is a learned part
at the conservation rung: it cannot invent mass, its ledger says so on every step, and
the ledger says nothing about the two hundred steps where it is the better lane or the
steps after where it is the worse one. The pressure figures above it sit at the
residual rung, where a network can be sabotaged into noise and the answer moves by
seven millionths, and where the gain is a sweep count that a 1952 algorithm beats. The
closure, the inputs, and the whole-model surrogate sit below the conservation rung,
where the only checks are offline or are observations, and where the learned parts
that survived did so because something was forbidden to them by construction and
because they were trained inside the solver they live in.

What no learned component can be allowed to do, at any rung, is replace the thing that
checks it. The pressure network is the top of the ladder and buys the least; the
closure is the bottom and buys the most; and the tools to replace Smagorinsky's
constant have arrived first for the arithmetic around it, then for the grid it sits
on, and not yet for the formula itself.

## Further Reading

Dmitrii Kochkov and colleagues, [*Machine learning–accelerated computational fluid
dynamics*](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2101784118) (PNAS 2021), is the
discretization-correction result at full strength: the network inside the flux, the
solver in the loop, the 8–10× at Re = 1000, and the caveat that all of it is periodic
two-dimensional turbulence.

Kiwon Um, Robert Brand, Yun Fei, Philipp Holl, and Nils Thuerey, [*Solver-in-the-Loop*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00016)
(NeurIPS 2020), measured how much of that gain comes from training through the solver,
and named the method the opening figure's model was trained by.

Dmitrii Kochkov and colleagues, [*Neural general circulation models for weather and
climate*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07744-y) (Nature 2024), is the
hybrid exemplar: a classical differentiable core, learned column physics, decades of
stable rollout, and the most specific failure list in the field.

Andrew Ross, Ziwei Li, Pavel Perezhogin, Carlos Fernandez-Granda, and Laure Zanna,
[*Benchmarking of machine learning ocean subgrid parameterizations in an idealized
model*](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1029/2022MS003258) (JAMES 2023),
is 148 learned closures scored online, and the finding that the readable one
generalized.

Bas Sanderse, Panos Stinis, Romit Maulik, and Shady Ahmed, [*Scientific machine
learning for closure models in multiscale problems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02913)
(2024), organizes the closure literature around the offline–online gap, the
Mori–Zwanzig memory argument, and structure preservation.

Hendrik Tolman, Vladimir Krasnopolsky, and Dmitry Chalikov, *Neural network
approximations for nonlinear interactions in wind wave spectra* (Ocean Modelling 2005),
is the failure in the authors' own words, and Ikuyajolu and colleagues' NLML (JGR:
Machine Learning and Computation 2026) is the emulator that survived a year.

Wouter Mostert, Stéphane Popinet, and Luc Deike, [*High-resolution direct simulation
of deep water breaking waves*](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2022.330) (JFM 2022), is
the breaking-wave reference and its own statement of what it does not converge.

Massimo Bonavita, [*On some limitations of current machine learning weather prediction
models*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023GL107377) (GRL 2024), and Tobias Selz and George
Craig, [*Can artificial intelligence-based weather prediction models simulate the
butterfly effect?*](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023GL105747) (GRL 2023), are the two
measurements of what the whole-model surrogates leave out.
