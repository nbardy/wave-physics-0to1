# Teaching a Solver to Guess

Lesson 01's solver ends every timestep with a linear system: on the 96 × 64 channel
below, 5,631 pressure values, each tied to the cells beside it, that together must
cancel the divergence the rest of the step left behind. The solver sweeps through it
— every cell set to the mean of its neighbours, minus a quarter of the local defect —
and after forty sweeps moves on. The middle pane below starts from an empty grid. The
right-hand pane starts from a pressure field written before the first sweep by a
network of 809 weights, trained on this solver's own divergence fields; against the
converged answer it is about nine tenths right. Under each of those panes a meter
reads how much divergence its field leaves uncancelled, as a fraction of all of it.
The empty grid scores 1.00. The field that is nine tenths right scores 2.42.

On the left is the <C k="div">divergence</C> the projection is handed. The middle
pane sweeps from zero. The right pane sweeps from the network's field, which is
computed once, before sweep one, and does not change while the sweeps run. Both
panes sweep until the meter reads below one part in a thousand; that threshold is
the <C k="visc">gate</C>, and the green chip marks the sweep on which each pane
crossed it. The pane that opened at 2.42 crosses in 792 sweeps. The pane that opened
at 1.00 crosses in 2,679. The two accepted fields differ by 0.24% of peak pressure.
The gate has three settings: at 10⁻² the ratio of the two counts is 8.3, at 10⁻⁴
it is 2.1.

projection has no meter. It stops at forty.

## Forty sweeps

Lesson 01 ran the sweeps on a small grid, said a few dozen of them left the
divergence gone, and called them Jacobi iterations. The code updates each cell in
place, reading the neighbours already updated on the same pass, which is
Gauss–Seidel: about twice as fast as Jacobi and otherwise the same negotiation.
`project` runs forty of them and stops. Nothing is checked. Whatever divergence the
fortieth sweep leaves rides into the next timestep.

I ran this figure on lesson 01's own 144 × 88 cylinder rather than the network's
96 × 64 channel, because it is the one figure here that never touches the network,
and the larger grid sheds a wake. The trace beside the wake is one pass over the grid
after each projection: the divergence left uncancelled, as a fraction of the
divergence the projection was handed, on a log axis with the gate drawn as a line.

<SolveDebt height={250} />

At forty sweeps the trace sits at 0.28. A quarter of the defect survives every
timestep, 280 times the gate. At four sweeps three quarters survives, and the wake
behind the disc changes shape. At 240 sweeps, the right end of the slider, the trace
reads 0.087 — still 87 times the gate. Nowhere on the slider does it touch the line.

The system the sweeps are working on, with $A$ the five-point stencil at unit spacing
and $b$ the divergence:

$$A\,p = b, \qquad (A p)_{ij} = p_{i-1,j} + p_{i+1,j} + p_{i,j-1} + p_{i,j+1} - 4\,p_{ij}$$

One sweep sets each $p_{ij}$ to $(p_{i-1,j} + p_{i+1,j} + p_{i,j-1} + p_{i,j+1} - b_{ij})/4$,
the value that makes the equation hold at that cell if the neighbours hold still. The
meter is $\|b - A p\| / \|b\|$, one pass over the grid. At $p = 0$ it reads exactly 1,
and the trace above is that quotient, once per timestep.

Forty sweeps remove nearly three quarters of the defect. The next two hundred remove
two thirds of what those forty left. The sweeps are removing something quickly and
then something slowly, and the next figure separates the two.

## The shape of the leftover

The figure below solves $A p = 0$, whose exact answer is zero everywhere, so whatever
is in the field is error and the sweeps can be watched removing it. The field starts
as the sum of two patterns of equal height: a <C k="pLo">bulge</C> the size of the
box, and a <C k="pHi">ripple</C> whose wavelength the slider sets, from sixty-three
cells down to seven. The plot tracks how much of each pattern is left after every
sweep, and, in violet, what <C k="div">the meter</C> reads — $\|A e\|$ relative to
its starting value, which is the residual with $b = 0$.

<SlowModes height={250} meter />

At the slider's default, a ripple fourteen cells per wave, nine tenths of the ripple
is gone in twelve sweeps. Nine tenths of the bulge is gone in 1,287 — off the right
of the plot; at six hundred, where the figure restarts, a third of it is still there.
At seven cells per wave the ripple lasts three sweeps. At sixty-three, 233.

The meter follows the ripple. After thirty sweeps it reads under 1% of where it
started, and two thirds of the error is still in the field.

Each pattern's curve comes from one number. The operator $A$ maps a sine pattern to
a multiple of itself:

$$A\,\phi = -\lambda\,\phi, \qquad \lambda \approx \left(\frac{2\pi}{L_x}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{2\pi}{L_y}\right)^{2}$$

with $L_x$ and $L_y$ the pattern's wavelengths in cells along each axis. On this grid
the bulge has $\lambda = 0.0036$; the sixty-three-cell ripple, 0.020; the
fourteen-cell ripple, 0.41; the seven-cell ripple, 1.63; and the roughest pattern the
grid can hold, a checkerboard, 8.0. Gauss–Seidel removes a fraction of each pattern
per sweep that grows with its $\lambda$, and the sweeps it takes to remove nine
tenths of a pattern come out between $4.6/\lambda$ and $4.9/\lambda$ on all four
patterns measured above: 1,287, 233, 12 and 3. A pattern twice as wide costs four
times the sweeps.

The 4.6 is $2\ln 10$. A sweep replaces each cell by the mean of its neighbours — the
defect is zero here — and the mean of a sine pattern's four neighbours is
$(1 - \lambda/4)$ times the pattern, so one Jacobi sweep leaves that fraction of
it. Gauss–Seidel, reading updated neighbours as it goes, leaves about
$(1 - \lambda/4)^2 \approx 1 - \lambda/2$, and $(1 - \lambda/2)^n = 0.1$ at
$n = 2\ln 10/\lambda$. A pattern with $\lambda$ near zero is nearly a fixed point
of the sweep.

The meter reads the same number the other way round. With $e$ the error in the
field, $r = b - A p = -A e$, so on each pattern the residual is $\lambda$ times the
error. The meter hears the checkerboard 2,200 times louder than it hears the bulge,
and it hears the bulge — the pattern that costs a thousand sweeps — at a third of a
percent of its size.

The expensive error is the smooth one, and a smooth field on this grid is described
by far fewer than 5,631 numbers: a 12 × 8 grid of them holds the bulge. Few enough
to set by hand.

## Paint the bulge

The guess is 96 numbers on a 12 × 8 grid, one per block of 8 × 8 cells. You can drag
a block up or down; its value is stretched to the full grid by interpolating between
the centres of neighbouring blocks, which is the same prolongation the network uses.
The middle pane is what the meter hears, $|b - A p_0|$ cell by cell. The right pane
is the answer, to paint toward.

<PaintTheBulge height={250} />

Raise one block under the wake to the height the answer shows there and the meter
reads between 1.10 and 1.35, with at least 96% of the field still missing. The block lights the
middle pane along the lines where its ramp meets its neighbours, and nowhere inside.
Keep painting and the field error falls while the residual climbs. Before three
tenths of the distance to the answer is closed, the meter is above the empty grid's
1.00; the best painting there is — every block at the box average of the answer —
leaves 11% of the field missing and reads 2.68. Sweeping from that painting reaches
the gate in 915 sweeps, against 2,679 from the empty grid.

Between the centres of two blocks the guess is a ramp, and a ramp has zero Laplacian,
so $A p_0$ is zero there. Along the lines through the block centres, eight cells
apart, one ramp meets the next at a kink, and a kink is a spike in $A p_0$ a cell or
two wide: the pattern from the last section at $L \approx 2$, which the meter hears
at the top of its range. On the best painting, 99% of $\|A p_0\|^2$ sits on those
lines. So $r = b - A p_0$ is the divergence plus a lattice of spikes, and the spikes
outweigh the divergence.

A painted guess is two errors: the bulge it got right, which the sweeps charge over
a thousand for, and the seams it invented, which they charge a handful for. The
painter's job is the first. The meter cannot tell which one it is hearing: its
lowest reading on the way from the empty grid to the best painting is 0.91, at a
seventh of the bulge painted, and from there it rises. A hand takes a minute per
painting.

## The network paints

We replace the hand with 809 weights. The divergence is averaged over 8 × 8 blocks
to the same 12 × 8 grid — 96 numbers, with a second channel of 96 giving the fraction
of each block that is solid. Three 3 × 3 convolutions run on that grid; each output
reaches one block further than the last, so the third reaches seven blocks, 56 cells,
most of the channel. Then the prolongation from the last section. Averaged over
8 × 8 blocks, the divergence has nothing left in it finer than a block, so the
network cannot propose a ripple: it has no input to build one from. The second
channel is there because the pressure around a disc is not the pressure of the same
divergence in open water. The input is divided by its own root-mean-square on the
way in and multiplied back on the way out, so the network never sees a magnitude and
a doubled inflow cannot surprise it. The forward pass is 76,032 multiply-adds, the
arithmetic of about three sweeps. The weights are in this site's repository.

<ProposalAnatomy height={220} />

Left to right: the divergence; the 96 numbers the network sees; <C k="dye">its
field</C>; the answer, from conjugate gradients — a different solver — run a
thousand times past the gate. On the field from the top of the page the network's
field is 92% right and reads 2.42 on the meter. Averaged over sixteen held-out
fields it is 89% right and reads 2.27.

The training set is six channels at different inflows, viscosities, disc positions
and sizes, two of them stirred, each warmed up and sampled forty times: 240
divergence fields, read from `solver.div` after real timesteps — the array the
projection is handed. The targets are conjugate-gradient solves to
10⁻⁵, a hundred times past the gate. The loss is the mean squared error on the
pressure field — 8,000 steps of Adam in batches of twelve, about a minute on a
laptop — and it is on the field rather than on the meter because of the last
section's lowest reading: a network trained to make the meter fall would learn to
paint a seventh of the bulge and stop.

The race again, with the field on a knob and the gate fixed at 10⁻³. On the second
held-out field, a stirred channel, 22% of the network's field is missing and the
race is won by 1.4×. The last three fields are shapes the training set never held.
On the airfoil 26% is missing and the ratio is 1.9. On two discs, 20% and 2.0. On the

What the weights hold can be read off a single poke.

<ImpulseResponse height={330} />

The divergence is zero except for a 2 × 2 patch in an empty channel, and the slider
moves the patch. The true pressure is a well, deepest at the poke, and the same well
wherever the poke lands: that is the operator. The network's is a blurred patch with
the seams of its 12 × 8 grid, 35 to 45% wrong for pokes across the middle of the
channel and worse than the empty grid near the inlet and the outlet. It holds the
shape of pressure around a wake in this channel. A poke in open water is not that
shape, and neither is an airfoil.

The head start pays for the bulge, and the bill is still 792 sweeps: the meter
charges for the seams, then for three decades of residual after them. The obvious
economy is to stop reading the meter.

## Take the guess

The meter is one pass over the grid, and it hears rough error at full volume and
smooth error at almost none. Without it, the network's field is the pressure, the
velocity is corrected by its gradient, and the timestep moves on — the arrangement
of Tompson, Schlachter, Sprechmann and Perlin's 2017 paper, with a far larger
network. Below, two channels start from the same flow, warmed up for 120 timesteps
with forty sweeps after every proposal. On the left the network's field is then
accepted as it stands. On the right it is swept a fixed number of times, forty by
default, as the slider says. Both meters read the divergence left in the flow after
the projection has had its say; a healthy channel sits near 0.43.

<UngatedRollout height={300} />

The left channel reads 0.70 after one step, then 0.90, 1.35, 2.6, 6.5, 17, 45, and
84 after eight, while the right channel stays at 0.43. The wreckage is blocks eight
cells across: the seams. The gradient of the network's field has a kink at every
seam, the kink becomes a velocity error at that one wavelength on every timestep,
the next divergence is larger, and the next field larger with it. Slide the
correction sweeps down to find the edge: at twenty the right channel holds; at ten
it lasts eleven steps.

The meter cannot see what the guess got right, and it cannot miss what the guess got
wrong, and the wrong part is the part that wrecks a flow. So the meter stays, as the
finish line: the guess starts, the sweeps run, the meter says when.

## The gate

The slider below adds noise to all 809 weights: at the left end the trained model,
at the right end each weight plus twice its tensor's root-mean-square in noise, a
random function of the divergence. Three numbers move.

<SabotageGate height={300} />

At 10% noise the network's field is 18% wrong and the sweeps to the gate are 760,
fewer than the trained model's 792. At 20% it is 76% wrong and the sweeps are 2,326.
At 80% it is 162% wrong and the sweeps are 2,714, past the cold start's 2,679; from
there the guess costs more than no guess. At the right end it is 258% wrong and costs
3,059 sweeps.

The accepted field does not follow. It differs from the cold start's by 0.23% at the
trained end, by 1.24% at 20% noise, which is the worst anywhere on the slider, and by
0.0007% at the right end.

The worst is set by the gate. Both accepted fields satisfy
$\|b - A p\| < \varepsilon \|b\|$, so their difference $d$ satisfies
$\|A d\| < 2\varepsilon\|b\|$, and since $A$ scales every pattern by at least
$\lambda_{\min}$:

$$\|d\| \;<\; \frac{2\,\varepsilon\,\|b\|}{\lambda_{\min}}$$

On this grid $\lambda_{\min} = 0.0037$, and at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$ the right-hand
side is 1.24% of the answer. The noise at 20% found the one direction the gate is
loosest in, the bulge, and the two fields differ by 1.24%. The inequality has no
weights in it. It is loose by the same $1/\lambda$ that hid the guess: at a gate of
$10^{-2}$ it allows 12%, and the two paths at the top of the page differ by 6% of
peak pressure.

Behind the gate the network cannot move the answer. It can move the sweep counter,
and a sweep count is a ratio — 2,679 over 792 — whose denominator is lesson 01's
sweep.

## The denominator

The 3.4× is measured against Gauss–Seidel, which is the runner slowest at the bulge.
Conjugate gradients solves the same matrix to the same gate with no learning
anywhere. Below, four runners on the field from the top of the page: sweeps from
zero, sweeps from the network, <C k="vel">conjugate gradients</C> from zero, conjugate gradients from the
network.

The axis is passes over the grid. A conjugate-gradient step applies the stencil once
and then does the vector arithmetic that makes it conjugate gradients, about three
touches per cell against a sweep's one; counted in iterations, the comparison would
flatter it threefold. The four counts: sweeps from zero, 2,679; sweeps from the
network, 792; conjugate gradients from zero, 140 iterations, 420 passes; conjugate
gradients from the network, 126 iterations, 378 passes.

Cold conjugate gradients crosses the gate in 53% of the passes the network-warmed
sweeps need. Hestenes and Stiefel published it in 1952. It never sweeps: each step
moves the whole field along one direction, chosen so that the progress made along
every earlier direction is kept, and its cost is set by the square root of
$\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ — 47 on this grid — where the sweeps' cost is set
by $1/\lambda_{\min}$. The square root is why it is already good at the bulge. The
network is worth 3.4× against sweeps and 1.1× against conjugate gradients, because
there is one bulge to remove and conjugate gradients removes most of it on its own;
the two speedups compete for the same error and do not add. Changing nothing about
the learning and everything about the linear algebra is worth 6.4×.

One runner is not on the plot. The classical name for a guess built on a coarser
grid is a coarse-grid correction — Brandt's multigrid, 1977 — which does what the
network does with a ladder of grids and no training set. This article did not build
one. The network is competing with it whether or not it says so.

Those are the counters under the opening figure. Its curves have not been drawn.

## The opening frame, read

The race again, with the meter of each pane plotted against sweeps on a log axis and
the gate as a line. Before it runs, the curves can be drawn from the sections above.
The <C k="dye">warm curve</C> starts at 2.42, above the cold curve's 1.00: the seams.
It drops under the cold curve by sweep five, because a seam is the roughest error the
grid holds and the roughest error goes in a few sweeps. Through the middle of the
race it pulls further ahead: what the network's field left behind lives at
wavelengths of eight to thirty cells, which the sweeps clear faster than the bulge.
Past a thousand sweeps both curves fall at one rate, 0.08 decades per hundred sweeps
— the bulge's rate — with the warm curve forty times lower. Both cross the line, at
2,679 and 792.

<WarmStartRace height={350} gate plot />

The gate knob moves the ratio because the lead stops growing. In sweeps, the warm
curve is ahead by 1,246 at 10⁻², by 1,887 at 10⁻³, by 2,037 at 10⁻⁴. Once both
curves fall at the bulge's rate the lead is fixed, and a fixed lead is a smaller
fraction of a longer race: 8.3×, 3.4×, 2.1×. The two accepted fields differ by 6% of
peak pressure at 10⁻², 0.24% at 10⁻³, 0.003% at 10⁻⁴: the gate's bound at each
setting.

## Further Reading

Jonathan Tompson, Kristofer Schlachter, Pablo Sprechmann and Ken Perlin's
[*Accelerating Eulerian Fluid Simulation With Convolutional Networks*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.03597)
(ICML 2017) is the paper the ungated pane re-runs at toy scale: a network trained to
supply the pressure field, accepted without a residual check, and the long-rollout
divergence that follows.

Ayano Kaneda and colleagues' [*A Deep Conjugate Direction Method for Iteratively
Solving Linear Systems*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10763) (ICML 2023) takes the
denominator question seriously: the network supplies search directions inside a
Krylov method, so it has to beat conjugate gradients rather than a sweep counter.

Magnus Hestenes and Eduard Stiefel's *Methods of Conjugate Gradients for Solving
Linear Systems* (Journal of Research of the National Bureau of Standards, 1952) is
the blue curve. Jonathan Shewchuk's
[*An Introduction to the Conjugate Gradient Method Without the Agonizing Pain*](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~quake-papers/painless-conjugate-gradient.pdf)
(1994) is the friendliest route to it, and its spectral picture is the one *The
shape of the leftover* measures.

William Briggs' *A Multigrid Tutorial* is the book-length version of this article's
central measurement — error has a wavelength, smooth error is the expensive kind,
and the fix is to look at it on a coarser grid — and Achi Brandt's
[*Multi-Level Adaptive Solutions to Boundary-Value Problems*](https://www.wisdom.weizmann.ac.il/~achi/LOP16.pdf)
(1977) is the runner this article owes a race.

## Behind the gate

The meter reads the error through $A$, and that one fact is both its blindness and
its guarantee. It cannot see the nine tenths the network supplied, because that part
is smooth and $A$ scales it by 0.0036. It cannot miss the seams the network invented,
because they are not smooth, and it cannot miss the blocks that wreck an unchecked
flow for the same reason. And the distance between the accepted field and the cold
start's is set by $\varepsilon$ and $\lambda_{\min}$, neither of which the weights
can touch.

So a learned guess belongs in this solver at the one move of the timestep that has a
meter of this kind, and behind that meter, as a head start. It is worth 3.4× against
the runner worst at the bulge, 1.1× against a runner good at it, and an unmeasured
amount against multigrid, which does the same job without a training set and which
this article did not run.

At the right end of the damage slider the 809 weights are noise, the network's field
is 258% wrong, the sweep counter reads 3,059 against the cold start's 2,679, and the
accepted field differs from the cold start's by seven millionths.
