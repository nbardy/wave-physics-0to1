# Building the Navier–Stokes Equations

Stir cream into coffee and a pale ribbon stretches around the spoon. The spoon
stops, but the coffee keeps turning; the ribbon folds into finer threads until its
edges disappear. The motion comes from interactions throughout the cup, including
places the spoon never touched.

A computer can produce a similar flow while storing only a few numbers at each
point of a grid. It needs rules for carrying those numbers, exchanging momentum
between neighbours, and keeping fluid from accumulating in a cell that is already
full. The wake below is the result of those updates, repeated.

<WingFlow variant="hero" />

The two currents of <C k="dye">dye</C> part at the wing's nose, slide along its two
faces, and fold into the trail behind it. Increase the viscosity and the velocity
differences fade more quickly. The flow needs a few seconds to respond, and dye
already in the channel takes longer to leave.

Every quantity in this article is color-coded, so <C k="vel">velocity</C> is always this blue,
<C k="dye">dye and drifting parcels</C> are always amber — the wing's lower current
wears <C k="dye2">rose</C> — and when something goes physically wrong you will see it
in <C k="div">violet</C>. The prose and the pictures speak the same colors. The amber
and the rose are the same water wearing different shirts; a color only marks where a
current came from.

The flows here are two dimensional and incompressible. The small grid experiments
below expose individual updates; the wing combines them at a higher resolution.

## Seeing Flow

If you've ever watched a stream from a footbridge, you've noticed that the water
itself is nearly invisible — what you actually see is what the water *does*. Reeds
bend where the current drags on them, and petals drift wherever the surface carries
them. In the demonstration below, the slider controls the speed of time:

<FlowVis mode="creek" />

The <C k="visc">reeds</C> and the <C k="dye">petals</C> are reporting the same
underlying thing in two different ways, and both reports are worth keeping.

A reed is anchored in place. It tells us how fast the water moves *at its own spot*,
and it keeps telling us about that spot forever. We can distill this idea into a grid
of <C k="vel">arrows</C>, each fixed in place, each showing the direction and speed of
the flow right there — the longer the arrow, the faster the water:

<FlowVis mode="arrows" />

The grid is not a fact about the water. Between any two <C k="vel">arrows</C> the flow
has a velocity too, and we could draw one at any point we liked.

A petal is the opposite kind of witness. It is anchored to nothing; it goes where the
water goes, and its path traces out a history of the current that carried it. We can
distill this idea too — little <C k="dye">markers</C>, light enough to instantly match
the flow around them, each dragging a ghostly trail of where it has been:

<FlowVis mode="markers" />

Notice that the two instruments genuinely disagree about what to report. An
<C k="vel">arrow</C> watches one place and sees many different parcels of water pass
through it. A <C k="dye">marker</C> watches one parcel of water and carries it through
many different places. That distinction is the heart of our first
equation.

Sometimes we don't care about direction at all — only about how fast things are moving
everywhere at once. For that we can paint speed as color, brighter blue where the flow
runs faster:

## A Field of Velocities

Water is made of molecules, and at a small enough scale the neat blue arrows dissolve
into chaos. In the demonstration below you're watching hundreds of particles rattling
around a box, colliding with the walls in a frenzy. The dashed box in the middle
averages the velocities of every particle inside it, and you can change its size with
the slider:

<ParticleBox mode="still" />

Two things here are staged: the <C k="vel">blue arrow</C> is drawn several times longer
than the average it reports, and the particles pass through each other instead of
colliding — a particle's direction is re-randomized now and then to stand in for the
collisions real molecules are having constantly.

With a small averaging box the readout is jittery because it samples only a few
particles. Make the box bigger and the average settles to almost exactly zero.
The molecules keep moving even when the water as a whole is at rest.

Now let's add a current. In the demonstration below the second slider superimposes a
drift on every particle — and notice the numbers before you touch it: the drift is
tiny compared to the thermal speed of the particles themselves.

<ParticleBox mode="wind" />

Watching individual particles, you can barely tell the difference between still water
and a strong current. However, the averaging box is not fooled. Even a drift far
smaller than the thermal chaos shows up immediately and unmistakably in the average.
Averaging around different points gives the local velocity at each one.

That is what our arrow grid has been all along: every <C k="vel">arrow</C> is the
average velocity of the swarm of molecules near a point — and with that, we can leave
the molecules behind. From here on we'll write $\mathbf{u}(x, y, t)$ for the
<C k="vel">velocity</C> at position $(x, y)$ and time $t$: the whole arrow grid,
every point, every instant, folded into one symbol. A quantity with a value at every
point like this is a *field*.

We can watch a field from the footbridge. The harder question is what the field looks
like to something *in* the water.

## Following a Parcel

Water speeds up as a channel narrows. In the steady current below, each blue arrow
stays fixed: water keeps passing it at the same velocity. The amber parcel moves
from the wide entrance into the narrower channel. The gray probe stays bolted to
one spot. Both report the horizontal component of velocity, in metres per second.

  <ParcelAcceleration />

Scrubbing time moves the parcel through the narrowing. Its speed rises from about
1.05 to 3.49 metres per second, while the fixed probe keeps reading 2.20. The graph
compares those readings on the same scale. A field can be steady even while every
parcel passing through part of it accelerates.

This is a prescribed current, chosen to isolate that distinction. The narrowing
and the velocity fit together so the same volume crosses every section each
second. Water can slide along these walls; we have not solved the viscous flow in
a real nozzle. The parcel follows the specified velocity exactly.

“How fast is this quantity changing?” therefore needs a location:

- At a fixed point, the change is written $\partial q/\partial t$.
- Following a parcel, it is written $Dq/Dt$, the *material derivative*.

A parcel moving with velocity $\mathbf{u}$ through a quantity whose spatial
variation is $\nabla q$ picks up an additional change, $\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla q$:

$$\frac{Dq}{Dt} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}}_{\text{change at a point}} \;+\; \underbrace{(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,q}_{\text{change from moving through the field}}$$

For a stationary parcel, $\mathbf{u}=0$, the second term vanishes. If the quantity
is uniform in space, $\nabla q=0$, motion through it changes nothing. In the steady
channel, the first term vanishes instead: the parcel's acceleration comes entirely
from moving through a velocity field that varies with position.

Newton's law applies to the parcel's acceleration, $D\mathbf{u}/Dt$. A computer
usually stores velocity at fixed grid locations, so it needs both terms to recover
that acceleration. The same distinction also tells it how to carry dye.

## Advection

The flow carries dye. Let's watch it do that in the demonstration below, where a
prescribed current sweeps an amber blob downstream:

<DyeCarry field="uniform" />

A uniform current moves the blob without changing its shape or sharp edges.
Differences in velocity across the blob deform it. A shearing current draws it into a
slanted streak: the amber inside is exactly as concentrated as before, but its
boundary has grown longer.

<DyeCarry field="shear" />

And a vortex winds it into a spiral, each turn drawing the amber into a thinner
filament. The filament's length grows without bound; its width collapses toward
zero. Nothing has blended — the amber is still pure amber — but the distance any
blending would have to cross is shrinking toward nothing.

<DyeCarry field="vortex" />

This carrying-and-deforming is *advection*. For any carried quantity $q$, in
a world with no other physics, the parcel's value never changes —
$Dq/Dt = 0$ — which, unpacked with our new derivative, reads:

$$\frac{\partial q}{\partial t} \;=\; -(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,q$$

Velocity is itself carried by the flow. A gust of fast water doesn't stay behind
while the river moves on — the fast water *is* water, and it goes where the water
goes. So $q$ above can be $\mathbf{u}$ itself, and the advection term becomes
$(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{u}$: **the field carrying the field**. The equation
feeds on its own output. This is a *nonlinearity*: doubling velocity multiplies
this term by four. A prescribed current can deform dye, as the earlier figures
showed; carrying velocity also changes the current that does the deforming.

On a computer, dye concentration is a list of numbers. In the row below, a full bar
means concentration one and an empty cell means zero. The blue outline selects one
destination cell. Change how far the fluid travels during a timestep, and the blue
arrow traces that cell's incoming fluid back to its old position:

<BacktraceLab />

At a whole number of cells, the destination can copy a stored value directly.
Between cells, it combines two neighbouring values in proportion to their
distances. The readout shows that weighted average. This is *semi-Lagrangian
advection*: look backward along the flow, then interpolate the old field. In two
dimensions, the interpolation uses four surrounding samples.

There is another plausible update: estimate the slope in each cell and use the
advection equation directly. Both methods below start with the same rectangular
pulse in a uniform current. Scrub through the updates; the dashed gray outline is
the exact translated pulse, averaged over the cells. The horizontal axis is
position, and the vertical axis is dye concentration:

<TransportErrors />

The local-slope method produces negative concentrations and peaks above one, then
grows without bound. This particular combination of forward time stepping and
centered spatial differences is unstable for pure advection. A smaller timestep
delays the failure but does not remove it.

Backward tracing stays between the values it samples. It remains bounded, but its
edges spread away from the exact outline as interpolation is repeated. That is
*numerical diffusion*: the update blurs dye even though this experiment contains
no physical diffusion. Stability keeps the calculation from blowing up; accuracy
asks how faithfully it follows the intended motion.

Real dye can also spread through still water by molecular diffusion. Stirring
stretches it into thin filaments, shortening the distance that diffusion has to
cross. Pure advection leaves each parcel's concentration unchanged; molecular
diffusion does not. The computer's interpolation can blur the boundary too, for
a different reason.

The velocity field has its own process of exchange between neighbours.

## Viscosity

Layers sliding past each other exchange momentum, pulling slow layers along
while holding fast ones back. That internal friction is
<C k="visc">viscosity</C>.

The left profile below is a *jet*: a band of fluid moving right between still
layers. The right profile shows the same fluid two seconds later. Increase the
viscosity while keeping that observation time fixed, or try the two other initial
profiles. Arrow length is horizontal speed; the dashed outline preserves the start:

<DiffusionLab />

Near each edge of the jet, a fast layer slows while its slower neighbour speeds up.
The thin stripes disappear much faster than the broad jet because momentum has a
shorter distance to travel between fast and slow layers. The stationary walls hold
their adjacent layers at zero speed — the *no-slip condition* — and absorb momentum
as it reaches them. Viscosity also dissipates kinetic energy, shown beneath the
profiles. The viscosity here is measured in grid-cell units, so the dial controls
the calculation without claiming to select a particular liquid.

The profiles measure speed. To see what happens to the water itself, mark a line
of parcels across the same jet and follow them for two seconds. The left pane
removes viscosity; the right retains it. Amber dots are the final positions, faint
traces show how far they travelled, and small gray squares preserve where each
parcel would have arrived without viscosity. Blue arrows give its final velocity:

<LayerMotion />

The central parcels fall behind their gray squares. Just outside the initial jet,
parcels that would have stayed still now move forward. They stay in their own
horizontal layers throughout: momentum crosses between layers without those
marked parcels having to cross. The longer an arrow is now, the faster its parcel
is moving now; the trail includes all its earlier speeds too.

For this parallel flow, viscosity changes velocity according to a diffusion
equation:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} \;=\; \nu\,\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$$

Read the right-hand side as a measurement of disagreement: $\nabla^2\mathbf{u}$ at a
point is, up to a constant, how far that point's velocity sits from the average of its
neighbours'. Every instant, each parcel drifts toward its neighbourhood's average, and
the <C k="visc">viscosity</C> $\nu$ sets how fast. As a check, a perfectly linear
velocity profile has zero disagreement — every point is exactly the average of its
neighbours — and indeed such a profile doesn't change at all.

Two viscosity quantities appear in fluid mechanics. *Dynamic viscosity*,
$\mu$, relates a shear force per area to the rate at which neighbouring layers
slide past one another. Its units are pascal-seconds. Dividing it by density
$\rho$ gives *kinematic viscosity*, $\nu$, measured in square metres per second:

$$\nu = \frac{\mu}{\rho}$$

It is $\nu$ that controls the spread of velocity in the diffusion equation.
For a model liquid with $\mu=0.1\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$ and
$\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $\nu=10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$.
Density and viscosity describe different properties; “thick” is too vague to
specify either. The workbench's cells²/s are the same kinematic quantity with
length measured in cells instead of metres.

Molecular diffusion of dye spreads a concentration. Viscosity spreads momentum.
Interpolation on a coarse grid can blur either field even if neither physical
diffusion is present. They may soften similar-looking pictures, but they belong
to different parts of the model.

## Comparing Flows

A faster flow carries momentum farther in a given second. Higher kinematic
viscosity spreads velocity differences farther in that second. A useful comparison
is the time to travel a distance $L$, about $L/U$, against the time for viscosity
to act across it, about $L^2/\nu$. Their ratio is the *Reynolds number*:

$$Re = \frac{L^2/\nu}{L/U} = \frac{UL}{\nu}$$

Small $Re$ means smoothing acts quickly compared with transport; large $Re$
means it acts slowly. This does not make a particular Reynolds number a universal
threshold for turbulence. Geometry, boundaries, and the initial disturbance
matter too.

Below are three copies of a square flow, one metre on each side. Opposite edges
join. All start with the same pattern: fluid moves downward while alternating
bands also move left and right. The first pane is the reference. In the middle,
the multiplier increases speed and viscosity together. On the right, it increases
speed alone.

The arrows show velocity divided by each pane's speed $U$, using one common scale.
The clocks compare equal $Ut/L$: how many domain lengths the downward current
has travelled. They need not show equal seconds.

<ReynoldsSimilarity />

The reference and middle patterns agree even though their velocities, viscosities,
and clocks differ. At twice the speed, half the elapsed time puts the pattern in
the same place; twice the viscosity acting for half as long smooths it by the same
amount. On the right, the faster current has had less time to smooth, so more of
the sideways motion remains. At multiplier one, all three return to the same flow.

This comparison uses a laminar flow whose exact solution is known. The displayed
profiles are computed on 48 rows; a finer-grid check compares them with that
solution. Its matching geometry, periodic boundaries, scaled initial velocities,
and observation time are part of the comparison. Equal $Re$ alone would not make
a flow through a pipe identical to a flow around a wing.

## Pressure

Back to the molecules one last time. Their frenzy doesn't just average into velocity —
it also *pushes*. In the demonstration below, particles bombard a solid block from all
sides; every impact flashes red on its surface:

<ParticleBox mode="collide" />

Each individual impact is imperceptibly small, but there are staggeringly many of
them, and their combined, uninterrupted push on every surface is known as *pressure*.
In balance, the bombardment from the left cancels the bombardment from the right and
the block goes nowhere. And a parcel of fluid is bombarded by its neighbours in
exactly the same way: the fluid pushes on itself.

Equal pressure on opposite faces produces equal and opposite pushes. What
accelerates a parcel is a difference across it. In the next figure, you can move
a region of <C k="pHi">high pressure</C> and a region of
<C k="pLo">low pressure</C>; the shading shows the resulting landscape:

<PressureLandscape mode="map" />

Contour lines make the unevenness visible: <C k="pHi">high pressure</C> is a hill,
<C k="pLo">low pressure</C> is a valley, and where the lines crowd together, the
terrain is steep.

<PressureLandscape mode="contours" />

A parcel of fluid in this landscape behaves like a marble on the terrain.

<Predict
  question="A parcel sits between a pressure hill ahead of it and a valley behind it. Which way does it accelerate?"
  a="Toward the hill — pressure attracts"
  b="Away from the hill, toward the valley"
>
  <PressureLandscape mode="parcels" />
</Predict>

The <C k="dye">parcels</C> roll off the <C k="pHi">hills</C> and gather speed toward
the <C k="pLo">valleys</C> — always down the steepest slope, faster where the slope is
steeper. The mathematical name for "the direction and steepness of the slope" is the
*gradient*, written $\nabla p$, and the pressure contribution to acceleration points down it:

$$\text{acceleration due to pressure} \;=\; -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p$$

The minus sign is the rolling-downhill; $\rho$, the density, converts push into
acceleration — the same shove moves light fluid more than heavy fluid. As a check: on
flat terrain, $\nabla p = 0$, so pressure contributes no acceleration. A parcel
may already be moving; zero acceleration does not mean zero velocity.

These terms change velocities. In an incompressible fluid, those velocities must
also agree on how much water crosses between neighbouring regions.

## The Broken Fluid

Carrying and smoothing give us new velocities, but those velocities can disagree
about how much fluid should cross a cell. Below, the arrows lie on the cell's
faces, where we store the velocity components used to count flow. Pressure will
live at the centre. The cell has a fixed volume of one cubic metre; adjust its
right-hand outflow until the accounting works:

<CellFlux />

If 1.5 cubic metres enter each second and 2.2 leave, the cell would have to supply
the missing 0.7 cubic metres itself. If less leaves than enters, fluid must
accumulate. Neither is allowed in our fixed, completely filled, incompressible
cell. The velocities must change. A plausible-looking dye image would not reveal
this error reliably; counting flow through the faces does.

What we've violated is easier to see with a counting argument. Draw any closed loop in
a real flow and tally the water crossing it — in through some parts, out through
others. Since water doesn't compress and doesn't vanish, the ins must exactly cancel
the outs, for *any* loop, *anywhere*. See for yourself; drag the loop around and
resize it:

<DivergenceLoop withSource={false} />

However you place the loop, the net flux reads zero. Now let's plant a hidden spring —
a spot that manufactures new fluid out of nothing. Catch it inside the loop:

<DivergenceLoop withSource={true} />

The loop measures a surplus, and the <C k="div">violet</C> shading marks its source.
The local, per-point version of this counting rule is the
*divergence*, written $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}$ — roughly, the net outflow from a tiny
neighbourhood. For real, incompressible fluid the law is stark:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} \;=\; 0$$

This constraint holds everywhere at every instant in our incompressible model.
A proposed velocity field can have nonzero divergence even when its arrows look smooth.
A pressure correction must bring that divergence back to zero.

## Solving for Pressure

The proposed field below combines circulation with an outward flow from the
middle. Its violet cells have an imbalance between incoming and outgoing fluid.
The right pane applies a fraction of the pressure correction computed for that
same field. Move from zero correction through 100%, then try going farther:

<ProjectionLab />

The pressure is **lower** near the centre, shown in cyan. Its inward push cancels
the outward component. At 100% the unbalanced flow disappears while circulation
remains. Beyond that, the correction introduces an imbalance of the opposite sign.
The meter measures the root-mean-square divergence across the grid, relative to
the original error; it does not count visible dye.

Where a proposed update sends too much fluid into a region, a pressure hill can
push it out. Here the error points the other way, so the repair needs a pressure
depression. The sign of the gradient matters as much as its size.

For our periodic domain, a velocity field can be separated into a part with
zero divergence and a part that is a gradient. Pressure correction removes the
latter. The example below uses a vortex for its divergence-free part and a
source-and-sink pattern for its gradient part. You can inspect each separately:

<HelmholtzSplit />

Zero divergence means balanced local inflow and outflow, not “only swirl.” A
uniform straight current also has zero divergence and survives the correction.
The decomposition must respect the domain's boundary conditions; solid walls
require different conditions from the periodic edges used in our little grids.

That split is the *Helmholtz decomposition* — shown here, proven in the
further reading. Whatever mess a flow proposes, some pressure field's push can cancel
its piling exactly, leaving only honest swirl behind.

Finding that pressure field means solving a puzzle where every point's pressure
depends on its neighbours'. Demanding that the corrected flow have zero divergence
turns out to require, in the discrete world of our grid:

$$\nabla^2 p \;=\; \frac{\rho}{\Delta t}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}^*$$

— where $\mathbf{u}^*$ is the flow *before* correction. You don't need to unpack this
one symbol by symbol; read it as "the pressure landscape whose disagreement-with-
neighbours matches the divergence." An equation of this shape is a *Poisson
equation*, and a computer solves it by patient negotiation: every cell repeatedly
updates its pressure toward what its four neighbours (and the local divergence)
suggest. Scrub through that negotiation yourself:

<ProjectionLab iterations />

Ten sweeps leave about 26% of the initial imbalance; forty leave about 4.4%; 160
leave less than 0.1%. Each setting starts from the same proposed velocities, so
scrubbing backward really removes iterations rather than continuing an old solve.
These are *Gauss–Seidel iterations*: each cell uses its neighbours' freshest
pressure values. Other algorithms reach a small residual faster. The useful
stopping criterion is the remaining imbalance, not a special number of sweeps.

There is a second requirement hidden in the grid. The pressure gradient used to
correct face velocities and the divergence used to check cells must compose to
the same Laplacian that the pressure solver uses. Otherwise, accurately solving
the pressure equation can still leave an inaccurate correction. The little grids
here use matching face and cell operators, and check the corrected velocities
directly.

Pressure has no independent time-evolution equation here: there is no
$\partial p/\partial t$ term. At each instant, we solve for the pressure field
that keeps $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$.

<Waypoint>
The constraint $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$ determines the pressure field through a
Poisson solve. We can now combine that correction with advection and viscosity.
</Waypoint>

## Assembling Navier–Stokes

Newton's law says that a fluid parcel's mass times its acceleration equals the
sum of the forces on it. Combining the terms above gives:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t}}_{\text{change at a point}} \;=\; \underbrace{-(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\,\mathbf{u}}_{\text{the flow carries itself}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p}_{\text{downhill from pressure}} \;+\; \underbrace{\nu\,\nabla^2\mathbf{u}}_{\text{neighbours drag on neighbours}} \;+\; \underbrace{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{outside pushes}} \qquad \text{with} \qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$$

These are the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, written for a Newtonian
fluid of constant density and viscosity. Here $\mathbf{f}$ is an applied force **per unit mass**, with units of acceleration;
gravity is one example. Moving solid surfaces act through boundary conditions.
The workbench represents a local push as an imposed change in velocity.

Now compare complete calculations. Both panes below start with identical swirls,
dye, and one local push. The left runs all three operations; the right omits the
one you select. Scrub time forward and backward to compare equal elapsed times.
These small grids wrap at their edges, so fluid leaving one side returns on the
opposite side:

<TermExperiment />

Without pressure correction, the local imbalance survives. Without viscosity,
more kinetic energy remains in the swirls. Without velocity advection, the
velocity pattern can smooth and undergo pressure correction, but cannot carry
itself across the grid; dye still moves through that field. Removing one operation
isolates its contribution without changing the start or the observation time.

Even with viscosity disabled, interpolating velocity and dye still loses detail.
The difference between the panes is the effect of explicit viscosity in this
calculation, not a comparison between perfectly inviscid and viscous fluids.

A finite grid producing a bounded animation does not prove that the corresponding
continuous equations remain smooth. The three-dimensional existence and smoothness
problem has a precise formulation in the Clay Mathematics Institute's problem
statement; it cannot be settled by watching a simulation.

## One Liquid, Two Geometries

We can put the assembled equation to work without a large grid when the geometry
is simple enough. Start with fluid between two parallel plates. The lower plate
stays still; the upper plate moves at speed $U$. With no pressure gradient and
no slip at either plate, the steady velocity rises linearly across the gap $h$.
The force needed to keep the upper plate moving depends on the liquid's dynamic
viscosity.

In **1. Plate** below, change the upper plate's speed. Its velocity profile gets
steeper and its drag rises. The reference plate stays at 0.02 metres per second.
Both use the same model liquid, at a fixed temperature. The force readings are
calculated values, not laboratory measurements.

<ViscosityTransfer />

For plate area $A$, the tangential force is $F=\mu A U/h$. Rearranging lets us
infer the viscosity from the displayed force, speed, area, and gap:

$$\mu = \frac{Fh}{AU}$$

At $U=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}$, the readout is $F=0.05\ \mathrm{N}$. With the displayed
area and gap, this gives $\mu=0.1\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$. Doubling the speed doubles
the force and leaves that inferred value unchanged. The idealization neglects
the finite plate's edges and the initial motion before the steady profile forms.

**2. Pipes** carries that same viscosity into another geometry. The reference is
a circular tube of radius one millimetre. On the right, four tubes of half the
radius have the same total open area; sixteen tubes of a quarter the radius do
too. Each tube is ten centimetres long and has a pressure drop of 1000 pascals.
Scrubbing collection time starts both vessels at zero and compares the same
interval under the selected conditions.

Despite equal open area, four tubes collect only a quarter as much as the single
tube. Sixteen collect a sixteenth. For this steady, fully developed laminar flow,
the equation and no-slip wall condition give

$$Q = \frac{\pi\,\Delta p\,R^4}{8\mu L}$$

Here $Q$ is volume per second, $R$ is tube radius, $L$ is its length, and $\Delta p$
is the pressure drop. Halving $R$ reduces each tube's flow sixteenfold; four such
tubes recover only a quarter of the reference flow. At zero pressure drop the
flow is zero. Doubling viscosity halves it. The displayed parabolic velocity
profile is another prediction of the same solution.

The tube calculation excludes entrances, connecting manifolds, and any change
in temperature. It uses the value inferred at the plate, but both readings come
from mathematical models. Agreement here checks how the calculation transfers;
experimental validation would require independent measurements in both apparatuses.

**3. Wall slip** changes an assumption while keeping that fluid value fixed.
The reference wall remains no-slip. On the right, the fluid may have a nonzero
axial velocity at the wall, proportional to its local shear rate. The constant
of proportionality is a *slip length*, $b$; zero recovers no-slip.

The blue profile shifts away from the gray no-slip outline, including at its
ends where it meets the wall. More fluid passes through. For uniform slip around
a circular tube, the same pressure drop gives

$$Q_{\mathrm{slip}} = Q_{\mathrm{no\ slip}}\left(1+\frac{4b}{R}\right)$$

The control deliberately reaches slip lengths comparable to these tube radii so
the effect is visible. It describes a chosen mathematical boundary condition,
not an ordinary pipe coating. The viscosity inferred at the plate has stayed
unchanged: changing what happens at a wall changes the predicted flow too.

## Running the Equation

The numerical solvers here belong to the family popularized by Jos Stam's
*Stable Fluids*: split a timestep into smaller operations, then apply them in
sequence. The prescribed currents earlier in the lesson were simpler experiments
used to isolate those operations.

Below is one timestep, lasting 1/40 of a second. Each button selects an operation
and shows its input beside its result. Start with the push, then carry velocity,
smooth it, and correct pressure. The final step carries dye using the corrected
velocity. For changes too small to see at the normal scale, enlarge the change in
velocity tenfold:

<SolverCycle />

The push is local, but pressure changes velocities farther away because the cells
must agree on the fluid crossing their shared faces. Smoothing reduces velocity
differences without updating the dye. Carrying dye changes its stored concentrations
without changing velocity at all. The buttons inspect successive states from one
calculation; they do not run each selected operation repeatedly on its own.

That distinction is useful when debugging. If dye transport changes velocity,
the code has coupled states that should be separate. If pressure leaves a large
divergence, the next pretty frame does not make the update correct.

Release the timestep and those same operations produce a moving flow. Drag across
the grid to push the fluid, or use **Push upward**. **Add dye** only marks the
fluid near the centre; it gives the flow no extra momentum. Changing viscosity
affects subsequent updates, so a disturbed flow takes time to settle:

<LiveSolverLab />

The wing from the opening uses the same combination of terms. Its slider controls
the Reynolds number, changing the balance between advection and viscosity.
Here you can also stir the stream with your pointer:

<WingFlow variant="finale" />

At lower Reynolds number the two dye currents remain in broader, smoother
bands; at higher settings, the wake stretches and folds them into finer threads.
An orderly flow can still mix by diffusion, and even a steady shear can stretch
a patch. What changes here is how strongly the wake rearranges the currents.
The disappearing color boundaries also reflect numerical blurring on the grid.

The small workbench uses a 48-by-32 grid, backward tracing with bilinear
interpolation, explicit diffusion within its stability limit, and a pressure solve
stopped by its residual. Its grid lines make the stored samples visible; hiding
them does not add resolution. The wing uses a larger grid and a different
implementation of the same operations, with a lower-resolution fallback on
browsers without WebGPU.

Both lose fine detail to interpolation, and both omit the third dimension. The
constraint can be satisfied accurately while a coarse grid still misses an eddy.
These are useful models for examining a calculation; they are not measurements
of an aircraft's performance.

These models have neither compressibility nor a moving free surface. Sound needs
the first; waves on water need the second. Adding either changes the equations
and boundary conditions being solved. A rippling drawing alone would not supply
that missing physics.

## Further Reading and Watching

Lorena Barba's [*CFD Python: 12 Steps to Navier–Stokes*](https://github.com/barbagroup/CFDPython)
builds a solver through executable notebooks, beginning with transport and ending
with two-dimensional cavity and channel flows. It is a good next step if you want
to implement the updates yourself.

Jos Stam's [*Stable Fluids*](https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs448-01-spring/papers/stam.pdf)
describes the splitting and backward tracing behind this family of graphics
solvers. Compare its treatment of stability with the numerical blurring in our
transport experiment.

Robert Bridson and Matthias Müller-Fischer's
[*Fluid Simulation* course notes](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/fluidsimulation/)
develop staggered grids, pressure projection, boundary conditions, and more
accurate transport methods. They are especially useful for understanding why the
discrete gradient and divergence must agree.

Bartosz Ciechanowski's [*Airfoil*](https://ciechanow.ski/airfoil/) develops the
physical intuition behind pressure, viscosity, and flow around a wing through
interactive experiments. Its approach to making mechanics visible helped shape
this lesson.

David Tong's [fluid mechanics notes](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/fluids.html)
develop stress, dynamic and kinematic viscosity, and the simple plate and pipe
solutions used here. Lauga and Stone's [study of slip in pressure-driven flow](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/lauga/papers/4.pdf)
gives the circular-pipe slip relation; the [review by Lauga, Brenner, and Stone](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0501557)
examines the physical conditions under which liquid boundaries depart from no-slip.

The Clay Mathematics Institute's [Navier–Stokes problem statement](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/navier-stokes-equation/)
specifies the separate question about three-dimensional existence and smoothness.
Its conditions matter; a claim about a numerical solver is a different claim.

## Final Words

The wing's wake changes when a velocity field carries itself, exchanges momentum,
and responds to pressure. Those operations can be stopped and examined. A dye
patch can reveal blurring, a cell can expose missing volume, and a matched flow
can show whether a change in speed has been accompanied by the right change in
viscosity and time.

The same equation also predicts how a plate drags on fluid and how much that fluid
can deliver through a tube. The result depends on the material, the geometry, and
what the boundaries allow. Computing a flow means making each of those choices
concrete enough to check.
