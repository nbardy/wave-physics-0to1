## Past the oracle's edge

The obvious move is to add pixels, and it dies twice before anything runs.
An 8×8 glyph is sixty-four spins, and sixty-four spins have $2^{64}$ states —
the exact enumeration behind every <C k="ghost">gray ghost</C> since Part 1's
atlas would need on the order of $10^{19}$ additions, and no figure will ever
carry one again. The second death is quieter. A bigger picture needs richer
conspiracies among its pixels, and the reflex is to wire pixels to pixels —
denser couplings for a denser image. The fabric never offers the chance.

Choose instead *which* sixty-four cells of a 16×16 fabric patch are the
pixels, and take them all from one checkerboard color — the same one-color
condition Part 2's layer figure quietly relied on. Then hang the rest of the
patch from them by wiring distance, exactly the re-hang you have now watched
twice as a curiosity. This time it is the model. The 256-cell patch falls
into three banks — the 64 pixels, then 128 hidden cells one wire away, then
64 more behind those — and all 2,048 wires of the patch land between
adjacent banks. Part 1 called this hanging an architecture nobody designed.
It has now designed ours: a deep, layered Boltzmann machine, its depth and
widths dictated by nothing but where sixty-four squares sit on the die.

And look at what the one-color choice did to the reflex we started with.
Every wire in this fabric joins opposite checkerboard colors, and every
pixel is the same color — so the number of pixel-to-pixel wires is exactly
zero. Whatever makes two patches of ink agree across the glyph must route
through the hidden banks, because no direct route exists. Part 1 showed
pixels-that-cannot-conspire as a *failure* — the factorized dreams, half a
box welded to half a checker — and the repair was a small hidden bank all
pixels consult. The fabric turns that repair into the only grammar on
offer: 192 hidden spins are not a design choice here, they are everything
the patch contains that is not a pixel. Per noise level the model owns
2,048 couplings on real wires, 256 biases, and 64 input wires — one held
wire per pixel, carrying the evidence $x_t$ in — 2,368 numbers, three times
over for the three levels.

Here it is dreaming. Left, the patch itself: squares are pixels, faint dots
the hidden banks, everything flickering under the same chromatic sweeps as
always. Right, the filmstrip — coins at $x_3$, a glyph at $x_0$ — with the
frame currently being consumed as clamped evidence wearing the
<C k="held">green halo</C>. The glyph family is new, seven members at 8×8,
no two closer than twenty pixels apart. The weights ship on credit one last
time, and this time the debt settles by receipt rather than re-performance:
the paint-box wall at 4×4 keeps the live training job, while this model
trained offline in forty-five seconds, seven hundred epochs per level, and
the shipped constants match a fresh run of that stated recipe to five parts
in a million — an identity this article's check harness re-verifies on
every run.

Where the ghost bars used to be, the canvas prints an epitaph instead:
*joint oracle dead*. Part 1's last page promised what happens next — the
oracle "degrades into a hierarchy of witnesses, each with a smaller
jurisdiction" — and the meter row under the filmstrip is that promise doing
its job. Three witnesses keep live tallies on the canvas as dreams finish;
a fourth ticks offstage; and the shipped model's full audit — 250 dreams
and a deeper probe set, run in the same harness that re-verifies the
weights — puts a number on each. The **fenced conditional**: inside a 2×2
pixel window with its surroundings held, the exact reverse conditional is
still computable, and the trained model sits 0.053 from it in total
variation at the gentlest level, 0.178 at the middle one — against 0.937
untrained. The **pinned moments**: the pairwise correlations of the 250
dreams miss the glyph family's by 0.117, versus 0.343 before training, with
183 of the 250 landing within ten pixels of a family member and no glyph
left undreamed. The **known-answer probe**: wreck a known glyph with the
forward noise, run the descent on the wreck, and 15.63 pixels astray come
back as 2.78. And the **autocorrelation clock**: the visible magnetization
of the last level's chain decorrelates in 5.7 sweeps — the going rate, in
Gibbs time, for one independent look at what this chain believes. No single
one of these is the oracle. Together they are what trusting a sampler means
at any size the world actually uses — and they still catch old crimes: run
this same chain on the stale-field synchronous schedule and the moments
witness moves by 0.063, the write-conflict offense from Part 1's big
lattice convicted at a scale its meter never reached. One warranty from
Part 2 rides along unchanged: the chain's drift still saturates at the
depth-independent floor $\tilde\delta \le \bar\varepsilon/(1-\rho)$,
because contraction never cared how many spins were doing the contracting.

The knob deserves a farewell reading. At sixteen pixels, the allocation
measurement came back a shrug — split the sweep budget any way you like,
the dreams barely notice. At sixty-four, the shrug is gone: at four sweeps
per level the finished dreams sit a mean 12.01 pixels from the nearest
family member, at twenty-four they sit at 4.95. The coherence is bought
with Gibbs time, the mixing tax finally presenting at figure scale — and
each further notch of scale moves the sweep line of the bill the same
direction, from rounding error toward rent.

## The ceiling

Fabric has been spent in this article before — the disjoint-region schedule
bought its zero reflashes with 432 p-bits against the conditioned kernel's
144. The model above spends 256, one patch, pixels and hidden banks
included. The remaining question is what spending the *whole die* buys, and
for one recipe the papers pin the answer to an anchor: a 16×16 field of
8-bit pixels, embedded their way, costs about 14,000 p-bits.

Both answers are on the chart, and the distance between them is the honest
finding. The curves scale the anchor naively — p-bits proportional to area
times bit depth — and the 8-bit curve crosses the 250,000 ceiling at
$16\sqrt{250000/14000} \approx 67.6$ on a side, computed live from the two
verified constants and nothing else. The papers themselves claim 32×32 to
48×48, the <C k="ghost">gray band</C>. The naive curve runs optimistic
because its one assumption is flat overhead per pixel, and minor-embedding
chains lengthen as fields grow; the band is the paper's own number. So the
chart plots the band as the claim and the crossing as the arithmetic, and
lets the gap between them stand as the measured price of that assumption.
Two more readings from the same constants: at one bit per pixel the naive
crossing runs to 191 on a side, and our entire 8×8 model — the one you just
watched dream — is a dot at 256 p-bits, about one-thousandth of the die.

## The billed wall of dreams

The wall is back for the third and last time — the forty lattices that
opened Part 1 wearing static, that closed it dreaming your painted glyph,
and that opened this article with a bill it could not defend. It runs here
under the settled schedule: batched, conditioned, the sweep budget split
evenly across levels because the allocation measurement said the levels do
not yet care. Hold it against the opening figure and the first thing to
check is what did not move: the dreams. The sample stream is bit-identical
under both schedules — same seeds, same kernels, same sweeps — because
reordering loops and sharing one flashed landscape changes when operations
happen, never what they compute. The split-meter beside the wall says the
same thing in its own language, panes matching Part 1's trained quality
throughout. The schedule changes the bill, and only the bill.

The bill it changes, it changes by 91,918 to 11,582 iteration-equivalents
per dream — 7.9 times cheaper at a batch of sixty-four, 8.2 in the limit —
and the composition bars show where the money went. The reflash item, 89
percent of the opening's total, is gone to amortization: one flash, ever.
What refused to leave is the floor: at the default budget the remaining
bill is 92 percent clamps, and driving the sweeps a hundredfold higher only
dilutes them to 79. Every reverse step must hold its evidence, holding is
priced like flashing, and no schedule ever invented waives it — the
amortizations did not abolish the bill, they stripped it down to the part
that is the algorithm. In the corner where the opening printed its
indefensible number, the settled figure prints the paid one: 3.47
microjoules per dream at the papers' per-iteration estimate, roughly
288,000 dreams per joule, up from roughly 36,000 — with a margin listing,
on the same canvas, everything the number leans on: modeled rates from
verified constants, never hardware measurements; a browser running the
chip's contract, not the chip; patches that wrap where the die runs short;
a hero that ships pretrained.

## Further Reading

The Thermalizers paper (arXiv:2608.01615) supplied this article's spine
twice over, and its appendices are the part to read now. Appendix K is the
14,000-p-bit anchor's home — fixed-point registers assembled from chains of
p-bits, a compiled Gaussian posterior held against exact inference — and
Appendix B is the energy accounting every bill in this article modeled its
rates from. Its most imitable habit is the one the cost strips borrowed: it
keeps its estimates and its measurements in visibly different registers,
and never lets one impersonate the other.

The Torx paper (arXiv:2608.01612) carries the acknowledged ancestor of this
finale: a discrete-diffusion chain on binary MNIST, run as τ-leaping over
gate programs — independent bit-flip gates first, then coupled ones, with
the coupling buying back a measured share of the quality the independent
gates leave on the table. Their chain and ours differ in machinery and
agree in thesis — a diffusion model for this hardware is a program of
kernels, judged by what coupling buys — and reading their version after
building this one is the fastest way to see which choices were forced and
which were ours.

THRML and the Torx toolkit remain the public, runnable half of the stack,
and for this article's questions in particular they are the natural next
instrument: every amortization staged above is, in the end, a loop you can
reorder yourself, at sizes where the bill arrives as wall-clock time
instead of a modeled strip.

Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan, and Ganguli's 2015 paper, *Deep
Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics*, is where
diffusion models began, and where they began is the point: the
destroy-then-learn-the-undo loop this series trained three times was
derived there from the statistical physics of diffusion itself, years
before the architectures that made it famous. A decade of engineering
later, this lesson ran the same loop on a machine whose native act is
thermal relaxation. Read it last and the whole series folds into one
sentence of history: the idea came from thermodynamics, went to the GPUs,
and here it is being fitted for the trip home.

## Final Words

The wall dreaming at the top of Part 1 was forty distributions wearing
static. The same wall, closing this page, reads three levels deep, one
level per article: the Boltzmann law each
lattice dwells in, the compiled program each descent executes, the bill
each dream leaves behind. Nothing was added to the wall in three articles —
layers of reading were.

Pricing the dreams did not shrink them. The strays on the wall are still
strays, the glyphs still condense out of static unannounced, and the one
line of the bill that survives every amortization is the clamps — the cost
of holding the evidence still while a picture forms around it.

Somewhere right now a building of GPUs is dreaming pictures against a bill
nobody prints; this page printed one, small and modeled and confessed line
by line. Part 1 closed its atlas on a fact read at two scales — the local
rule and the global law, one object. The series closes on that fact's
sequel, one level up: the schedule and the bill are one object read twice,
once by the fabric, which executes it, and once by whoever pays.
