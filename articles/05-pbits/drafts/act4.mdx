## Two measurements and a subtraction

Compiling one instruction meant one hand on one knob until a meter read ten
percent. The machine ahead of us needs hundreds of weights at once, and nobody's
hand is that patient. The weights will have to come from examples.

The examples are six little pictures — a bar, a cross, a box, an X, a checker,
a corner — each drawn on a 4×4 grid of p-bits, <C k="sUp">ink up</C>,
<C k="sDn">blank down</C>. Sixteen p-bits per image, on purpose: small enough
that exact enumeration — the meter's gray half — still audits everything that
happens here.

The first move is destructive. Take a glyph and run it through a short loop:
at step $t$, every pixel flips with probability $p_t$ — eight percent, then
twenty, then fifty. This loop deserves an honest label: it is three lines of
ordinary code, a biased coin per pixel, and nothing about it is thermodynamic.
The machine's native act is still red/black Gibbs on a programmed landscape —
the schedule that turned out to be the chip — and its role here is the
opposite one: it will learn to run this cheap wrecking loop *backwards*.

<Filmstrip />

Watch a few beats. The strip redraws with a fresh wreck each time — corruption
is a distribution, so one ruined frame proves nothing. Each pixel's
keep-probability is the same S-curve as ever, $\sigma(2\beta_t)$, which makes
the last step $\beta_3 = 0$: at fifty percent the pixel forgets which way it
pointed, and $x_3$ is sixteen fair coins. Drag the noise up and the glyph dies
earlier in the strip; the last frame cannot get any deader. The useless fair coin this article
opened with turns out to be manufacturable on demand — and that is the trick's
hinge, because a picture erased down to fair coins can be *re-grown* from fair
coins, one gentle step at a time, if something knows each step's undo.

That something is one small conditional machine per noise level. Its cells come
in three populations. The noisy input $x_t$: sixteen p-bits, always
<C k="held">held</C> — the machine consults the wreck, it never edits it. The
output $y$: sixteen free p-bits that must settle into a plausible
one-step-cleaner image. And four hidden p-bits $w$ with no pixel meaning at
all, free in every phase — room for the machine to represent *which glyph this
smells like*. Wires run from input to output and from hidden to output, none
within a layer — so the free cells form two banks with no internal edges, and
the two-coloring rescue applies to them by construction: flash all of
$y$, flash all of $w$, exactly Gibbs.

Training this machine takes two measurements. In the **data phase**, clamp the
input *and* the answer — $x_t$ and the true cleaner frame $y$ both wear halos —
and let only the hidden four flicker. In the **dream phase**, release the
answer: clamp $x_t$ alone and let $y$ and $w$ run free under red/black sweeps —
the machine hallucinating what it currently believes cleaner frames look like.
The data phase sits on a <C k="held">green</C> wash — the clamped phase, in the
clamp's own color — and the dream phase on a <C k="meter">violet</C> wash, the
sampler running loose. Below the phases, two strips accumulate the same
statistic from each: how strongly input pixels agree with output pixels. Their
difference is the third row.

<PhaseTrainer />

For the first few seconds nothing trains, and the two strips disagree
loudly — the dreams are static, the data is not. Then the trainer starts, and
you can watch the disagreement curve fall as the dream strip creeps toward the
data strip. That is the entire learning rule:

$$\Delta U_{ij} \;\propto\; \langle x_i\, y_j\rangle^{+} \;-\; \langle x_i\, y_j\rangle^{-}$$

— nudge each wire by the gap between what it measured with the answer held
$(+)$ and what it dreamed without it $(-)$, and the same subtraction for every
bias and every hidden wire. A wire that dreams too little agreement gets
warmed; one that dreams too much gets cooled; a wire whose two phases agree is
finished. When every strip matches, the machine's dreams are statistically
indistinguishable from the data, which is the only sense of "learned" on offer
here.

The landscape being nudged is worth writing down. Call the input→output wires
$U$, the hidden→output wires $W$, the output and hidden biases $b$ and $c$:

$$E_\theta(x_t, w, y) \;=\; -\,b\cdot y \;-\; c\cdot w \;-\; x_t^{\mathsf T} U\, y \;-\; w^{\mathsf T} W\, y$$

Check its corners. Kill $W$ and the hidden four disconnect: each output pixel
votes alone, fed only by input pixels — hold that corner, it comes back as a
machine of its own. Grow one diagonal $U_{ii}$ wire large and that pixel
becomes the noisy-copy gate you compiled by hand, one section ago; this energy
is sixteen of those plus company. The dream phase here runs ten sweeps from
the data rather than to full stationarity — the standard shortcut, called
contrastive divergence — so the gradient is slightly approximate, and the
warranty comes from the meter instead: every few seconds the pane on the right
compares the learned kernel against the *exact* reverse conditional, computed
by summing all 65,536 output states, six-glyph mixture arithmetic the
enumerator can still do at this size. The pane shows the auditable corner —
the fenced-patch move again, a 2×2 marginal — while the full 65,536-column
comparison runs offstage in this article's check harness. This is the oracle's
last stand: one lattice size larger and no such number could be printed at
all.

## The wall of dreams

Generation is the training diagram run as a chain. Start from sixteen fair
coins — the fully erased frame the corruption ends at. Clamp them as $x_3$,
let level 3's outputs settle through a few red/black sweeps, and the sample
becomes $x_2$. Clamp, settle, step down. At $x_0$ a glyph has condensed out of
static in three moves.

<DreamChain />

The finished dreams collect along the bottom, and they are the readout: bars,
checkers, crosses — family members, never on a schedule, never in a fixed
order. Starve the slider to one sweep per level and the resemblance slips —
more strays, more smudges — because each sample gets clamped as the next
level's evidence before it has settled. Gibbs time is what coherence costs.

Where the coherence comes from is easiest to see by removing it. The corner
of the energy we flagged — kill $W$, no hidden units — trains in seconds right
below, on the same data, seeds, and budget, and dreams beside the full model:

<DreamCompare mode="factorized" />

The factorized dreams are wrong in a way you can see before you can say it:
plausible ink in implausible company, half a box welded to half a checker.
Every pixel obeys its own conditional; nothing enforces that the sixteen
choices belong to the *same glyph*, because "same glyph" is a fact about the
joint, and this model has no machinery for joints. You met this exact failure
at two coins — separate knobs buy separate leans, never agreement — and
here it is at sixteen, with the pairwise-correlation readout under each row
keeping score. Four hidden p-bits are the wire that fixes it: a private
sixteen-state summary that all sixteen outputs consult, so the pixels can
conspire.

One more thing can poison the dreams, and it is an old acquaintance. The
trainer's dream phase is a sampler, and a sampler can be run on the wrong
schedule. Train the identical model with the all-at-once schedule as
its negative phase — every free cell updating on its neighbors' stale
values — and compare:

<DreamCompare mode="synchronous" />

Look honestly: the lower strip does not look broken. It looks like glyphs.
The stale reads decouple the hidden bank from the output bank during the
dream phase, so the trainer subtracts the wrong statistic and bakes the error
permanently into the weights — and both rows here are *sampled* honestly, with
red/black sweeps, so nothing in the flicker can warn you. Only the exact audit
under each row can: the all-at-once-trained kernel sits well over twice as far
from the true reverse conditional. Back on the big lattice, the broken
schedule was at least running live, one rescue away from honest. Here it has
retired into the weights, and no schedule fix will ever reach it. The
wrongness has moved up a level — out of the schedule, into the learned
landscape — and the only witness that can follow it there is the same kind
that caught it the first time: an exact number, not an impression.

The wall that opened this article is back — the one that flickered before any
of its vocabulary existed, running weights it promised to explain.

Forty independent chains, each running the three-level descent you just built,
each on its own seed. Most cells land on a recognizable family member;
every so often one lands *between* — a bar with a checker's accent, a box
missing a wall. That is not a defect to apologize for. The machine's product
was never a picture; it is a distribution, and the strays are the visible
proof that the wall is sampling one rather than stamping copies. The weights
running here are the ones the opening shipped on credit — trained by exactly the loop
you just watched, on exactly these six glyphs. The paint-box settles the debt
properly: draw your own sixteen pixels, press train, and the browser reruns
the whole clamp–dream–subtract loop with your glyph in the dataset — four
hundred epochs, about a second; the model is that small. Then the wall
starts dreaming yours, ringed in <C k="held">green</C> whenever a cell lands
on it.

This lesson ran on a luxury it never hid: at sixteen p-bits the truth is
65,536 additions, so every figure could carry its gray ghost, and "correct"
was always one subtraction away. The Z1's state space has $2^{269{,}568}$
corners. The complete oracle does not survive the trip — but it does not die
all at once, either. It degrades into a hierarchy of witnesses, each with a
smaller jurisdiction: conditionals that stay exact inside a fenced patch;
moments that theory pins; autocorrelation and effective sample counts; small
embedded problems whose answers are known in advance — every one of them an
instrument this article built at toy scale, still on duty at full scale. A
stochastic computer is trusted the way this page's little walls came to be:
not because its output looks random — everything's output looks random — but
because every witness that can still speak agrees.

Every resistor in the room with you is flickering right now. A few microvolts
of thermal noise ride the lamp cord, the charger cable, the tracks of the
laptop this page is running on — the same jitter this whole article computed
with, and the thing every other chip in the room spends most of its power
shouting down. Nothing is listening to it. For one afternoon, on this page,
some of it had a job.
