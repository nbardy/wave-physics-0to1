## Everyone talks at once

Sixteen coins were a toy. Here are 256, wired <C k="ferro">agree</C> to their
grid neighbors — and a problem that was invisible until now. *One at a time* is
how we've updated coins so far, each consulting the freshest state of its
neighbors. It is correct, and it is serial: a quarter of a million cells means a
quarter of a million turns per sweep. Every coin's update is just its own little
S-curve — surely we can run them all at once?

The grid below offers both schedules. In its center, four cells are fenced by
eight <C k="held">held</C> neighbors — held in a deliberate split, up on the
west, down on the east, so a domain wall runs straight through the fence and the
patch inside stays honestly undecided. Because the fence never moves, the patch's
exact distribution is computable, and the meter audits it live:

<GridSchedules />

*One at a time* idles at the floor. Now press *all at once*. The lattice starts
to shimmer with a strange crosshatch — and the meter climbs an order of
magnitude and pins there. It does not descend with more evidence. This is not
noise; it is a different, wrong answer, delivered confidently at enormous speed.

The walker's map shows how different:

<StateGraph schedules />

Under *all at once*, the walker jumps along chords that are not edges — several
coins changing in a single move, where the one-at-a-time walker only ever slides
along one edge at a time. But look carefully at what the chords do and do not
prove. They say this is a *different process*. They do not say it is an unlawful
one — moving many coins in one tick is something a perfectly legal schedule can
also do, and one is about to rescue this grid. The crime is somewhere else.

Here is where. When every coin updates simultaneously, each consults its
neighbors' *old* values — two wired coins persuade each other's ghosts. Two
coins that want to agree, both currently down: each sees a down neighbor an
instant before both flip up; next tick each sees up, and both may crash down
again. The lattice crosshatches with exactly this oscillation. The crime needs
two things at once: a wire, and both of its ends being *written* in the same
instant. Call that a write conflict. The panel below freezes a bare copy of the
grid — no fence, all 256 cells free — at the moment one dispatch goes out. Every
cell being written this instant wears a ring, and every wire joining two ringed
cells glows red:

<WriteConflict />

*One at a time* rings a single cell, and a wire cannot join a cell to itself, so
the count reads zero by construction. *All at once* rings the whole board, and
all 480 wires turn red at once — every wire is a pair of coins deciding on each
other's stale ghosts. That number, not the multi-coin moves, is what separates
the two schedules; hold on to it, because the rescue is going to drive it to
zero without giving up the rings.

And notice what the grid itself never told you. The all-at-once lattice still
finds low-energy states — happily aligned domains everywhere you look — and its
flicker is as random-looking as the honest one's. Watch the two numbers under
the meter: evidence keeps accumulating by the thousands, and the distance stops
falling anyway. A correct sampler's error starves with evidence; a broken one's
error is a fact about the process, and no amount of watching dilutes it.

## The two-coloring rescue

The failure was never parallelism. It was *wired neighbors updating together*.
So: color the grid like a checkerboard. Every wire joins a red cell to a black
cell — no red touches red, no black touches black. Freeze black and update all
128 red cells at once: each consults only frozen black neighbors, so nothing it
reads is stale. Then freeze red, flash black. Two half-sweeps, and every coin
has taken a fully honest turn.

The floor — not a compromise between speed and truth but both at once. Run the
freeze-frame again with the half-sweep on its menu and watch the discriminator
call it:

<WriteConflict chromatic />

128 rings — half the board written in one simultaneous stroke, exactly the kind
of multi-coin move the walker's chords could never tell apart from the crime —
and zero red wires. That zero has an equation. With the black cells frozen, no
wire runs between any two red cells, so the red half's joint update splits into
independent factors:

$$p(s_R \mid s_B) \;=\; \prod_{i \in R} p(s_i \mid s_B)$$

— each factor the same S-curve that cell was already running on its own turn.
One 128-cell write *is* 128 legal one-at-a-time updates that happen to share a
clock tick. And the equation shows its own boundary: write the same product over
all 256 cells at once and it is false, because wires run inside that set — the
480 red wires of the all-at-once frame are precisely the places the
factorization breaks.

One defense of *all at once* is still standing, and it is the one you will
actually meet: if the job is optimization — find the deepest valley, never mind
the law — does the distance even matter? Stage the fight fairly. Below, one
frustrated problem: a 4×4 patch with its 24 wires assigned at random, nine
<C k="ferro">agree</C> and fifteen <C k="anti">disagree</C>, so that three of
its nine little squares can never satisfy all four of their wires — real
frustration, a real search problem. Sixteen spins means the oracle still holds
everything: the true minimum energy is *certified* by enumeration, and so is the
exact Boltzmann weight of every energy rung. Both panes race in hardware ticks —
*all at once* writes the whole lattice every tick, *red/black* needs two ticks
per sweep:

<Race />

Both bold traces fall to the certified floor. The broken sampler finds the true
minimum — not approximately, the enumerator-certified ground state — about as
fast as the honest one, even while the states it passes through run hot (the
faint ribbon riding high is its instantaneous energy, the oscillation you
already know). Now read the meters: a distance of 0.65 against 0.02, a third of
all probability in the wrong place, forever. As an optimizer, *all at once* is
defensible. As a sampler, it is wrong, and nothing on either pane except the
violet number can tell you which machine you are holding. Optimization success
is not sampling correctness.

That verdict forces a bookkeeping question the field keeps getting wrong: what
is the honest speed of a sampler? The board below scores all three schedules on
the same fenced grid, three columns each. *Writes per tick* is raw parallel
width — the hardware brag. *Samples per thousand ticks* discounts it by measured
dependence: consecutive sweeps are correlated, so they are not each a fresh draw,
and the column divides the sweep rate by an autocorrelation time read from the
audited patch (a lag-one reading — crude, and enough to rank schedules).
*Distance from exact* is the meter you already trust:

<Dashboard />

Parallelism honestly buys evidence: *red/black* posts a couple hundred
independent samples per thousand ticks against *one at a time*'s two — a
hundredfold, real, and yours to keep. And *all at once* posts the best
evidence-rate on the whole board, which is exactly why its row is struck
through. Throughput and evidence-rate are properties of whatever sampler you
have; if it samples the wrong law, they measure speed toward the wrong answer.
The first two columns mean nothing until the third one passes — a gate, not a
trade-off. A splendid effective-sample rate for the wrong distribution is
splendid garbage.

The only thing the coloring costs is generality: it works because the grid
happens to admit a two-coloring, which is to say the graph is *bipartite*. That
small observation is worth more than it looks, because it is the entire design
secret of the actual chip.

Extropic's Z1 is 269,568 p-bits, wired in a fixed sparse graph — sixteen
neighbors each — that was *designed bipartite*. The chip's native act is the
figure you just rescued: program the biases and the wire weights, then sweep
red, sweep black, at millions of sweeps per second, on under one watt, with
thermal noise itself playing the dice. The schedule is not a simulation trick;
it is the machine. A p-bit chip is a Gibbs machine — that is the name this
one-at-a-time-in-parallel sampling carries — and everything it will ever compute
must arrive dressed as biases and wire weights on that fixed two-colored graph.

tick — 72 simultaneous cells, zero write conflicts, the same zero the
freeze-frame printed.
