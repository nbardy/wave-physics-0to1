# Where the simulation is wrong, and what the network may touch

The pressure solve in this site's fluid lessons stops after forty sweeps with 28% of the divergence still in the field, and the semi-Lagrangian advection in the same solver loses between 35% and 46% of a dye's mass across one figure window. Both errors have meters that run inside the loop: the residual of the linear system, and the integral of the dye. The error that is larger than either has no meter. In every cell, the stress exerted by motions smaller than the grid is filled in by a formula whose constant was fit in 1963 to a handful of experiments, and nothing in the run reports when the formula is wrong. Networks are now trained to replace that formula, and the solver's arithmetic, and the grid's smearing, and the forecast's inputs, and, at ECMWF and NOAA since 2025, the whole simulation at once. A learned component earns its place by what it is forbidden to do.

## Five things a model is

A running fluid or wave simulation is five separable objects, and error enters at each one.

1. **The equations.** Incompressible Navier–Stokes for one fluid; two-phase free-surface Navier–Stokes for a breaking wave; the wave-action balance for a spectral wave model, where the state is a spectrum and all the physics is in the source terms. Each set is exact for what it describes and silent about what it omits.
2. **The representation.** A grid, a mesh, a particle set, or a set of spectral bins, at spacing Δx and step Δt. Anything smaller than Δx does not exist in the run.
3. **The closure.** A rule for what the unrepresented scales do to the represented ones: sub-grid stress, whitecapping dissipation, four-wave transfer, bottom friction, air entrainment. Every closure in operational use is a formula with tuned constants.
4. **The inputs.** Initial state, boundaries, forcing, geometry. Measured incompletely or not at all.
5. **The solver.** The arithmetic that advances the representation one step, each piece with its own numerical error and its own stopping rule.

"Deep learning makes the model better" is five claims with five different tests, and a learned component that replaces one of the five is checked by different machinery than one that replaces another.

## The five bins

**The closure is wrong.** Direct numerical simulation of turbulence needs a number of grid points that grows as roughly Re^9/4, so every engineering and geophysical flow is under-resolved by orders of magnitude and stays that way as computers grow. The missing scales drain energy from the resolved ones and, intermittently, hand it back. The algebraic closures that stand in for them (Smagorinsky's eddy viscosity in LES, the k–ε and SST models in RANS) fail in separation, adverse pressure gradients, rotation, and transition, which are the regimes the simulations are run to predict. In a phase-resolving breaking wave, the air entrained below the surface and the position of the interface inside a cell are closures as well.

The form of a closure is constrained by a theorem. The Mori–Zwanzig projection writes the exact effect of the unresolved scales on the resolved ones as a memory integral over the resolved history plus a noise term orthogonal to the resolved state. Parish and Duraisamy built an LES closure from that integral with a finite-memory assumption and no tunable constant, and ran it on Burgers, decaying isotropic turbulence, and channel flow. A closure that is a deterministic function of the current, local, resolved state (every classical closure, and most learned ones) is therefore wrong in form, and parameters added to a wrong form move the fit rather than the ceiling. The three questions to ask of any learned closure are what history it sees, what neighborhood it sees, and whether it is stochastic.

**The representation smears.** Numerical diffusion from the advection scheme kills vortices and damps waves; numerical dispersion changes the speed of a wave; time-stepping error and grid anisotropy add their own. The 35–46% dye loss above is this bin, and the vortices in this site's solver die of numerics long before they die of viscosity. In ocean models, spurious mixing across density surfaces produced by the advection scheme is a first-order error in the deep circulation. Discretization error shrinks with resolution at a known rate. It is the one bin where running finer is a correct answer, and the one bin where a learned correction has a clean teacher: the same model, run finer.

**The solver stopped early.** Unconverged pressure solves and loose tolerances. The 28% residual above is this bin. It is the smallest of the five, fully fixable with multigrid or conjugate gradients, and the only bin with a meter that can acquit an answer rather than merely convict it: a small residual proves the linear system was solved, whatever produced the solution. Lesson 04 version II of this site is entirely about this bin, which is why it found a 3.4× speedup and no accuracy.

**The inputs were never right.** In weather, initial-condition error dominates and chaos amplifies it to a predictability limit of about two weeks. In wave forecasting the wind forcing dominates, and a wave model is as good as the wind it is handed. A large share of the skill gained in forty years of operational forecasting came from data assimilation, the blending of model state with observations, and from the observing system, rather than from the model's physics.

**The statistics were fine and the event was the point.** A rogue wave, the breaker that reached the deck, the separation that stalled the wing. A model tuned to the mean can be right on average and wrong where it matters, and nothing in a mean-squared error reports the difference.

**Chaos moves the target.** For turbulent flow, pointwise prediction beyond a Lyapunov time is impossible for any model, learned or not, and only statistics (spectra, distributions, correlations) are meaningful. A model trained on pointwise mean-squared error regresses to the conditional mean and smooths, and the smoothing is measurable: Pangu-Weather's forecast spectra lose energy from total wavenumber 60–80 onward, so its effective resolution is nearer 500–700 km than its nominal 0.25°, and the loss grows with lead time; the physics-based IFS keeps the ERA5 spectrum out to wavenumber 200. GenCast's authors report the same effect in their own deterministic sibling: GraphCast's spectra have less power at short wavelengths than the reanalysis, while GenCast's stochastic samples match it. Wherever a blurred surrogate has been diagnosed, this is the mechanism. The right meter is statistical, and a model that is pointwise wrong and statistically right may be the correct one.

## What checks it

The ranking that decides whether a learned insertion is trustworthy is what can check it while it runs.

| What checks it | Verdict it can give | Insertions that have it |
|---|---|---|
| The residual of a linear system | Acquits: the answer is right to tolerance, whatever produced it | Warm starts, preconditioners, learned search directions |
| Conservation (mass, momentum, energy, action) | Convicts only: a violation proves wrong; obedience proves nothing | Flux-form corrections, antisymmetric transfer terms |
| Statistics against a finer run | Offline; hours per verdict | Closures, discretization corrections |
| Observations | Offline, sparse, and themselves uncertain | Assimilation, surrogates |
| Nothing | The checker was replaced along with the physics | Whole-model surrogates |

## The solver: learned arithmetic under a residual

A network that proposes a solution to the pressure Poisson equation, with conjugate gradients still run to tolerance afterward, cannot corrupt the state. Li and colleagues (ICML 2023) train a graph network to emit a sparse approximate factorization used as a preconditioner for PCG on 2D and 3D second-order PDEs; the residual still decides when to stop. Kaneda and colleagues (ICML 2023) go one step closer to the iteration: a convolutional network proposes search directions inside a CG-like loop for the pressure system of incompressible Euler on grids up to 256³, the residual is still computed every step, and the method reaches a fixed tolerance in fewer iterations than plain CG or incomplete-Cholesky PCG, with counts nearly independent of problem size. Reinforcement-learned mesh refinement (Yang and colleagues, AISTATS 2023) has the same property from a different angle: the policy decides where degrees of freedom go, a finite-element solver still solves, and the error-versus-degrees-of-freedom curve is the check.

The counterexample is older and more cited. Tompson and colleagues (2017) trained a convolutional network to infer the pressure directly, with the squared divergence of the projected velocity as the loss, and used its output in place of the Poisson solve. There is no iteration to a residual; the divergence left in the field is whatever the network left. It is a whole-model surrogate for one step of the solver, with nothing in the loop to check it.

## The representation: learned corrections and the rollout problem

A network inside a finite-volume solver, trained through 32 unrolled steps of that solver, runs 2D turbulence on a 64×64 grid as accurately as the same solver on a grid 8–10× finer in each direction. That is Kochkov and colleagues (PNAS 2021), and it descends from Bar-Sinai, Hoyer, Hickey, and Brenner (PNAS 2019), who trained networks to emit finite-difference coefficients optimized end to end through the time integrator and integrated Burgers, KdV, and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky at 4–8× coarser resolution than standard finite differences allow, in one spatial dimension only. In the 2D version a network replaces the interpolation inside the convective flux of a finite-volume solver, the pressure projection and the conservative flux form are kept, and the model is trained by unrolling 32 steps of the differentiable solver on 32 trajectories of 4800 steps at Re = 1000, with the ground truth at 2048². The 8–10× in resolution is a 40–80× reduction in compute. It generalized to a doubled domain, to decaying turbulence, and to Re = 4000, and remained stable over tens of thousands of steps. All of it is 2D, periodic, one forcing family, one solver.

Um, Brand, Fei, Holl, and Thuerey (NeurIPS 2020) measured how much of that gain comes from training through the solver. On an unsteady cylinder wake the uncorrected coarse solver has mean absolute error 0.146 against a fine reference; a correction network trained on precomputed pairs brings it to 0.049; a network trained with the solver in the loop for 32 unrolled steps brings it to 0.013, a factor of ten below the uncorrected run and 73% below the simpler variants at identical architecture and weight count. On a buoyancy-driven flow, 128 unrolled steps take the error from 1.59 to 0.62. On a randomly forced Burgers equation the in-the-loop training gains nothing over the simpler variants, and the authors say why: a correction can only anticipate what the state determines, and random forcing removes that. Dresdner and colleagues (2022) repeated the exercise with a spectral rather than finite-volume baseline and found 2–4× accuracy at equal resolution for about 2× runtime, and note the gain is smaller than over the finite-volume baseline because the spectral baseline was already accurate.

Every one of these hybrids is a recurrence, and a recurrence trained one step at a time sees a distribution at test time it never saw in training: its own errors. Three fixes recur in the literature, and they are all versions of showing the model its own mistakes. Sanchez-Gonzalez and colleagues (ICML 2020) train their particle simulator on ground-truth single steps and corrupt the input velocities with random-walk Gaussian noise of standard deviation 3×10⁻⁴, so that rollouts of up to 2000 steps over systems of up to 20,000 particles stay bounded; they name noise and message-passing depth as the two determinants of long-term performance. Stachenfeld and colleagues (ICLR 2022) report that tuning training noise and temporal downsampling is what stabilizes learned coarse turbulence models, and that generalization beyond the training distribution remains the open failure. Brandstetter, Worrall, and Welling (ICLR 2022) pose stability as a domain-adaptation problem and train on the model's own prediction with the gradient stopped through the earlier step (the "pushforward trick"). Lippe and colleagues (NeurIPS 2023) diagnose the residual failure: a solver trained on mean-squared error neglects the low-amplitude high-frequency components of the spectrum, which the nonlinearity then feeds back into the resolved scales, so the rollout error grows from the high frequencies into the low. Their PDE-Refiner adds three diffusion-style denoising passes per step, extends the high-correlation rollout on Kuramoto–Sivashinsky from about 75 to about 100 time units and on 2D Kolmogorov flow from 9.7 to 10.7, costs about 4× per step, and beats both ensembling and the pushforward trick on the same test. The gains are tens of percent, not factors, and they are gains in how long the model stays correlated with the truth.

Two things are settled in this bin. Training through the solver beats training on pairs, by factors, whenever the state determines its future. And the teacher is clean: the target is the gap between the coarse and fine runs of the same equations, which is the only bin where the target is known to be the truth.

## The closure: the gap between offline fit and online behavior

A learned closure can fit the exact sub-grid term offline to a correlation no classical closure reaches and still destroy the simulation it is placed in. Beck, Flad, and Munz (JCP 2019) trained networks on filtered DNS to reproduce the exact LES closure terms and reached a cross-correlation of 47% (73% on interior points), with every network dissipative on average; used directly as the closure, the models "lack long-term stability as high frequency errors accumulate," and the stable model they ship uses the network's prediction only to set a pointwise eddy viscosity. Frezat and colleagues (JAMES 2022) report the same on quasi-geostrophic turbulence: CNN closures trained on the offline (a priori) target drift or blow up online, while closures trained end to end through a differentiable solver are stable and match the reference spectra and enstrophy. Guan, Chattopadhyay, Subel, and Hassanzadeh (JCP 2022) get a stable a posteriori LES from a CNN on 2D turbulence only with enough training data; with too little, the network under-captures backscatter and the run blows up unless the output is clipped, and clipping costs accuracy. Ross, Li, Perezhogin, Fernandez-Granda, and Zanna (JAMES 2023) benchmarked 148 closures with 19 online metrics in a two-layer quasi-geostrophic model and found that the choice of filtering operator is decisive, that all their physics-constrained networks are stable and perform well online in the regime they were trained in, and that all of them generalize poorly to a new regime, where a symbolic closure found by linear regression plus genetic programming performs on par in-domain and better out of it. Sanderse, Stinis, Maulik, and Ahmed's 2024 review organizes the field around the a priori/a posteriori gap, the Mori–Zwanzig memory argument, and structure preservation.

The RANS branch has the longest record. Ling, Kurzawski, and Templeton (JFM 2016) built a network whose output is a combination of Pope's ten basis tensors weighted by functions of five invariants, so that Galilean invariance holds by construction, trained it on duct flow at Re_b = 3500, channel flow at Re_τ = 590, a jet in crossflow, and a converging–diverging channel, and tested it on duct flow at Re_b = 2000 and a wavy wall at Re = 6850. A priori it beat a generic network. A posteriori, in a RANS solver, the linear eddy-viscosity model predicts no secondary flow in the duct, the tensor-basis network predicts corner vortices but over-predicts their strength and adds spurious counter-rotating vortices near the center, and on the wavy wall it predicts separation where the baselines predict none, in a smaller region than the DNS. Even the DNS anisotropy tensor, fed into the same solver, only "slightly over-predicts" the corner vortices, which bounds what any anisotropy model can do with the k and ε transport equations left as they are. Duraisamy's field inversion takes the other route: an adjoint solve infers a spatial correction field to the Spalart–Allmaras production term from measured lift, and a network then learns that field as a function of local flow features. Singh, Medida, and Duraisamy (AIAA Journal 2017) report improved lift and surface pressure on separated airfoils at high angle of attack, and transfer to airfoils and conditions not in the training set. The inferred quantity is a correction to one term of one model, fitted from the discrepancy in one integral.

The reinforcement-learning branch trains only online. Novati, de Laroussilhe, and Koumoutsakos (Nature Machine Intelligence 2021) let agents at grid points adjust the Smagorinsky coefficient locally, with a reward for matching the DNS energy spectrum of homogeneous isotropic turbulence, and report generalization across grid sizes and Reynolds numbers where supervised closures did not. Bae and Koumoutsakos (Nature Communications 2022) let agents set the wall shear stress in wall-modeled LES, with rewards from the log law and known statistics, and report transfer to Reynolds numbers far from training. The forbidden thing in both is explicit: the agent may only turn the dial of an existing, dissipative model.

The ocean branch has run inside a production ocean model. Zanna and Bolton (GRL 2020) used a relevance vector machine to discover a closed-form mesoscale eddy closure with conservation built into its form, and a CNN alongside it; online in an idealized ocean model all their closures improved the coarse run's statistics, the CNN was more stable, and the RVM was the one a person could read. Guillaumin and Zanna (2021) then trained a CNN to predict both the mean and the standard deviation of the sub-grid momentum forcing, which makes the closure stochastic, and Zhang and colleagues (JAMES 2023) put it inside MOM6 in a double gyre, NeverWorld2, and the global OM4 configuration: it ran stably, injected energy as backscatter, and its action diminished as resolution improved, which is the behavior a closure should have. It also needed depth-dependent tuning of its output, did worse near boundaries than in the open ocean, and was "prohibitively high" in cost on CPUs.

The atmospheric branch found the multi-step-training rule in 2018 and hard-constraint layers by 2021. Brenowitz and Bretherton (GRL 2018) trained a network on a 4 km aquaplanet to predict apparent heat and moisture sources on 160 km boxes, and obtained a numerically stable scheme only by minimizing the error over multiple time steps rather than one. Rasp, Pritchard, and Gentine (PNAS 2018) ran multi-year climate simulations with a network emulating super-parameterized convection that reproduced the mean climate and precipitation extremes, with energy conserved only approximately, and with the model unable to cope with temperatures far outside its training manifold. Yuval and O'Gorman (Nature Communications 2020) used random forests, whose predictions are averages of training samples and therefore obey the training data's constraints, and got stable coarse simulations that reproduce the fine climate. Beucler and colleagues (PRL 2021) built conservation layers that enforce energy and mass to machine precision without degrading the fit, against soft penalties that do not.

Memory is the least explored axis. Gupta and Lermusiaux (Proc. R. Soc. A 2021) start from the Mori–Zwanzig argument, build closures as neural delay differential equations, and report that the non-Markovian closures outperform Markovian ones on Burgers and ocean ecosystem models with smaller networks. Ma, Wang, and E (2019) draw the same analogy between the memory kernel and a recurrent network and test it on Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and Navier–Stokes. Nearly every closure in the previous four paragraphs is stateless.

Three independent tests measured what these models contain. Bonavita (GRL 2024) computed Pangu-Weather's spectra and balances and found the ratio of ageostrophic to geostrophic wind smaller than in ERA5 and shrinking with lead time, so the wind and mass fields are "increasingly dynamically inconsistent with one another." Selz and Craig (GRL 2023) rescaled ECMWF's initial-condition perturbations to 0.1% of their operational amplitude and ran Pangu-Weather and ICON: ICON's error grows very fast at small scales and saturates, the signature of the butterfly effect, while Pangu's 0.1% run "essentially reproduces the error growth properties of the 100% experiment," with the difference kinetic energy up by only a factor of 3.1 after 72 h, five orders of magnitude below ICON, which "would incorrectly indicate an unlimited predictability of the atmosphere." Charlton-Perez and colleagues (2024) ran four machine-learned models on Storm Ciarán: all put the minimum pressure within 951–955 hPa of the analyzed 954, and all forecast peak 10 m winds of 25–26 m/s at 48 h against 34 m/s in the IFS analysis and 36 in the HRES forecast, missing the rapid intensification of the winds after 30 h; forecasts from the ERA5 system itself do not have the low-wind bias, so training data are not the cause. Hakim and Masanam (2024) went the other way and found that Pangu reproduces the Matsuno–Gill response to tropical heating, extratropical cyclogenesis, geostrophic adjustment in about 6 h, and hurricane development that vanishes when humidity is set to zero. The models encode the balanced dynamics of the training distribution and lack the small-scale, unbalanced, fast physics, which is where mean-squared error puts no weight.

NeuralGCM is the only model in the table that has been run for decades. It keeps a differentiable dynamical core and learns only the column physics, trained end to end through the core. It also has the most specific failure list: 15 of 37 forty-year runs crashed, the learned physics sees a single column and no history, and a +4 K sea-surface warming produces drift the authors attribute to processes with "subtle impact on weather timescales" that the training signal cannot see.

Below the weather scale the same architecture family is being tested on benchmark PDEs. PDEBench (Takamoto et al. 2022) found FNO the strongest baseline, PINNs poor, and every method failing on compressible flow with shocks and on long time horizons. The Fourier Neural Operator itself (Li et al. 2021) reports relative errors on 2D turbulence that grow by more than an order of magnitude as viscosity falls from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ with 1000 training samples. The Well (Ohana et al. 2024) supplies 15 TB across 16 physics datasets and reports turbulent rollouts as the hardest task for FNO, U-Net, and their variants. Multiple Physics Pretraining (McCabe et al. 2023) shows one transformer matching task-specific baselines across the PDEBench systems and transferring to unseen ones, in-distribution. Poseidon (Herde et al. 2024) pretrains on compressible Euler and incompressible Navier–Stokes at 128×128 and transfers with fewer samples to fifteen downstream tasks including Allen–Cahn, wave, and Poisson equations it never saw. Subramanian and colleagues (NeurIPS 2023) measured the scaling and found what the weather models found: in-distribution error falls as a power law in model and data size, transfer needs orders of magnitude fewer samples than training from scratch, and the benefit decays as the downstream coefficients leave the pretraining range.

## The inputs: learning from observations

Aardvark Weather (Allen et al., Nature 2025) ingests raw ASCAT backscatter, AMSU, MHS, HIRS, and IASI radiances, geostationary composites, and station, ship, and radiosonde reports, about 8% of what an operational center assimilates, and produces a global 1.41° forecast on five vertical levels in about one second on four A100s, against roughly 1000 node-hours for HRES assimilation and forecast. It beats GFS on 2 m temperature at all leads and on several fields from day 5, and trails on 500 hPa geopotential through day 8. Its encoder is pretrained with raw observations as input and ERA5 as output, and its processor with ERA5 on both sides, so a physics-based reanalysis remains the teacher even though no physics runs at inference. GraphDOP (Alexe et al., ECMWF 2024) removes that too: it is "trained and initialised exclusively from Earth System observations, with no physics-based (re)analysis inputs or feedbacks," predicts observed quantities, and its authors report skill to five days. WeatherNext 3 feeds live geostationary radiances directly into a model that also takes the HRES analysis. Aardvark removes the physics model from inference, GraphDOP removes it from training, and WeatherNext 3 adds live radiances beside an analysis that still comes from the physics model; the check at every stage is observations, which are sparse and uncertain themselves.

## Structure by construction

Everything in this section removes a degree of freedom from the network, and each removal is a check that runs for free.

| Constraint | Construction | Exemplar | Reported effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Galilean invariance of a stress | Output as a combination of Pope's tensor basis | Ling et al. 2016; Beetham & Capecelatro 2020 (sparse regression on the same basis) | Beats a generic network a priori; the sparse-regression version matches networks and is readable |
| Rotation, scale, uniform-motion equivariance | Equivariant layers | Wang, Walters, Yu, ICLR 2021 | Robust to the corresponding distribution shifts on Rayleigh–Bénard and ocean-current data |
| Energy conservation | Learn a scalar Hamiltonian or Lagrangian; take its symplectic gradient or Euler–Lagrange equations | Greydanus et al. 2019; Cranmer et al. 2020 | No energy drift where a baseline dissipates; LNN extends to the 1D wave equation via a graph network; conservative systems only, canonical coordinates required for HNN |
| Divergence-free velocity | Predict a vector potential and take its curl; or the exterior-derivative construction | Mohan et al. 2020; Richter-Powell, Lipman, Chen, NeurIPS 2022 | Local mass conservation "drastically improved" on 3D turbulence coarse-graining without loss on other metrics; continuity satisfied exactly by design with a universality proof |
| Joint treatment of scalar and vector fields | Clifford (multivector) convolutions and Fourier layers | Brandstetter et al., ICLR 2023 | Consistent generalization gains on 2D Navier–Stokes, shallow water, and 3D Maxwell |
| Column energy and mass budgets | Conservation layers in the architecture | Beucler et al., PRL 2021 | Conserved to machine precision; soft penalties do not |

The control group is the soft constraint. Physics-informed neural networks put the PDE residual in the loss; Krishnapriyan and colleagues (NeurIPS 2021) showed they fail on convection, reaction, and diffusion problems the network could represent, because the residual term makes the loss landscape ill-conditioned, and recovered one to two orders of magnitude with curriculum and sequence-to-sequence training. Chuang and Barba (2022) ran a PINN on the cylinder at Re = 200 and it converged to a steady solution with no vortex shedding; on a Taylor–Green vortex it took about 32 hours of training to match a 16×16 finite-difference run that took under 20 seconds. The penalty is weighted against the data term; the construction has no weight.

## Equation discovery

SINDy (Brunton, Proctor, and Kutz, PNAS 2016) regresses the time derivative of a state on a library of candidate terms with a sparsity penalty, and recovered the Lorenz system and a mean-field model of the cylinder wake from its POD modes. PDE-FIND (Rudy et al., Science Advances 2017) does the same for partial derivatives and recovered Burgers, KdV, nonlinear Schrödinger, reaction–diffusion, and the Navier–Stokes vorticity equation from a cylinder-wake DNS. Both need the true terms to be in the library and both need derivatives of noisy data; weak SINDy (Messenger and Bortz 2021) integrates against test functions instead of differentiating and recovers equations at signal-to-noise ratios approaching one, and Ensemble-SINDy (Fasel et al. 2022) bootstraps the regression and tolerates more than twice the measurement noise. Cranmer and colleagues (NeurIPS 2020) trained graph networks with sparse messages and then fit symbolic expressions to the messages; the expressions generalized out of distribution better than the network they came from. That result, Zanna and Bolton's readable eddy closure, Beetham and Capecelatro's sparse-regression turbulence models, and Ross and colleagues' symbolic closure that transfers between ocean regimes where the networks do not, are the same finding four times: when a closure can be written down, the written form generalizes further than the network that found it.

## Waves

**Spectral models.** Every operational spectral wave model whose documentation could be checked computes the four-wave transfer with the discrete interaction approximation of Hasselmann and colleagues (1985): ECMWF's ecWAM, whose IFS Cycle 49r1 documentation says the exact integral "is too time consuming and in practice we resort to the Direct-Interaction Approximation," and the Met Office's WAVEWATCH III v7.12 with the ST4 package and the NL1 switch. WAVEWATCH III's own menu for the term is the DIA, the exact Webb–Resio–Tracy method, the generalized multiple DIA, and the two-scale approximation; no learned option exists in it. The exact term costs 102× the DIA in Chen and colleagues' measurement and 142× in Ikuyajolu and colleagues'. The words "neural network" do not occur in the ecWAM documentation.

The first learned emulator of the exact term failed inside the model, and its authors said so. Tolman, Krasnopolsky, and Chalikov (Ocean Modelling 2005) trained a network on 15,000 parametric spectra with exact source terms, reached 4.7× the DIA's accuracy on spectra of that kind and an order of magnitude on held-out spectra of the same kind, at 4.7× the DIA's cost, and then ran it in a WAVEWATCH III growth test: the model "is not able to reproduce consistent wave growth," loses directional symmetry, and suppresses high-frequency energy after about three hours, because "modeled wave spectra can be expected to include features not found in the training data set." The 2008–2009 fix reverted to the exact method whenever the network's error grew, at the exact method's cost. Puscasu (2014) reported an "operationally acceptable" integration in WAVEWATCH III on Lake Michigan, trained and tested on the same location and period.

Two independent emulators reported stable integration in 2026. NLML (Ikuyajolu, Van Roekel, Brus, and Thomas, JGR: Machine Learning and Computation) is a four-layer, 3,072-wide network trained on 3.2 million samples from twin global WAVEWATCH III runs of 2010, one with the DIA and one with the exact method, on a 59,072-node mesh with a 36-direction by 50-frequency spectrum; its inputs are the spectrum, the group velocity, and the depth. Called from Fortran through FTorch, it ran a year-long global simulation of 2020 from a cold start with no fallback and "no signs of error growth." Against the exact term it cuts the DIA's error in the transfer by 45% and in the spectrum by 47%, cuts the global significant-wave-height RMSE from 0.17 m to 0.07 m (up to 90% in the Southern Ocean), and improves peak period by about 20%, at 3.2× the DIA's cost on CPU and 1.04× on a GPU in half precision, 136× faster than the exact method. Its authors list the failures: offline R² below 0.8 in the Maritime Continent, the equatorial Indian Ocean, the Gulf of Guinea, and some polar regions, all low-wind; instability unless the action spectrum is converted to a frequency spectrum before the network sees it ("without these preprocessing steps, NLML becomes unstable"); dependence on the spectral grid; and a comparison against ERA5 that they flag as biased toward the DIA, since ERA5's wave model uses it. Chen, Adcock, Liu, Clark, and Tang (same journal, 2026) trained a U-Net on 210,384 ERA5 spectra from nine deep-water locations and tested on four other locations in a later year; the version that takes the DIA field as a second input channel reaches about 16× the DIA's accuracy at 5.5× its cost, and it survives a 48-hour single-point growth test of 2,880 consecutive calls only with a post-processing filter derived from the DIA that zeroes the network where the DIA is zero, because "even small prediction errors can accumulate and gradually distort the spectral shape"; their plain multilayer perceptron diverges. They state the entanglement: the ST4 wind-input and dissipation terms "were originally calibrated in combination with the DIA method," so a more accurate transfer inherits a package tuned to compensate for a worse one, and whether their network captures "underlying physical processes, rather than simply correcting systematic DIA biases, remains an open question." Neither emulator has been validated against a measurement; both compare to the exact method.

No learned dissipation, wind-input, or bottom-friction term runs inside a spectral model. One paper (Huang, Wang, Jiang, and Hua, Ocean Modelling 2025) emulates the MASNUM-WAM source functions offline with R² above 0.95; nothing in its abstract places the emulated terms in a rollout, and no learned bottom-friction term was found at all. The learned pieces that exist are outside the model. SWRL Net (Mooneyham and colleagues, Weather and Forecasting 2020) corrects WAVEWATCH III spectra with buoy data at three sites off the U.S. West Coast, cutting wave-height RMSE 23–50% in the first six hours and 10–20% after, with sea frequencies improved more than swell, one model per buoy. Campos, Krasnopolsky, Alves, and Penny (Ocean Modelling 2020) replaced the arithmetic mean of NCEP's 21-member wave ensemble with a 140-neuron network trained on altimeters, pulling a ±10% wave-height bias to within 5% and gaining two days of predictability at day 10. García-León and colleagues (Ocean Science 2025) corrected the wind instead: a GAN trained on Sentinel-1 SAR cut the forcing's RMSD 35% against SAR and improved wave-height bias about 10% and RMSD 5% in the northeast Atlantic, made the northwest Mediterranean worse for lack of training data there, and met the same entanglement from the other side: reducing forcing error "requires recalibration of several physical processes, including wave generation and dissipation." Zhou, Darmenov, and Yousefi (GRL 2026) measured what a closure fit to a model is worth against the sea: their air-entrainment network reproduces the WAVEWATCH III quantity it was trained on with R = 0.999 and reaches R = 0.76 against the HiWinGS field measurements, with a bias of +54 cm/h on an RMSE of 64.

The one learned component in operational wave forecasting is a whole-model surrogate. ECMWF's AIFS has carried waves since 12 May 2026 as AIFS Single Wave and AIFS ENS Wave, its "first operational data-driven wave forecasts," trained on an ecWAM hindcast of 1979–2025 at 9 km with altimeter assimilation. The state is ten fields per point (significant wave height, mean period, mean direction, the wave-dependent drag coefficient, and wave height in six period bands above 10 s) in place of the more than 1,200 spectral components ecWAM carries; the reported gain is about 10% in medium-range significant-wave-height error, "roughly one day in lead time," against altimeters and buoys over May–August 2024. Its authors report reduced skill at the sea-ice edge, where the attenuation to zero "is difficult to learn and partially smoothed," and that the model under-represents the strongest winds but not the largest waves, which they attribute to the hindcast's statistically corrected wind and to wave fields having fewer fine-scale features than wind. Swell is implicit in the period bands. ecWAM still runs; ECMWF's explainer says physics-based models "remain central" and that the AI systems are "trained using large datasets generated by physics-based models." Aurora's wave result is in the table above. The other published surrogates (Wang and Jiang, Science Advances 2024; WaveCastNet, 2024; the GWSM4C family; Wang and colleagues, GMD 2025) predict significant wave height from wind histories, most with no spectrum or period, and each that reports a breakdown loses in the same three places: swell pools (correlation falls to about 0.9 in one and about 0.7 in another, against above 0.99 in the westerlies), the sea-ice edge, and coasts and small islands. A state of integral parameters has nowhere to hold a remotely generated, multi-modal, or sharply attenuated sea. The 2025 Copernicus review of machine learning for ocean forecasting (Heimbach and colleagues) calls progress "somewhat limited" and names the absence of any benchmark for wave forecasting as the gap; a 2025 review of phase-averaged and phase-resolving wave models does not mention machine learning in its abstract.

**Phase-resolving breaking.** The reference simulation is under-resolved where most of the bubbles are. Mostert, Popinet, and Deike (JFM 2022) ran two-phase Navier–Stokes with surface tension in Basilisk at Reynolds numbers to 10⁵ and Bond numbers to 1000, with adaptive refinement to an effective 2048³: 181 million cells, about half a million CPU-hours and 1.4 months for one run, 1.76 million CPU-hours for the campaign. Bubbles are resolved to one order of magnitude below the Hinze radius and their size distribution matches the laboratory; about 70% of the wave's energy is dissipated, matching plunging breakers in tanks. The droplets are not converged: many have "radii of approximately the smallest mesh size," the Bond-200 case produces fewer than 100 of them, "which prevents any statistical convergence," and the next refinement level that would test convergence "is not feasible to run currently." The total bubble count "is dominated by sub-Hinze scale bubbles," the least resolved part of the field. Deike's 2022 Annual Review gives the numbers behind that: under typical breaking the Hinze scale is 1–2 mm and about 95% of the entrained air volume is in bubbles larger than it, so the resolved bubbles carry the volume and the unresolved ones carry the count. The same review contains no occurrence of "machine learning," "neural network," or "data-driven"; its proposed path is mechanistic parameterization.

Against that reference, the learned components are three, and none ships in a released solver. Eeltink and colleagues (Nature Communications 2022) attached an LSTM to the modified nonlinear Schrödinger envelope equation, correcting the envelope modulus at each gauge step; it has 369,728 parameters, is trained on 438 wave-tank trajectories from Marseille plus random seas from Sydney, and on held-out breaking cases reduces the error against the tank by about an order of magnitude relative to the uncorrected equation, which predicts no loss where the tank loses about 30% of a focused group's energy. By its authors' account it "cannot yet extrapolate from one wave type to another," must run at the training step size, is unidirectional, and has no stability guarantee. The onset criterion such a model would need, the crest-velocity ratio B = U/C with threshold 0.85 ± 0.02 (Barthélémy et al. 2018; Derakhti, Banner, and Kirby 2020), has no learned version: an arXiv search for "breaking onset" with "machine learning" or "neural" returns nothing.

Inside two-phase solvers, the learned parts are interface operators trained on synthetic shapes. Qi, Lu, Scardovelli, Zaleski, and Tryggvason (JCP 2019) learn curvature from the nine volume fractions of a 3×3 stencil, trained on 65 circles; the paper reports no error table and states that "there is no explicit order that guarantees convergence under grid refinement." Önder and Liu (JCP 2023) state the field's failure in one sentence, that data-driven curvature methods "outperform conventional methods on coarser grids but diverge with grid refinement," and their symmetry-preserving network reaches parity with the best conventional method using a smaller stencil. Buhendwa, Bezgin, and Adams (2021) recover first-order convergence by gating: a classifier hands coarse interfaces to the network and fine ones to the conventional reconstruction. Cahaly, Evrard, and Desjardins (2024) predict the PLIC normal from a 3×3×3 stencil and then translate the plane so that volume is conserved exactly. None of these has been run on a breaking wave. The nearest thing to a sub-grid entrainment closure, Bhattacharjee, Hatashita, and Jain (2026), learns sub-grid interfacial area density with a fractal prior and reports that the prior "becomes ineffective in fragmentation-dominated regimes characterized by topology change and droplet breakup," which is the regime a breaker is in.

Learned particle solvers have the best-measured free-surface failure. On LagrangeBench's 2D dam break (5,740 particles), a graph-network simulator has a 20-step position error of 3.3×10⁻⁵; Toshev and colleagues (ICML 2024) report the error over the full trajectory of about 400 steps as 1.9×10⁻¹, four orders of magnitude of growth, and describe the mechanism: the fluid compresses to 1.4–1.5 times the reference density in a method whose reference allows 1%, particles then detach from the free surface after about 50 steps, and in about 8 of 25 test trajectories the wave front spreads at step 80 "as if a virtual wall exists." Their fix reinserts SPH relaxation steps at about 1.4× cost per step. No learned particle solver has been run on a breaker; the free-surface cases in that literature are dam breaks and sloshing. There is no published neural surrogate of a breaking-wave DNS, so the failures a builder would expect from one, a blurred jet or a mistimed plunge, have not been measured, because the object has not been built.

## What more parameters buys

More parameters help only where there is signal to fit them to and where the model's form can express the answer. Per bin:

- **Closure.** There is a true target (sub-grid stress from DNS, the exact transfer integral, the difference field from a resolved run), so capacity helps up to the data. But a stateless, local, deterministic net has the wrong form, and its ceiling is set by form, not size. History, neighborhood, and a stochastic term move the ceiling; parameters do not.
- **Discretization correction.** The target is the gap between coarse and fine, which is small and known. Capacity is rarely the limit; rollout stability is, and every stability fix in the literature is a training-procedure change (unrolling, noise, pushforward, refinement), not a bigger network.
- **Whole-operator surrogate.** Error scales with size and data in-distribution; Aurora at 1.3 billion parameters reports "we have not yet hit a performance ceiling," and Subramanian's power laws say the same at benchmark scale. Out of distribution, both find the benefit decaying with distance from the training range, and NeuralGCM's drift at +4 K is the same fact at climate scale. Generalization to physics not in the data comes from structure kept in the loop, not from parameters.
- **Inputs.** Limited by observations, not parameters. Aardvark's remaining deficit against HRES at upper levels is, by its authors' account, the sparsity of observations there.
- **Solver.** A learned preconditioner's gain is measured in iterations to tolerance; the tolerance is fixed by the residual, and parameters change the speed of acquittal, not the verdict.

So "with more parameters?" is answered yes in exactly one bin, the closure, and only after the closure's inputs are right. Everywhere else the lever is structure.

## Settled, open, and this year

Settled. A learned correction trained through a differentiable solver recovers discretization error by factors, in two dimensions, in the flow family it was trained in, and the same correction trained on precomputed pairs recovers a fraction of that. A closure's offline fit does not predict its online behavior; the learned closures that stayed stable were trained online, constrained by construction, or both. Whole-model surrogates trained on reanalysis beat the physics model on the scores the physics model is tuned to, lose spectral energy below a few hundred kilometers, under-forecast peak winds, and do not contain the butterfly effect; ensembles trained on proper scores keep the spectrum and the skill. A closure fit to a model reproduces the model. Nothing learned runs inside an operational spectral wave model's source terms, and nothing learned ships in any released phase-resolving code.

Open. Whether a closure with history and a stochastic term reaches a ceiling a stateless one cannot; the two 2026 emulators of the four-wave term are stateless, and no one has trained one with memory. Whether a learned four-wave term can be run with a source-term package retuned against it rather than against the DIA. Whether a learned interface operator can converge under refinement without gating to the classical method. Whether a hybrid at climate length can learn processes with "subtle impact on weather timescales." Whether any wave surrogate can carry a spectrum. Whether a learned four-wave term agrees with a buoy; neither 2026 paper checked.

This year, for a builder of wave simulators. Put a meter on the closure before touching it: a finer run or a tank record, an a-posteriori statistic (spectrum, dissipation rate, bubble size distribution), and a rollout long enough for a bad closure to blow up, since the online failures in the record (Tolman 2005, Beck 2019, Frezat 2022, the perceptron of Chen 2026) were all offline successes. If a learned part goes in, put it where a check runs: as a preconditioner or a search direction under the pressure residual, or as a flux or an antisymmetric exchange under conservation, and train it through the solver with the state unrolled and its own predictions in the training input. In a spectral model, the exact four-wave term now has two emulators that survive integration, both locked to their spectral grid and both untested against a measurement; the experiment nobody has run is one of them against the buoys with the ST4 constants re-fit. In a phase-resolving breaker, the bottleneck is the teacher: the reference simulation does not converge its droplets, produces fewer than a hundred of them at low Bond number, and costs half a million CPU-hours per run, and the two-phase interface operators that exist have never seen a breaking wave. Smagorinsky's constant is still in the cell, and the tools to replace it have arrived first for the arithmetic around it, then for the grid it sits on, and not yet for the formula itself.
